Tehilim 120: In my distress I called

תְּהִלִּים ק״כ · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 120
The first of the fifteen Songs of Ascents, and the one furthest from the destination. Nothing in it is Jerusalem: the speaker is among foreigners, surrounded by lying talk, and the climb the collection is named for has not started. Fourteen of the fifteen headings read a song of the ascents; Psalm 121 alone reads it with a different preposition. The grammar to watch here is the nominal sentence, a whole clause with no verb in it. Verse 4 has none, verse 7 opens with none, and the last line of the psalm is four words that put a man and a war side by side and leave you to supply the collision. In many Ashkenazi communities the Songs of Ascents are said after Minchah on the Shabbatot between Sukkot and Passover.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Seven verses that go the wrong way. Verse 1 reports a call that was answered; verses 2 to 7 are the trouble, and it does not lift. The subject throughout is speech: lying lips, a deceitful tongue, a tongue addressed to its face, and finally the speaker's own words, which are peace and are heard as an opening for war. Verse 5 places him among foreigners and verse 6 gives the length of it. Read as the first step of the collection, the psalm establishes what the ascent is from: not a siege or an illness, but living far away among people whose words cannot be trusted.

רמז · Remez: allusion

בַּצָּרָתָה (ba-tza-ra-ta, v.1) carries an ending that is unusual on this noun, and the possessor is supplied by the separate word after it rather than by a suffix, so the phrase reads in the distress, the one that is mine. מֶשֶׁךְ (me-shech, v.5) and קֵדָר (ke-dar, v.5) are the crux: elsewhere in the Bible one is a people of the far north and the other an Arabian tribe, and no one lives in both, so the pair is generally read as a figure for exile among strangers rather than as a location. רְתָמִים (re-ta-mim, v.4) is the desert broom, named here because its charcoal is reputed to hold heat long after other coals have died, which is the point of pairing it with arrows: one wound arrives fast, the other keeps burning. Whether verse 4 answers verse 3 or describes the tongue is disputed, since the verse has no verb to settle it. רַבַּת (ra-bat, v.6) turns up again as the first word of the body of Psalm 129, also a Song of Ascents, in the same sense of long enough.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The psalm was read early as being about the tongue rather than about geography, and the reading has a foothold in the text: the two named places are unreachable from each other, while lying lips are portable. On that reading the exile of verse 5 is the condition of living where speech has stopped meaning anything, and Meshech and Kedar are its two ends. The weapons of verse 4 have been taken as measure for measure, a mouth answered by things that come out of a mouth, an arrow and a burning coal, which is why the verse gives no subject for the giving. Verse 7 has been used as the standard case of the person who cannot be at peace unilaterally: he is peace, and it changes nothing, because peace made by one side is not yet peace.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The collection climbs fifteen steps and this is the ground floor, so the psalm describes the state before any ascent: distant, surrounded by false speech, and unable to be understood. In the mystical idiom that is the condition of a soul in a body that speaks a language the world answers with war, and the pairing in שָׁלוֹם (sha-lom, v.7) and לַמִּלְחָמָה (la-mil-cha-ma, v.7), peace and war set as bare predicates, is read as two whole systems facing each other with no verb to mediate. The answer already given in וַיַּעֲנֵנִי (va-ya-a-ne-ni, v.1) is then the thing to hold on to: the psalm reports that the call was heard before it says anything about the trouble, so the trouble is described from inside a rescue that has already happened and has not yet arrived.
