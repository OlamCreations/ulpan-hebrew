Shema Yisrael: Hear, O Israel

שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל · morning + evening + bedtime · the central declaration · Deut 6:4-9
HomeShema
The central declaration of Jewish faith, six words in V1 that every Jewish child learns first, every Jewish adult says twice a day, and every Jewish martyr has tried to say last. The text is Deuteronomy 6:4-9: Moses' command to Israel to acknowledge God's oneness and to teach this acknowledgment across generations. The prayer extends into V'ahavta (Deut 6:5-9), Vehayah im shamoa (Deut 11:13-21), and Vayomer (Num 15:37-41), three biblical passages joined into one continuous liturgical block. Said in the morning service (Shacharit), in the evening service (Maariv), and as the last words before sleep. Worn on the body (tefillin) and on the doorpost (mezuzah). This page teaches the most-said opening lines.

Read Deuteronomy 6:4-9 for the surrounding biblical/liturgical context.

♦ Lesson

  • When to say it, morning Shacharit, evening Maariv, the bedtime Shema (Keriat Shema al HaMita) before sleep. Three times a day is the universal minimum.
  • How to say the first verse, cover the eyes with the right hand while saying it. Drag out the last word echad (one), three syllables, e-CHA-D, held long enough to consciously affirm God's oneness over each direction (some imagine the four directions + up + down + center).
  • The whispered second line, Baruch shem k'vod malchuto l'olam va'ed is said quietly because it is not biblical text but a rabbinic response. Once a year, on Yom Kippur, it is said aloud.
  • V'ahavta, the second paragraph, which begins with the command to love God with all heart, soul, and might. The original Hebrew word for "might" is me'odecha, an unusual word the rabbis read as "with all your resources / with everything you have".
  • Grammar, pronominal suffixes: this passage is a masterclass. Elohecha (your God), levavcha (your heart), nafshecha (your soul), me'odecha (your might), same 2ms suffix -cha attached to four different nouns. Memorize the pattern.
  • The verbs: shma is a qal imperative (hear!). V'ahavta is a qal perfect 2ms with vav-consecutive, a future command framed in perfect aspect, very common in biblical Hebrew ("and you shall love").

♦ About this prayer

Six Hebrew words that became the spine of Jewish identity. Said morning and night for 3,000 years; said as the last words by Rabbi Akiva while being tortured to death by the Romans; written on every Jewish doorpost and on every observant Jew's tefillin straps.

The text: Deuteronomy 6

Moses' farewell speech in Deuteronomy chapter 6 contains the central statement of Israelite monotheism. Verse 4, six Hebrew words, is the declaration; verses 5-9 are the obligations that flow from it: love God totally, teach these words to your children, speak them at home and on the road, when you lie down and when you rise, bind them on your arm and between your eyes, and write them on the doorposts of your house.

The orthography of Deut 6:4 in every Torah scroll has two enlarged letters: the ע (ayin) at the end of shma and the ד (dalet) at the end of echad. Together they spell עֵד (ed, "witness"). The reader of the Shema is a witness; saying it is testimony.

Three paragraphs, one prayer

The full liturgical Shema combines three biblical passages:

  • Deut 6:4-9, the declaration + V'ahavta (love God, teach the children, bind, mezuzah).
  • Deut 11:13-21, Vehayah im shamoa (the reward/punishment paragraph; many Reform siddurim omit this).
  • Num 15:37-41, Vayomer (the commandment of tzitzit and remembering the Exodus).

The three paragraphs together take ~3-4 minutes to say. The opening verse alone takes 5 seconds. Learn the first verse first, then add V'ahavta, then expand.

What "echad" means

The word echad in Adonai echad is usually translated "one". But it is also the root of unity / unified-ness, not the numerical one (which can be yachid), but the integrative one. Maimonides reads it as: God is not a being you count among beings, God is the only true unity, and everything else's apparent oneness is borrowed from God's.

This is why the word is held long. Each second the speaker extends the syllable chad, the rabbis read as another moment of consciously affirming that there is no other power, not luck, not fate, not the various deities of the surrounding world. Just one.
