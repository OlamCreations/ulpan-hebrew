Tehilim 112: Happy is the one who fears the LORD

תְּהִלִּים קי״ב · הַלְלוּ יָהּ
HomePs 112
Ten verses carrying the twenty-two letters of the alphabet, and the count only works if you stop reading by verse. The acrostic runs on half-lines: each verse breaks into short clauses and each clause opens with the next letter. Verse 1 begins with Halleluyah, which stands outside the scheme, then Aleph on ashrei and Bet on bemitzvotav. Verse 2 takes Gimel and Dalet, verse 3 He and Vav, on in pairs down to verse 8, which closes on Ayin. The last two verses carry three clauses each instead of two, so verse 9 takes Pe, Tzade and Qof, and verse 10 takes Resh, Shin and Tav. Sixteen letters across the first eight verses plus six across the last two is twenty-two. Psalm 111, immediately before, is built on the same plan and shares phrases with this one, including the clause his righteousness stands for ever and the pair gracious and compassionate.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A portrait, not a story. Nine verses describe one man from the inside out: what he fears, what he delights in, what he leaves behind him, what is in his house, how he handles money, what he does when bad news comes. He never speaks and nothing happens to him. Then verse 10 puts a second man beside him for three clauses and builds him out of the first man's words in reverse. Both see, יִרְאֶה (yir-e, v.8) and יִרְאֶה (yir-e, v.10), but one looks at enemies without fear and the other looks and rages. One is never shaken, יִמּוֹט (yi-mot, v.6), the other melts, וְנָמָס (ve-na-mas, v.10). What stands for ever in צִדְקָתוֹ (tzid-ka-to, v.9) is righteousness; what perishes in the last clause is desire. The contrast is lexical before it is moral.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The alphabet is doing some of the choosing. Verse 1 calls him ashrei ish, happy is the man; verse 5 calls him tov ish, good is the man, and the Tet slot is why the second phrase is available at that point in the line. Reading the psalm as verse units hides this completely, which is the practical reason to learn its half-line structure. Verse 4 is the standing dispute: the verb has no explicit subject, so the light may be what rises for the upright, or God may be the one who shines, and the three adjectives that follow may describe the light, the God-fearing man, or God. The same pair, gracious and compassionate, is applied to God in Psalm 111, which is why many readers take verse 4 as a description of God dropped into a portrait of a man; others take the ambiguity as the point, that the man comes to resemble what he fears. It is not resolved. One more: the word rendered righteousness in וְצִדְקָתוֹ (ve-tzid-ka-to, v.3) and צִדְקָתוֹ (tzid-ka-to, v.9) is the ordinary word for charity in later Hebrew, and verse 9, giving to the needy, is the bridge between the two senses. JPS 1917 felt the strain and rendered it merit in verse 3 and righteousness in verse 9.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The promises are concrete and this-worldly: descendants who are strong, a house with wealth in it, a name that outlasts the man. Read against ordinary experience they raise the obvious objection, and one line of reading answers it by moving the weight onto the clause that repeats, his righteousness stands for ever. On that reading what is guaranteed is not the wealth but what was done with it, and verse 9 is the proof text, since a man who scatters is precisely one who has less than he had. The verb in פִּזַּר (pi-zar, v.9) carries that: giving is described in the vocabulary of loss, and then declared to be the thing that lasts. Verse 8 is read the same restrained way. He looks at his adversaries, and the psalm gives him no weapon and no action, so the vindication is that he is standing there to look.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Light rising in the dark, זָרַח (za-rach, v.4) with בַּחֹשֶׁךְ (ba-cho-shech, v.4), is the phrase the mystical readings work on. The light is not said to be made at that moment; it rises, as something already there becomes visible, so the darkness is treated as a condition of the seer rather than an absence of light. The three adjectives that follow are then read as the shape that light takes when a human being conducts it, which is why the same words can belong to God and to the man without the verse choosing. The acrostic carries its own claim: the entire alphabet is spent describing one ordinary person who lends money carefully, which sets every letter available to speech at the service of a life that looks unremarkable from outside.
