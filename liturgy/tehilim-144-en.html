Tehilim 144: Blessed be the LORD my rock

תְּהִלִּים קמ״ד · לְדָוִד בָּרוּךְ
HomePs 144
Fifteen verses in two halves that barely look like the same poem: a soldier's petition in verses 1 to 11, then a picture of sons, daughters, granaries and flocks in verses 12 to 15. The first half is assembled out of lines from Psalm 18 and a question borrowed from Psalm 8, which makes this a good page for seeing how a psalm can be built from older psalms. The last verse is the one every learner already knows by ear: it is the second line of Ashrei, said three times a day, paired there with a verse from Psalm 84. Watch the two verbs of verse 7, rescue me and deliver me, because verse 11 repeats them and the repetition is what holds the petition together.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two halves joined by a word that will not quite hold them. Verses 1 to 11 are a king's war prayer: blessing, then the question of what a man is worth, then a demand that God come down with lightning, then the plea at מִמַּיִם (mi-ma-yim, v.7) to be pulled out of deep water and out of the hands of men who lie under oath, with verse 11 repeating that plea to close it. Verses 12 to 15 are something else entirely, a description of a household and a town at peace, with no petition in them and no enemy. The joint is אֲשֶׁר (a-sher, v.12), and it can be read as so that, giving the second half as the purpose of the rescue, or as a relative pronoun continuing the previous verse, which reads badly in Hebrew. Translators divide, and the division is visible in any two English Bibles set side by side.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The first half is largely quarried from older poems. The rock, the fortress, the stronghold, the deliverer and the shield of verse 2 are the same run of titles that opens Psalm 18, the bending of the heavens and the arrows of verses 5 and 6 come from the theophany in that same psalm, and the question at verse 3 is the question of Psalm 8:5 asked with two different verbs. The single word that will not settle is חַסְדִּי (chas-di, v.2). Read plainly it makes my faithful love a title for God, which occurs nowhere else in this bare form, and translators split between keeping it as an epithet and reading it as my faithful God. Two more. אַלּוּפֵינוּ (a-lu-fei-nu, v.14) is the word for a chief as well as for cattle, so the line is read either as our oxen are laden or as our leaders are burdened, and the choice changes the verse from an inventory to a complaint. And צֹאונֵנוּ (tzo-vne-nu, v.13) sits in a run of vocabulary so unusual that the last four verses are often treated as an independent composition attached here.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

אַשְׁרֵי (ash-rei, v.15) to the end of verse 15 is the second sentence of Ashrei, recited twice in the morning and once in the afternoon, where it is preceded by a verse from Psalm 84 and followed by Psalm 145. The effect of that placement is to make this line an introduction rather than a conclusion: a psalm that ends by naming the condition of happiness becomes, in the prayer book, the door into the psalm that describes it. The three negatives of verse 14 have also carried more than one reading. No going out is taken as no breaching of the walls and no exile, and also as nothing lost from the womb or the flock, since the verb is simply going out and the Hebrew does not specify. The readings diverge, and the text does not settle them.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading fastens on the instrument at verse 9: a new song on a harp of ten. Ten is the count of the sefirot, so the ten strings are read as the full set of channels through which anything is given, and a new song is the praise that the fixed order of prayer cannot carry, the part that has to be improvised because the situation has never happened before. Read that way the two halves of the psalm are one motion after all. צוּרִי (tzu-ri, v.1) calls God a rock, the hardest and least giving image in the language, and the psalm ends in granaries, flocks and children, which is what the rock turns into when it opens.
