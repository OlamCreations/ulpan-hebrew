Tehilim 10: Why, O LORD, do You stand far off

תְּהִלִּים י · לָמָה יְהֹוָה תַּעֲמֹד
HomePs 10
A psalm with no superscription, it continues the alphabetic acrostic begun in Psalm 9, so that 9 and 10 together form one poem (the early Greek and Latin versions count them as a single psalm). Eighteen verses turning on a complaint: God seems absent (v.1) while the wicked hunts the poor and tells himself "there is no God" (v.4). The cry "Arise, O LORD" (v.12) breaks the silence, and the close affirms the LORD as king forever who hears the orphan (v.16-18). Recited in times of distress, when justice seems to sleep.

The opening cry of the whole psalm. Lamah ("why") is not a request for information but a protest. God is pictured not as hostile but as distant, standing far off (בְּרָחוֹק) and hiding (תַּעְלִים, lit. "You conceal") at the very moment of trouble.

The center of the wicked's creed. "In the height of his nose" (כְּגֹבַהּ אַפּוֹ) is a Hebrew idiom for haughtiness, the lifted nostril of contempt. The hinge of the psalm. After eleven verses of complaint and ambush, the prayer finally breaks out: קוּמָה (Kumah) = "Arise!", the cry that recalls the Ark setting out (Numbers 10:35). ♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm opens with a raw question: V.1: "Why, O LORD, do You stand far off, hide in times of trouble?" V.2-11: a long portrait of the wicked, he hunts the poor (v.2), boasts of his appetite (v.3), and in his arrogance concludes "there is no God" (v.4); his ways prosper, he says "I shall not be moved" (v.6), his mouth is full of oaths and deceit (v.7); he lurks like a lion to seize the helpless (v.8-10), telling himself "God has forgotten" (v.11). V.12-15: the prayer turns, "Arise, O LORD" (v.12), "break the arm of the wicked" (v.15). V.16-18: the resolution, the LORD is king forever, the nations perish from His land, He hears the lowly and judges for the orphan, so that mortal man may terrify no more.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm has no title because it continues the acrostic of Psalm 9: each section opens with the next Hebrew letter, and 10 picks up the alphabet (ל at v.1, ק at קוּמָה v.12) where 9 left off. The seam is the meaning: Psalm 9 praised the God who judges; Psalm 10 asks why that Judge is silent. Note the rare word חֵלְכָה (chelkhah, v.8, 14) and חֵל כָּאִים (chel ka'im, v.10), "the hapless / the wretched," a poetic term for the crushed poor that appears almost only here. And the wicked's verb נִאֵץ (ni'etz, v.3, 13), "to spurn," frames his whole inner life: he does not merely sin, he despises the One who calls to account.

דרש · Drash: interpretation

The heart of the psalm is the wicked's interior monologue: three times he "says in his heart" (אָמַר בְּלִבּוֹ), "I shall not be moved" (v.6), "God has forgotten, He has hidden His face, He never sees" (v.11), "You will not call to account" (v.13). His theology is not denial of God's existence so much as denial of God's attention: שָׁכַח אֵל (shakhach El, v.11) = "God has forgotten." The psalmist answers him at v.14 with the exact opposite verb: רָאִתָה (ra'itah) = "You have seen." The wicked says God hides His eyes; the poet says God's eye is precisely on the trouble (עָמָל) and the grief (כַעַס). The whole drama is fought over a single question: does God look?

סוד · Sod: mystical meaning

The psalm names God by a tender title found almost nowhere else: אֲבִי יָתוֹם is the idea behind V.14, "You have been the orphan's helper," and v.18, "to judge the orphan and the oppressed." The God who seems far off (v.1) turns out to be the nearest of all, the One who stands in for the absent father. The structure mirrors this hiding and revealing: the hester panim (הִסְתִּיר פָּנָיו, v.11, "He hid His face") that the wicked celebrates is answered by the God who, hidden, still sees. Mystically the silence of v.1 is not absence but tzimtzum, a withdrawal that makes room for the cry "Arise" (קוּמָה, v.12), the human word that draws the King back into His kingdom (v.16). To recite this psalm is to call the hidden Face back to the orphan.
