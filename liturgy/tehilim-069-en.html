Tehilim 69: Save me, God, the waters have reached my neck

תְּהִלִּים ס״ט · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 69
Thirty seven verses: thirty of drowning, seven of singing. It opens with water at the throat and mud with no floor under it, spends twenty verses on shame inflicted by other people rather than by an army, curses those people for seven verses, and ends with Zion rebuilt and lived in. This is a lament of the individual, the kind of psalm said when the trouble is other people. For a learner it is a workshop of the object suffix: save me, answer me, rescue me, sweep me away, swallow me, ransom me, redeem it, all one syllable hung on the end of a verb.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements. Verses 2 to 14 set out the trouble and give its cause: the speaker is drowning, and the reason people despise him is that he is identified with God's house. Verses 15 to 29 are the petition, first for rescue in the same water images that opened the psalm, then seven verses of curse against the people who did this. Verses 30 to 37 are the vow of praise and its widening, from the singer to the humble who watch, then heaven and earth, then Zion and the cities of Judah. Read as one argument it holds together: the rescue is asked for on the ground stated in עָלֶיךָ (a-lei-cha, v.8), that the reproach was incurred for God's sake, and the curses are justified in verse 27 on the ground that the enemies are attacking a man God has already wounded.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three cruxes worth knowing. נָפֶשׁ (na-fesh, v.2) is usually rendered soul, but the concrete sense of the word is throat, and with the water rising it is hard to read it any other way here. שׁוֹשַׁנִּים (sho-sha-nim, v.1), lilies, is a direction in the heading whose meaning is lost: it is taken as the name of a melody, as an instrument, or as a musical term nobody can now recover. And וְלִשְׁלוֹמִים (ve-lish-lo-mim, v.23), given above as those at ease, is genuinely disputed, read as their well being, as their allies, or as a state of security, with some editors changing the consonants to get a smoother sense. Outside the synagogue this psalm carries unusual weight: verses 10, 22 and 26 all reappear in the Christian scriptures, which is why it turns up in polemical literature far more than its neighbours in the book.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The curse section is why this psalm is hard to say out loud. Seven verses asking for blindness, desolation and erasure, and the tradition has never smoothed them over. One line of reading takes them as description rather than wish: this is what happens to people who kick a man who is already down, and the speaker is stating the consequence rather than calling it in. Another takes the whole psalm as spoken by the community in exile rather than by one man, which is the natural reading of the last two verses, where what is finally asked for is the rebuilding of the cities of Judah. On מַקְרִן (mak-rin, v.32), one classical reading notices that the word for horned is singular in form and identifies the animal with the ox that Adam offered, a single horn on its forehead, an animal older than the sacrificial system and therefore outside it. Either way the point is the ranking: what this psalm ends up offering is a song, not a bull.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The descent here is measured rather than chaotic: water closing over the head, then mud with no floor under it, then a body emptied by fasting, then a throat that can do nothing but sing. In the mystical reading that shape is the whole teaching. The return is not a reversal, because nothing that happened gets undone: the enemies of verse 5 are never removed, the cities of verse 36 are built anyway. The image the tradition works hardest is מִסֵּפֶר (mi-se-fer, v.29), the book of the living, a register in which names are written and out of which they can be struck. The same phrase, a book of life, comes back in the insertions said during the Ten Days of Repentance. In this verse it is not about the world to come. It is about being counted among those who are still here.
