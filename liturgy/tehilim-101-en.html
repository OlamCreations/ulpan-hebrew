Tehilim 101: I will sing of kindness and justice

תְּהִלִּים ק״א · לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 101
Eight verses in which a king writes down the rules of his own house. Ten of the verbs are first person, most of them future: I will sing, I will study, I will walk, I will set, I will destroy, I will not know. The negative comes six times, always standing in front of its verb, which makes this the cleanest drill in the book for that word order. The psalm also holds one of the strangest half lines in the Psalter: in the middle of the resolutions the speaker breaks off to ask when God will come to him, then carries on as if nothing had happened, and nobody has settled what that question is doing there. In the monthly cycle that reads the whole book it falls on the twentieth day, with Psalms 97 to 103.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

This is a job description written in the first person. Verse 1 names the two values, verses 2 to 4 cover the king's own conduct, verses 5 to 8 the people he will admit and the people he will remove. The repeated vocabulary makes the join visible and is worth tracing on the page: the way of the blameless at בְּדֶרֶךְ (bde-rech, v.2) is what he studies, and the same phrase at בְּדֶרֶךְ (bde-rech, v.6) is what qualifies a servant; in front of my eyes at לְנֶגֶד (le-ne-ged, v.3) returns at לְנֶגֶד (le-ne-ged, v.7); inside my house at בְּקֶרֶב (be-ke-rev, v.2) returns at בְּקֶרֶב (be-ke-rev, v.7). Nothing is demanded of anyone that the speaker has not first taken on himself, and the order is deliberate, self first and staff second.

רמז · Remez: allusion

מָתַי (ma-tai, v.2) is the difficulty. Four words of prayer sit inside a list of policies with no connective before or after. Readings divide between a request for the ark or the divine presence to arrive at this house, so that the resolutions are the preparation for it, and a reading in which the question is a lament that the presence has not come yet; a minority treat the half line as displaced from somewhere else. It is unresolved, and a page that told you otherwise would be inventing. Two smaller points: מְלׇשְׁנִי (mlosh-ni, v.5) is a verb built straight off the noun for tongue, and the root is rare, appearing in a different stem at Proverbs 30:10. And וּרְחַב (ur-chav, v.5) calls a wide heart a fault, while the same phrase at 1 Kings 5:9 in the Hebrew numbering, 4:29 in English Bibles, is the praise given to Solomon for breadth of understanding. Same words, opposite verdict, decided entirely by context.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Read as a charter, the psalm has always been applied past kings to anyone who runs a household or an office, since almost every clause is about hiring, admitting and dismissing rather than about war. The question of verse 2 gets pulled into the narrative books: 2 Samuel 6 has the ark brought up to the city, and in 2 Samuel 7 David proposes building a house for it and is told he will not be the one to build it. Read against that, the resolutions become an argument the king is making about the house he already has, and the sequence of the psalm answers a hard question, which is what a person does with a promise that has been deferred. He writes down the rules for the interval.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two nouns of the first verse carry the weight. In kabbalistic vocabulary chesed and din are the poles of divine action, generosity that gives without measure and judgment that gives exactly what is due, and mishpat is the word for their balance rather than a synonym for either. To sing of both together is therefore not a summary of two topics but a claim to be holding a tension. On that reading the house of בֵּיתִי (bei-ti, v.2) is the self before it is a building, and בְּקֶרֶב (be-ke-rev, v.2), the word for inside, is the same word used for the inward part of a person elsewhere in the Psalms, so the whole survey of doorways, servants and dismissals is also a description of what a person admits into himself, morning by morning, as verse 8 puts it.
