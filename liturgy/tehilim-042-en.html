Tehilim 42: As a deer longs for channels of water

תְּהִלִּים מ״ב · לַמְנַצֵּחַ מַשְׂכִּיל
HomePs 42
The psalm that opens Book Two of the Psalter, and the first of the collection attributed to the sons of Korah. A speaker cut off from the Temple remembers walking there in a festival crowd, and now hears the same question every day from people around him: where is your God. Twice he stops and talks to his own soul in the second person, and that refrain returns a third time at the end of Psalm 43, which is why the two are often treated as one poem. For a learner it is a dense drill in one noun, nafshi, my soul, and in verbs that carry their object inside the word: forgotten-me, taunted-me, I-remember-You.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A man far from Jerusalem, in the north near the sources of the Jordan, remembers going up to the Temple with a crowd and cannot go now. Around him people ask where his God has gone, and he has no answer. Twice he interrupts his own complaint to tell his soul to wait. Read the psalm as three moves repeated: a memory, a taunt, a refrain. Nothing in the situation changes between the first refrain at verse 6 and the second at verse 12; what changes is only that the second one ends by saying my God.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three details reward slowing down. In verse 2 the noun כְּאַיָּל (ke-a-yal, v.2) is masculine but the verb תַּעֲרֹג (ta-a-rog, v.2) is feminine, and commentators split between treating it as a form of the word for a doe and treating the mismatch as the poet already looking ahead to nafshi, the feminine soul of the second half. The verb itself appears only here and in Joel, so its exact sense is uncertain. In verse 3 the Masoretic vocalization makes וְאֵרָאֶה (ve-e-ra-e, v.3) passive, and be seen before the face of God, where the unpointed consonants would equally allow the active and see the face of God; the choice is deliberate and it is discussed. In verse 5 the word אֶדַּדֵּם (e-da-dem, v.5) occurs nowhere else in the Bible, and translations run from I led them in procession to I walked slowly, with no way to decide.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Numbers 26:11 says flatly that the sons of Korah did not die when the earth swallowed their father, and the Korahite psalms have long been read against that verse: the voices are those of survivors of a family disaster, which suits a poem that ends without rescue. Mount Mizar in verse 7 has never been identified with any known place, and the traditional reading takes advantage of that by hearing the ordinary meaning of the word, small, so that the speaker is remembering God from a little hill instead of from the mountain he wants. Whether the poet intended the pun is disputed, but the geography of the verse is real: Jordan and Hermon are the northern edge of the land.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes תְּהוֹם (te-hom, v.8) literally as two depths, the upper waters and the lower, calling across the space between them, and hears the whole psalm as that call: something below thirsting for something above, and the answer arriving as flood rather than as drink. In this reading the refrain is not consolation but instruction. The speaker addresses his nefesh, the lowest of the soul's levels, the one bound to the body and to appetite, and tells it to wait, because what it wants is real and what it can reach on its own is not. The last word of the psalm, my God, is then the point of the exercise: the same face that was His in verse 6 is turned toward the speaker by verse 12.
