Tehilim 35: Contend, LORD, with those who contend with me

תְּהִלִּים ל״ה · לְדָוִד רִיבָה
HomePs 35
Twenty-eight verses of a man asking to be defended in court and in the field at the same time. It is one of the harshest psalms in the book: it asks for the attackers to be shamed, tripped, and caught in their own net, and it never softens that. It is not part of the fixed daily service; it belongs to the monthly cycle that reads the whole book, and to the custom of saying Tehillim for someone in trouble. For a learner it is a grammar drill. Almost every enemy is a participle used as a noun (those who fight me, those who seek my life, those who plot my harm), and almost every request is a jussive, the short third-person form that means let them be.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three rounds, each with the same shape: a complaint, a petition against the attackers, then a vow to praise in public. They close at verses 9 and 10, at verse 18, and at verses 27 and 28. The vocabulary is mixed deliberately. Verses 1 to 3 are military, with shield, buckler and spear, and from verse 11 the setting is a trial: witnesses of violence, judge me, my case, my judgment. The verb at רִיבָה (ri-va, v.1) is the legal one, to conduct a lawsuit, and יְרִיבַי (ye-ri-vai, v.1) is built from the same root, so the psalm is already inside the courtroom before any weapon is named.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three difficulties, all in the middle section. מָעוֹג (ma-og, v.16) is a rare word usually taken as a cake or a round loaf, which would make the line mockers who perform for their food, parasites at a table; the reading is not secure and translations differ. נֵכִים (ne-chim, v.15) is equally uncertain: smitten ones, smiters, or people the speaker does not know, and the ancient versions divide. The third is not a rare word but a real ambiguity: תָשׁוּב (ta-shuv, v.13) says the prayer returns upon my bosom, which can mean the prayer came back unanswered, or that a prayer said for someone else comes home to the one who said it. Both readings are old and both are still printed.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The heading puts the psalm in David's mouth, and verses 13 and 14 are commonly read against the Saul chapters of Samuel: the man being hunted is the one who wore sackcloth and fasted when his hunters were ill, and who twice had Saul within reach and did not strike. Read that way the psalm is less a request for revenge than a demand that the record be entered. The line at עַצְמוֹתַי (atz-mo-tai, v.10), all my bones, is the verse people reach for when they explain why the whole body moves during prayer, on the argument that praise is not the tongue's work alone. And חִנָּם (chi-nam, v.7), without cause, is the phrase that becomes the standard name for hatred with no reason behind it, the failing the tradition blames for the loss of the Second Temple.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The angel of the LORD appears twice, at וּמַלְאַךְ (u-mal-ach, v.5) and again in the following verse, once in front of the enemy driving and once behind pursuing. The mystical reading takes the pair as two faces of a single measure, judgement that pushes away and judgement that follows, and sets it beside the same figure in the neighbouring psalm, who encamps around those who fear Him. The other line read this way is תָשׁוּב (ta-shuv, v.13): a prayer poured out for someone who will later hunt you does not vanish when it goes unanswered, it returns to the chest it came from and does its work there. On that reading the harshest verses of the psalm and its strangest act of kindness are one movement seen from opposite ends.
