Tehilim 125: Those who trust are like Mount Zion

תְּהִלִּים קכ״ה · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 125
Five verses in the middle of the Songs of Ascents, and unlike Psalm 120 this one has arrived: the first two verses stand in the hills around Jerusalem and look at how the city is held. The psalm names no person. Everyone in it is a participle or an adjective, those who trust, the righteous, the good, the upright, those who bend aside, those who do wrong, so the whole argument is about kinds of people rather than about anybody in particular. Verse 3 is the hinge and the surprising part: the reason given for limiting how long oppression may last is not the suffering it causes but what it would do to the people under it.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two verses of description, one of limit, two of prayer. The description works by a single image held from two sides: those who trust are like the mountain, and the mountain is itself ringed by mountains, so the psalm gets from geology to protection without arguing for it. Verse 3 then admits what the first two verses seemed to deny, that a rod of wickedness can lie on the land of the righteous, and restricts only its duration. Verses 4 and 5 turn the description into two requests pointing opposite ways, and the final phrase steps outside both.

רמז · Remez: allusion

יִמּוֹט (yi-mot, v.1) and the corresponding word in Psalm 93 verse 1 are the same verb, not be shaken, said there of the world and here of the person who trusts, which is the whole comparison in one root. שֵׁבֶט (she-vet, v.3) is the ordinary word for a rod or a sceptre, and also the word for a tribe, so it carries both the beating and the authority that permits it. גּוֹרַל (go-ral, v.3) is the lot, the word for land assigned by casting lots, so what the rod rests on is not property but an allotment, something received rather than earned. עֲקַלְקַלּוֹתָם (a-kal-ka-lo-tam, v.5) occurs nowhere else in the book: a reduplicated form of the root for crooked, so the ways are not merely bent but bent and bent again. אֶת (et, v.5) is disputed. Read as the object marker it makes the crooked ones and the workers of wrong one group; read as the preposition with, which is how most translations take it here, it makes them two groups sent off together.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The reasoning of verse 3 is the part that gets discussed, because it does not say oppression will end for the sake of the oppressed. It says it will end so that the righteous do not stretch out their hands to wrongdoing, which treats sustained injustice as corrosive to the people enduring it, and reads the limit as protection of their integrity rather than of their comfort. The final phrase has been read two ways that both survive in use: as a blessing pronounced over the whole people once the sorting of verses 4 and 5 is done, and as a liturgical closing that turns a psalm about two classes into a prayer for one nation. The same three words end Psalm 128, and those two verses are the only places in the book where they appear.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes the two circles of verse 2 as one figure drawn twice. The hills around Jerusalem are visible and fixed, and the surrounding named in the second half is neither, so the verse teaches how to read the invisible from the visible rather than instead of it. Trust in הַבֹּטְחִים (ha-bot-chim, v.1) is a participle, a continuous action and not a decision made once, which is why the psalm can compare a person to a mountain without claiming that the person is strong. On this reading verse 3 describes a limit built into the structure itself: the rod may pass over the allotment but cannot take up residence on it, because what is allotted was never the rod's to hold.
