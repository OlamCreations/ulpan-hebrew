Tehilim 76: God is known in Judah

תְּהִלִּים ע״ו · לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת
HomePs 76
Thirteen verses from the Asaph collection, and a battle poem with no battle in it. The fighting is over before the first line, and all that is reported is the wreckage: broken arrows, a shield, a sword, soldiers asleep who cannot find their own hands. No enemy is named, no year, no king, and Israel does nothing. Two things are worth watching. The verbs run in the past until verse 5, where the psalm turns and speaks to God directly for the rest of the poem. And the four-letter name appears exactly once, in verse 12; everywhere else the psalm says God, which is the signature of the collection it belongs to.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm has two tenses and a hinge between them. Verses 2 to 4 are past: God became known, His shelter came to be, He broke the weapons. Verse 5 turns to direct address, and everything after it is present and general. Nothing in the poem names the enemy, the year or the king. The battle survives only in its debris, broken arrows and soldiers who cannot find their hands, and no Israelite is reported doing anything at all. Verse 12 is the single place the four-letter name appears, לַיהֹוָה (lai-ho-va, v.12); everywhere else the psalm says God, which is the mark of the collection it sits inside.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three phrases here resist confident translation. At מֵהַרְרֵי (me-har-rei, v.5) the words read mountains of prey, and the prefix can be comparative, more majestic than the mountains of prey, or spatial, from the mountains of prey. There is no close parallel to settle it and the reading is disputed. The verb at אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ (esh-tol-lu, v.6) is a rare form, taken by some as stripped bare and by others as stupefied. And שְׁאֵרִית (she-e-rit, v.11), the remnant of furies, leaves it open whether God girds Himself with what is left of human rage or ties it up out of the way. One quieter detail lights the first crux: the noun for dwelling at וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ (um-o-na-to, v.3) is elsewhere the word for a lion's den, so a psalm with a lair in Zion and mountains of prey may be running one image twice.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

A reader looking for the event behind the debris reaches for 2 Kings 19 and Isaiah 37, where the Assyrian army encamped against Jerusalem is struck in the night and found in the morning without a battle having been fought. Verses 6 and 7 fit that scene closely: sleep, paralysis, chariot and horse, and no human action on the defending side anywhere in the psalm. The identification is inference, not something the text states, and the psalm keeps the enemy anonymous on purpose. Verse 11 has been read on its own as a principle about history rather than about one night: rage that intends the opposite ends up producing praise, which turns the fury of nations into an instrument instead of an obstacle.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The place is named twice, Salem at בְשָׁלֵם (ve-sha-lem, v.3) and then Zion, and the first of the two is heard in the tradition as the word for whole and as the root of peace. Read that way the geography carries the argument: the weapons stop working at the place whose name means completeness, and what breaks them is not a counter-army but a rebuke, a word issued from heaven and received on earth as silence. The remnant of wrath in verse 11 belongs to the same reading, a measure held back rather than spent, since a judgement whose stated purpose is to save the lowly has to stop somewhere short of everyone.
