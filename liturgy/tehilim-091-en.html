Tehilim 91: Whoever dwells in the shelter of the Most High

תְּהִלִּים צ״א · יֹשֵׁב בְּסֵתֶר
HomePs 91
Sixteen verses of protection, and one of the most used psalms in the book. It is said at the bedtime Shema, on Saturday night after Vihi Noam, and at a burial. The psalm has no heading and asks for nothing, which is rare here: it is all reassurance, arranged by the clock and the compass, night and day in verse 5, dark and noon in verse 6, your side and your right hand in verse 7. Watch who is speaking, because it changes three times: verse 1 describes a man, verse 2 is a first person confession, verses 3 to 13 address a you, and from verse 14 God speaks and the you becomes a him. Four names of God appear in the first two verses alone. In the printed monthly cycle of Tehilim it falls on the nineteenth day.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A psalm of protection with no petition in it, which is unusual. The threats come in two families, hunting and disease, and they keep trading metaphors, so a plague can be a trap and an arrow can behave like a fever. The structure is a set of oppositions that between them cover everything: night and day, dark and noon, your side and your right hand, the body in verse 10 and the tent it sleeps in. Nothing here promises that the danger stays away from other people. Verse 7 has a thousand falling close enough to touch and verse 8 has you watching it. What is promised is that it does not arrive at you, and at verse 14 God says why, in terms that are entirely about what the man did first.

רמז · Remez: allusion

אַתָּה (a-ta, v.9) is the crux of the psalm. The line runs because you, the LORD my refuge, and the persons collide: the possessive in my refuge belongs to the speaker of verse 2, אֹמַר (o-mar, v.2), not to the you being addressed. Some read it as a quotation, the addressee repeating verse 2 back; others take the form as archaic and read it without possessive force. It is genuinely unsettled, and translations diverge here more than anywhere else in the psalm. וְסֹחֵרָה (ve-so-che-ra, v.4) appears nowhere else in the Bible, so its sense is read off the shield standing next to it and comes out as buckler or encircling armour. מִקֶּטֶב (mi-ke-tev, v.6) belongs to a small family of nouns for destruction that later tradition treats as proper names of harmful spirits, which is part of why this psalm ends up on amulets. And שַׁחַל (sha-chal, v.13) opens a line of four animals in two matched pairs, a big cat and a snake each time.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Psalm 90 is headed a prayer of Moses and this one carries no heading of its own, so the tradition reads it as Moses still speaking, with the wilderness as the danger in view. Verse 11, the messengers charged to guard you, became the seat of the idea that a person acquires guardians by conduct rather than by birth, which is why מַלְאָכָיו (mal-a-chav, v.11) is usually rendered angels rather than messengers in translation. Rabbinic sources know the psalm as the song of afflictions and record its use against harm, and the same body of sources records a sharp objection to whispering verses over a wound. Both attitudes survive in practice, which is roughly why the psalm sits at the edges of the day and of the week rather than in the middle of the service: at the bedtime Shema, at the close of Shabbat, and at a burial.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Four names of God are used in the first two verses: עֶלְיוֹן (eli-on, v.1) and שַׁדַּי (sha-dai, v.1) in the opening line, then the four letter name and my God in verse 2. The kabbalistic reading takes the sequence as a descent through degrees of concealment, from a name that cannot be possessed to one a person can attach a suffix to, which is what the word seter, the hiding place of the first line, is already doing. The verb of חָשַׁק (cha-shak, v.14) is a verb of desire rather than obedience, so the protection described in the previous thirteen verses is presented as a response to longing. The last verse, אֹרֶךְ (o-rech, v.16), gives length of days rather than exemption from them, and in many rites that verse is said twice when the psalm is recited on Saturday night, so the psalm ends by returning to its own final measure instead of resolving past it.
