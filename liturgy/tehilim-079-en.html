Tehilim 79: Nations have come into Your inheritance

תְּהִלִּים ע״ט · מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף
HomePs 79
Thirteen verses of communal lament attributed to Asaph, spoken after the sanctuary has been sacked and while the dead are still lying unburied. The shape is the standard one for this kind of psalm: report of the disaster, the question how long, a demand for justice, a petition, and a vow of praise at the end. Verses 6 and 7 are the Shefoch Chamatcha of the Passover seder, said with the door open. Watch how the enemy is handled grammatically: always a plural they, never named, never given a king.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm divides cleanly. Verses 1 to 4 report what happened, in finished verbs and with almost no adjectives: they came, they defiled, they made, they gave, they poured out. Verse 5 asks how long. Verses 6 and 7 ask for the wrath to be redirected. Verses 8 to 12 are the petition proper, and verse 13 is the vow of praise. Note that the temple appears once, in verse 1, and is then dropped. What the rest of the psalm is about is people: servants, faithful ones, neighbours, prisoners, us.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verses 6 and 7 stand almost word for word in Jeremiah 10:25, where the verbs of verse 7 are plural throughout. Here אָכַל (a-chal, v.7) is singular and הֵשַׁמּוּ (he-sha-mu, v.7) is plural inside one sentence. Whether that is a scribal slip or a deliberate collapse of the invading army into a single devourer is disputed, and the psalm gives no way to decide. The word חֲסִידֶיךָ (cha-si-dei-cha, v.2), built on the root of chesed, is the term a later generation took as a group name, and 1 Maccabees 7:17 quotes verses 2 and 3 of this psalm over the bodies of the Hasideans killed under Alcimus.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

שְׁפֹךְ (shfoch, v.6), pour out, opens the passage said at the seder when the door is opened. Its position in the evening is deliberate: it falls after the grace after meals and before the rest of Hallel, so the harshest lines in the Haggadah sit between an act of hospitality and a song of praise. The date of the psalm is itself disputed. Read one way, verse 1 is the Babylonian destruction of 586 BCE and the temple is Solomon's. Read another way, the desecration is the Seleucid one, and the psalm and the book of Maccabees are near contemporaries rather than old text and later citation. The psalm names no king and no empire, so nothing in it settles the question.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The argument of verse 9 is made twice inside a single verse: help us for the sake of the glory of שְׁמֶךָ (shme-cha, v.9), and atone for the sake of שְׁמֶךָ (shme-cha, v.9). Nothing at all is claimed for Israel's merit. In the mystical reading the name is not a label but the aspect of God turned toward the world, so a ruined sanctuary and a mocked people register as damage to the name itself, and repairing one is repairing the other. Verse 12 then reads as more than revenge: what is to be returned שִׁבְעָתַיִם (shiv-a-ta-yim, v.12) is specified as the reproach aimed at God, not the one aimed at us.
