Tehilim 48: Great is the LORD in the city of our God

תְּהִלִּים מ״ח · שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 48
Fifteen verses from the sons of Korah about a city rather than a person, and the psalm said on the second day of the week in the Temple list of daily psalms (Mishnah Tamid 7:4). It moves in three blocks: the city described from outside, kings who come and break apart at the sight of it, and then a congregation standing inside the temple. The last three verses are the part to watch as a learner: a string of plural imperatives, walk, circle, count, set your heart, traverse, tell, all aimed at making the next generation see what this one saw.

The city drops out of view in the last verse and the object of trust turns out to be the one who lives in it. The final two words are the hardest in the psalm and are read in several ways.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements. Verses 2 to 4 describe the city from outside: high, beautiful, praised, and defended by its occupant rather than its stonework. Verses 5 to 8 tell a story with almost no detail, kings gathering, seeing something, and coming apart, with the psalm never saying what they saw. Verses 9 to 15 are spoken by a plural we standing inside, and the pivot is the pairing of שָׁמַעְנוּ (sha-ma-nu, v.9) with רָאִינוּ (ra-i-nu, v.9): what the fathers reported, this congregation has now seen for itself. The ending is practical. Walk the perimeter, count the towers, look at the ramparts, then go and tell a generation that has not seen it. The psalm ends by handing the evidence forward.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two cruxes and one geographical oddity. The oddity is יַרְכְּתֵי (yar-ktei, v.3) with the word after it, literally the far sides of the north, which is the standard biblical phrase for a remote mountain of the gods and is commonly read as language borrowed from Canaanite descriptions of Mount Zaphon and applied to a hill in Judah; that reading is widely held but it is a reconstruction, not something the psalm states. The first crux is the verb of פַּסְּגוּ (pas-gu, v.14), which occurs only here in the Bible: translations give traverse, pass between, and consider, and no reading is secure. The second is the last two words, מוּת (mut, v.15) and the preposition before it, read variously as beyond death, unto death, and, by taking the two words as one, as eternity; some connect the phrase to the musical heading of Psalm 9. The dispute is old and unresolved, and the theology people take from the verse depends on which way it is read.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

This is the psalm assigned to the second day of the week in the list of Temple psalms in Mishnah Tamid 7:4, and the Talmud in Rosh Hashanah 31a explains the daily psalms as matching the days of creation, the second day being the day of division. Read that way, the opening word of גָּדוֹל (ga-dol, v.2) is about a God who separates and then rules over what He has separated, which is exactly what the kings of verse 5 discover the hard way. The reading tradition also takes the tour of verses 13 and 14 as a rule for teaching rather than tourism: the towers are counted so that the count survives the counter. Later readers of the destroyed city read the same imperatives against ruins, which turns a survey of walls into an act of memory.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the kabbalistic reading the city is not primarily a place but a receiving vessel, the lowest of the divine measures, which is why she is spoken of in the feminine throughout and why her palaces, ramparts and towers can be counted at all: what can be measured is what has been given form. The kings who look and flee stand for a force that cannot bear an unmediated encounter with what is inside the walls, so it breaks apart at the boundary rather than crossing it. The last verse is then read as a route rather than a comfort: the same guide who leads within the city is said to lead beyond the place where measure stops, which is the sense the mystical reading gives to leading beyond death.
