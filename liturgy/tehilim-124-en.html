Tehilim 124: Our help is in the name of the LORD

תְּהִלִּים קכ״ד · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 124
Eight verses in the first person plural, and that is the lesson. Almost every line carries the suffix that means us or our: for us, against us, swallowed us, swept us away, our soul, gave us, we escaped, our help. The psalm describes a disaster that did not happen, using a construction English has to work around: had it not been for the LORD, then. The conditional is opened in verse 1, restarted in verse 2, and never formally closed, because verse 6 breaks off into a blessing instead. Three verses in a row begin with the same word for then, and the danger changes shape at each one, from a mouth to a flood to a trap. One of the fifteen Songs of Ascents, said in many Ashkenazi communities after Minchah on the Shabbatot between Sukkot and Passover, and its last verse ends with the same three words that end verse 2 of Psalm 121.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The shape is a sentence that breaks. Verses 1 and 2 open a counterfactual, verses 3 to 5 give three things that would have followed, and then verse 6 gives up on the syntax and blesses instead, which is more convincing than a completed sentence would have been. The three dangers escalate by changing category rather than by getting bigger: a predator, a flood, a snare. The rescue itself is never described. Nobody fights, no wall holds, no army arrives; the trap simply breaks in verse 7, in the passive, with no agent named, and verse 8 supplies the agent as a name rather than as an action.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The language of this psalm is late, and two markers show it plainly. The relative is written as a prefix in שֶׁהָיָה (she-ha-ya, v.1), שֶׁהָיָה (she-ha-ya, v.2) and שֶׁלֹּא (she-lo, v.6), where classical prose would use the separate word asher, and אֲזַי (a-zai, v.3) is a form of the word for then that belongs to the later stage of the language. Both features run through the Songs of Ascents as a group. Two words are worth checking against a translation. נַחְלָה (nach-la, v.4) is an unusual form, read by some as a noun meaning torrent and by others adverbially, so English versions differ on whether it names the water or describes how it came. הַזֵּידוֹנִים (ha-zei-do-nim, v.5) is built from a root that covers both boiling and presumption, which is why the water is called proud in older translations and raging in newer ones. Neither is wrong; the Hebrew holds both.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The enemy in this psalm has no name, no nationality and no weapon, and the danger is given only as images: teeth, water, a snare. That is what makes it reusable, and the instruction in verse 1 makes the intention explicit by handing the words to Israel to repeat rather than reporting one man's rescue. Read against the Torah's water crossings, verse 4 inverts them: there the waters go over the pursuers, here they are described as almost going over us. The psalm never claims a specific deliverance, so each generation saying it supplies its own, which is the opposite of how a historical psalm works.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes the three dangers as three levels of threat to the same thing, since נַפְשֵׁנוּ (naf-she-nu, v.4), נַפְשֵׁנוּ (naf-she-nu, v.5) and נַפְשֵׁנוּ (naf-she-nu, v.7) are one word, our soul, appearing at each stage. What is at risk is never property or life but nefesh, and the bird in verse 7 is that soul seen from outside, small and caught by something it did not see. The breaking of the snare is passive on purpose in this reading: the escape is not an act of the bird, and the bird's own effort is the one thing the verse leaves out. Verse 8 then says where the strength was, in the name, which is the standard way of pointing at an action without describing it.
