Tehilim 31: Into Your hand I entrust my spirit

תְּהִלִּים ל״א · לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 31
Twenty-five verses that swing between panic and confidence, twice over: the speaker is slandered, besieged, avoided in the street and forgotten like a dead man, and each time the description of the trouble hits bottom he turns and says again that he has taken refuge. Verse 6 is the line most people know before they know the psalm, since its first half is said at bedtime and reworked in the third person in Adon Olam. For a learner the psalm is a drill in the suffix: almost every verb in it carries an object suffix meaning me, and almost every noun a possessive suffix meaning my or Your. Watch the space vocabulary too, because verse 10 says literally that it is tight for the speaker and verse 9 answers with feet set in a wide place.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm says the same thing twice from opposite ends. Verses 2 to 9 run refuge, request, and thanks for a rescue already given, and close at their most confident with feet set in a wide place. Verse 10 then starts again from nothing, and the second lament goes much further down: wasted eyes, failing strength, neighbours crossing the street, a body treated as a broken pot. Verses 15 to 19 repeat the petition almost word for word, and verses 20 to 25 answer it with praise. The last two verses are the real ending, because the grammar changes: after twenty-two verses of I and You, the speaker turns and gives a plural instruction to other people. Nothing in the situation is reported as fixed. What has changed is who is being spoken to.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm is built on a contrast between narrow and wide. Verse 10 does not say I am in distress, it says צַר (tzar, v.10), it is tight, and the rescue in the verse before is בַמֶּרְחָב (va-mer-chav, v.9), a broad place, room to put your feet down. The siege of מָצוֹר (ma-tzor, v.22) is the same idea made literal: a city with walls is also a city with nowhere to go. Two other threads hold the halves together. The plea not to be put to shame appears in identical form at אֵבוֹשָׁה (e-vo-sha, v.2) and again at אֵבוֹשָׁה (e-vo-sha, v.18), bracketing everything between them. And מָגוֹר (ma-gor, v.14) with the word after it, terror on every side, is the phrase the book of Jeremiah uses repeatedly, including as a name given to a man in Jeremiah 20. Which text is drawing on which is not settled, and the psalm carries no date that would settle it.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The first half of verse 6 was lifted out of the psalm and given a nightly job: בְּיָדְךָ (byad-cha, v.6) and the two words after it are said before sleep, in the reading of the Shema at bedtime, and Adon Olam reworks the same clause into the third person. Read that way, sleep is rehearsal. The verb is the one used for leaving goods with a trusted keeper, so the soul is not given up but deposited, with the expectation of collecting it in the morning. The same phrase opens verse 16, where what is deposited is not the spirit but the timing of a life, which extends the reading from one night to a whole biography. The closing verse also has a near twin: חִזְקוּ (chiz-ku, v.25) and what follows it stand in the singular at the end of Psalm 27, the psalm many communities say every day from the start of Elul, so the same encouragement reaches a learner from two directions.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the mystical reading the deposit of verse 6 is taken literally as the nightly return of the soul to its source, and the morning blessing over the soul restored is its answer. Shelter is the other image the reading works on. Verse 21 puts the protected inside בְּסֻכָּה (be-su-ka, v.21), a booth, immediately after saying they are hidden in the shelter of the face, and a booth is the standard image for a covering that surrounds rather than a wall that blocks: it is closed on all sides yet open above, so the protection and the exposure are the same structure. Read this way the psalm is not asking for the siege to lift. It is asking for the space inside the siege to be experienced as a shelter, which is why the last word of the psalm is about waiting and not about deliverance.
