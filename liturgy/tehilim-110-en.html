Tehilim 110: Sit at My right hand

תְּהִלִּים ק״י · לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 110
Seven verses, a royal psalm built on two oracles: one in verse 1 about a throne, one in verse 4 about a priesthood, each introduced by a formula and each followed by its expansion. It is short, famous, and hard. Verse 3 is one of the most textually difficult verses in the whole book, and the ancient versions do not agree with the Masoretic text about what it says; this page prints the Masoretic text and says where the other readings come from. Watch the right hand: in verse 1 the king is seated at God's, and by verse 5 the Lord stands at the king's.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two oracles, two expansions and a coda. Verse 1 gives the throne and verses 2 and 3 unfold it; verse 4 gives the priesthood and verses 5 and 6 unfold what a priest-king does; verse 7 is a single picture at the end. The clearest structural fact is a pair of right hands: at לִימִינִי (li-mi-ni, v.1) the one addressed is told to sit at God's right hand, and at יְמִינְךָ (ye-min-cha, v.5) the Lord is at his. The second pair is a head: רֹאשׁ (rosh, v.6) is crushed and רֹאשׁ (rosh, v.7) is lifted, the same word both times. Two of the seven verses, 3 and 6, are difficult enough that translating them is a choice rather than a rendering, and readers should know which choice they are being handed.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 3 is the crux, and every clause in it has been read more than one way. נְדָבֹת (ne-da-vot, v.3) is an abstract plural, willingnesses or freewill offerings, standing where a verb or an adjective is wanted, so the line comes out either as your people volunteer or as your people are willingness itself. חֵילֶךָ (chei-le-cha, v.3) is force, army or wealth, which makes the day either a muster or a battle. בְּהַדְרֵי (be-had-rei, v.3) in the Masoretic text is the splendours of, giving in holy splendour; a nearly identical spelling, one letter away, would give the mountains of, and translations that print on the holy mountains follow that reading rather than the pointed text. מֵרֶחֶם (me-re-chem, v.3) and מִשְׁחָר (mish-char, v.3) give from the womb of the dawn, an image with no parallel elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible. טַל (tal, v.3) and יַלְדֻתֶיךָ (yal-du-tei-cha, v.3) give the dew of your youth, read either as troops arriving like dew or as the king's own youth renewed. The ancient Greek translation vocalized the last words differently and produced a verb of begetting instead of the noun youth, which is why Greek and Latin bibles read from the womb before the morning star I have begotten you. On the Masoretic pointing the verse says nothing about begetting. One more vocalization matters, in verse 1: לַאדֹנִי (la-do-ni, v.1) is pointed la-doni, my lord, the ordinary form for addressing a human superior, and not as the divine name. Translations that render it as a second divine title are reading against the vowels this page prints.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Jewish reading of this psalm has long refused to leave the one addressed unplaceable, and it has produced more than one answer. A passage in the Talmud at Nedarim 32b takes up verse 4 in connection with Genesis 14 and Melchizedek and reads the priesthood as something transferred rather than shared. Medieval Jewish commentary largely follows that direction and identifies the addressee as Abraham, which turns verses 5 and 6 into the defeat of the kings in Genesis 14 and verse 7 into the stream Abraham drinks from on the road home. A second Jewish line reads the addressee as David himself, spoken of in the third person by one of his own court singers, which is the simplest explanation of the awkward my lord. A third reads it of the messianic king. In Christian tradition the psalm is read of Jesus and it is among the psalms the New Testament quotes most; that reading depends on taking the fourth word of verse 1 as a divine title, which the Masoretic vowels do not support, and on the Greek rendering of verse 3 rather than the Hebrew. The disagreement is old and it is genuinely a disagreement about which text is being read, not only about what it means.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

One figure holds two offices that the rest of the Hebrew Bible keeps apart. Kings are not priests and priests are not kings, and the single Genesis figure who is both is named here at מַלְכִּי (mal-ki, v.4) and צֶדֶק (tze-dek, v.4) as two separate words, my king and righteousness, and he stands entirely outside the covenant line. Mystical reading takes that as the point: the priesthood promised here is older than Aaron and older than Sinai, which is why the oath at נִשְׁבַּע (nish-ba, v.4) has to be one that cannot be revoked. The imagery of verse 3 works the same way from the other side. Womb, dawn and dew are all images of something that arrives before anyone has worked for it, and dew in particular falls unasked and unwatched. Read that way, the difficulty of the verse is not only damage in transmission but the subject itself, since what is being described is an origin, and an origin is the part that resists being put into a sentence.
