Lecha Dodi: Come, My Beloved

לְכָה דוֹדִי · Friday evening · welcoming the Sabbath bride · Alkabetz, Safed c.1540
HomeLecha Dodi
The signature hymn of Kabbalat Shabbat, sung in every Jewish community on Friday evening. Written by Rabbi Shlomo HaLevi Alkabetz (Safed, ~1540 CE), a kabbalist of the Arizal's circle. Nine stanzas + a refrain that's repeated after each. The poem walks Shabbat, personified as a bride arriving to meet her beloved (the people of Israel), through the streets of Jerusalem and back to the Temple. During the final stanza, the entire congregation traditionally stands and turns toward the door of the synagogue, bowing to greet Shabbat as she enters, then turning back. An acrostic on the author's name (שלמה הלוי, "Shlomo HaLevi") is hidden in the first letters of stanzas 1-9. This page teaches the refrain + opening stanza.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Friday evening, the centerpiece of Kabbalat Shabbat (the service that welcomes Shabbat). Sung between Psalm 29 and Psalm 92. The whole congregation stands for the final stanza.
  • The Shabbat-as-bride metaphor, based on a Talmudic story (Shabbat 119a) where Rabbi Yannai used to put on Shabbat clothes and call out "Come, O bride!" as Shabbat approached. Alkabetz turned this into a full liturgical poem 1,400 years later. The metaphor: Shabbat is the bride; Israel is the groom; the encounter is consummated each Friday night.
  • The author's signature, the first letters of stanzas 1-8 spell שלמה הלוי (Shlomo HaLevi). The 9th stanza is the climactic "Bo'i b'shalom" (Come in peace), when the congregation rises and turns.
  • Shamor and Zachor, the two versions of the Shabbat commandment (Deut 5:12 says shamor "keep"; Ex 20:8 says zachor "remember"). The rabbis say both words were spoken simultaneously by God, a divine paradox impossible for human speech but accomplished in revelation. The Friday-night candles symbolize this dual command.
  • Vocabulary:
    • l'cha (לְכָה), "come!" Imperative of הָלַךְ (to go), used with directional l'+pronoun. The poetic form l'cha emphasizes the addressee.
    • dodi (דוֹדִי), "my beloved". The same word the female speaker uses for her lover throughout the Song of Songs (Dodi li va'ani lo, "my beloved is mine and I am his").
    • kalah (כַּלָּה), "bride". Same root as kol (כל, all) and kli (vessel), the bride is the "complete vessel" of the relationship.
    • n'kab'lah (נְקַבְּלָה), "let us receive!" Piel cohortative of ק.ב.ל (to receive). Same root as kabbalah (the received tradition / mystical theology).
  • Hundreds of melodies, Lecha Dodi has more recorded melodies than any other Jewish hymn. Carlebach, Yemenite, Polish, Andalusian, Sephardic, Hasidic, pick one. Most synagogues use 2-3 melodies in rotation, switching mid-hymn for variety.

♦ About this prayer

A 1540 wedding poem written for the weekly remarriage of God and Israel. The signature of every Jewish Friday night.

Rabbi Shlomo Alkabetz (~1505-1584)

Born in Salonika (Greece), Alkabetz migrated as a young scholar to Safed, the Galilean mountain town that became the global center of Kabbalah in the mid-16th century. There he joined the circle of Rabbi Moshe Cordovero (his brother-in-law) and was a senior figure when the young Rabbi Isaac Luria (the Arizal) arrived to revolutionize Kabbalistic thought.

Alkabetz wrote many poems, but only one survived into universal liturgy: Lecha Dodi. The custom in Safed was for the kabbalists to walk out of the synagogue into the fields on Friday afternoon, dressed in white, to physically greet Shabbat as she descended over the hills. Lecha Dodi was composed for that procession. Within 50 years the custom spread to every Jewish community.

The 9 stanzas: a tour of Jerusalem

The full poem moves through stages:

Stanza 1: "Shamor and Zachor", the dual commandment.
Stanzas 2-3: the call to wake up Jerusalem from her ruined state, "Shake off the dust, arise!"
Stanzas 4-5: the in-gathering of the exiles, the rebuilding of the Temple.
Stanzas 6-7: the defeat of Israel's oppressors, the climactic political/messianic stanzas.
Stanza 8: the joy of the people in their restored land.
Stanza 9: Bo'i b'shalom, "Come in peace, crown of her husband", the moment of greeting, when the congregation rises and turns to face the synagogue door.

So Lecha Dodi is not only a wedding song, it's a compact messianic vision in 9 stanzas: from exile to rebuilding to gathering to redemption to encounter.

The turn at "Bo'i b'shalom"

The most visually distinctive moment in Friday-night liturgy: at the start of the 9th stanza, every member of the congregation rises and turns 180° to face the back of the synagogue (the entrance). The bows alternate: bow right for "Bo'i kalah" (come, bride), bow left for "Bo'i kalah" repeated, bow forward for the third repetition. Then turn back to face the front of the synagogue. The bride has entered.

This turn is one of the few moments in mainstream Jewish prayer that uses choreography. Different communities do it slightly differently; the body language is the message. After this turn, Shabbat is functionally present in the room.
