Am Yisrael Chai: The people of Israel lives

Eyal Golan, 2023 · the verse under the title, Genesis 45:3 · עַם יִשְׂרָאֵל חַי
HomeAm Yisrael Chai
Eyal Golan's "Am Yisrael Chai" came out on 19 October 2023, twelve days into the war, written and composed by Avi Ohayon and Ofir Cohen. Its words are new and copyrighted, so they are not printed on this page — the links at the bottom take you to them, and to the recording. What this page holds instead is the sentence the whole song stands on. "Am Yisrael Chai" is a slogan older than the state, and its life as a sung line begins with Shlomo Carlebach in 1965, whose lyric was built out of one question in Genesis: Joseph, revealed to his brothers, asking whether his father is still alive — הַעוֹד (ha-od) אָבִי (a-vi) חָי (chai). Carlebach turned the question into an answer, od avinu chai, "our father still lives", and set "am yisrael chai" beside it. Learn this verse word by word and you have the grammar of the title phrase, and the one-syllable word it lands on.

The title of every "Am Yisrael Chai" song is three words, and the third one is here: חָי (chai), one syllable, "alive" or "lives" — the same word whether it is an adjective or a verb, which is why the slogan can be read as a description and as a claim at once. In the verse it comes at the end of a question. הַעוֹד (ha-od) is two pieces glued together, the question-marker ha- in front of od, "still", so the phrase asks "is he still...?" — and אָבִי (a-vi), "my father", is Jacob, whom the brothers had told Joseph about a chapter earlier. Carlebach's lyric keeps od and chai, changes "my father" to "our father", and drops the question: od avinu chai, "our father still lives". Then the second half of the verse is the brothers, unable to speak. נִבְהֲלוּ (niv-ha-lu) is stronger than "surprised": the root means to be terrified, thrown into confusion, and it is the same root as the modern word for panic.

♫ Singing notes

  • Golan's recording sits at a steady 105 beats a minute in D minor. The chorus is the title phrase itself — Am Yisrael Chai — sung over B♭ and F and answered by a line over Gm and Dm; the one piece of Hebrew you already have from the verse above is the one the whole room sings. Land on chai.
  • The song has three shapes and you can hear where you are from the chords: the verses sit on the Dm–Gm–A7–Dm turnaround, the bridge lifts to B♭–F–Gm–Dm (with a C on the way down), and the chorus stays up on B♭–F–Gm until an A pulls it back to Dm. Learn the turnaround first; the rest is the same four chords in a different order.
  • The chart at negina.co.il gives the whole song in Dm and also a capo-5 version played with Am shapes (Am, Dm, E, C, G, F), which is the easier grip for most guitarists and puts the song in the same sounding key.
  • After the chorus comes a long wordless o-o-o over Dm–F–C–Gm, ending on the title again. It is the part a crowd carries when it does not know the words, and it is where the song is loudest.
  • If you learn the Genesis line as spoken Hebrew, put the stress where the transliteration puts it: ha-OD a-VI CHAI.

♦ About this song

The song

Released as a single on 19 October 2023, during the war, and later on an album of the same name. Words and music by Avi Ohayon and Ofir Cohen; arrangement and production by Ofir Cohen and Shimon Yechia; the recording runs about three and a half minutes. Its proceeds were pledged to ERAN, Israel's emotional first-aid line. It reached number one on Media Forest's Israeli airplay chart, number five on the Galgalatz chart, and finished the Hebrew year 5784 as Media Forest's number one; by August 2025 the official video had passed 33 million views. The video is footage of soldiers, people holding flags, memorial candles being lit, rescue volunteers, solidarity rallies and worshippers at the Western Wall. In it, as the English-language coverage puts it, he sings about the return of the hostages and the solidarity and resilience of the Israeli people — the words themselves are his writers' and are linked below.

The slogan, before the song

"Am Yisrael Chai" is much older than any recording of it. A version of it appears in a Zionist songbook as early as 1895 and with sheet music in a 1927 collection. In 1933 Rabbi Stephen Wise closed the World Jewish Conference's boycott address with it. On 20 April 1945, five days after the liberation of Bergen-Belsen, a Shabbat service for survivors was recorded by the BBC: they sang Hatikvah, and a chaplain called out "Am Yisrael Chai" over the end of it — the recording survives. In 1965 Shlomo Carlebach wrote his song of that name for the Student Struggle for Soviet Jewry, at Jacob Birnbaum's request, and first sang it in public at their rally outside the Soviet mission to the UN on 4 April 1965. The Forward has called the phrase second only to Hatikvah as an anthem of the Jewish people. Every song titled "Am Yisrael Chai" since — and there were several new ones in October 2023 — is standing on that history.

What the song quotes

Besides the title, the second verse sings a line straight out of Hatikvah — the hope, two thousand years old, hatikvah bat shnot alpayim — which is Imber's 1878 text and is on this site word by word as the first song in this section. The chorus closes on "the faith is not lost", lo avdah ha'emunah, an echo of Hatikvah's od lo avdah tikvatenu, "our hope is not yet lost", with faith in place of hope. Those two lines, and the title, are the song reaching for the oldest words it can find; the rest of the lyric is 2023 and belongs to its writers.

Why Genesis 45:3

Because it is where the sung phrase comes from. Carlebach's lyric is derived from this verse: Joseph's question הַעוֹד (ha-od) אָבִי (a-vi) חָי (chai), "is my father still alive?", becomes od avinu chai, "our father still lives", and "am yisrael chai" is set next to it. The change from "my father" to "our father" has been read two ways — as Jacob, who is also called Israel and of whom tradition says he did not die, and as God, the father of the children of Israel. Either way the grammar of the phrase is the grammar of this line: od plus a subject plus chai. Golan's song is a different composition with different words, but its title is that phrase, and this is the oldest text under it.
