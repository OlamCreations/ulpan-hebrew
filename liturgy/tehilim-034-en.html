Tehilim 34: Taste and see

תְּהִלִּים ל״ד · לְדָוִד בְּשַׁנּוֹתוֹ
HomePs 34
An alphabetical psalm with a hole in it. After the superscription, each verse opens with the next letter of the alphabet, alef through tav, except that no verse begins with vav: the he verse is followed directly by the zayin verse. Then, after the tav verse has closed the sequence, one more verse is added, and it begins with pe, a letter already used earlier. The same two features appear in Psalm 25. The content is a testimony that turns into a lesson: the first half reports a rescue, the second half teaches a class of children how to live. Verse 4 is sung when the Torah scroll is lifted and carried, verse 11 is among the verses appended to the grace after meals, and in many rites the whole psalm is read in the morning service on Shabbat.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two halves joined at verse 12. Verses 2 to 11 are a report: I sought, He answered, this poor man called, He heard, so taste for yourselves. Verses 12 to 23 are a lesson: come and listen, here is what to do with your tongue, and here is what happens to the two kinds of people. The alphabet runs underneath both halves and does not quite hold. Twenty-two verses follow the superscription, but they carry only twenty-one letters, alef through tav with no vav verse, and then a twenty-second verse that starts again with pe. The one place a vav does stand at the head of a line is inside the he verse, at וּפְנֵיהֶם (uf-nei-hem, v.6), where the second half of the couplet begins with it. Whether that is why the letter has no verse of its own, the text does not say.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The superscription and the turn of the psalm share a root. In טַעְמוֹ (ta-mo, v.1) David changes his good sense, the noun for taste and judgement, in order to look insane in front of a foreign king; in טַעֲמוּ (ta-a-mu, v.9) the same root comes back as an imperative addressed to everyone, taste. The word that named a pretended madness names the recommended way to know that God is good. A second pairing closes the poem: the verb at יֶאְשָׁמוּ (ye-sha-mu, v.22), they will bear guilt, is the last word of the tav verse, and the added pe verse repeats it at יֶאְשְׁמוּ (ye-shmu, v.23) with a negative in front. Note also that the psalm has both an ayin verse at עֵינֵי (ei-nei, v.16) and a pe verse at פְּנֵי (pnei, v.17) in their normal order, so the extra pe at פֹּדֶה (po-de, v.23) is a genuine addition rather than a displaced letter.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verses 13 and 14 became the classic text on guarding speech. The Talmud tells of a peddler who went through the towns crying that he had the elixir of life for sale; when a crowd gathered he opened a book of Psalms and read out the question, who is the man who wants life, together with its answer, guard your tongue from evil. Those same words of עָנִי (a-ni, v.7) onward, the poor man who called and was heard, were read as the reason David could be quoted at all: the teacher of the second half is the rescued man of the first, and he is selling what he was given. The verse about bones at עַצְמוֹתָיו (atz-mo-tav, v.21), that not one is broken, was heard against the rule that no bone of the passover offering may be broken, so that the protected righteous stands where the offering stands.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical readings work on the order of the two verbs in verse 9. Sight keeps its object at a distance and leaves the one seeing unchanged; taste takes the thing in and changes the one tasting, and it cannot be done on someone else's report. Putting taste first makes knowledge of God a matter of ingestion rather than observation, and the invitation is then unanswerable by argument. The missing letter is read in the same key. An alphabet is the full set of what can be said, and this one is short by a letter and long by a verse, so the psalm neither closes on completeness nor claims it: what falls outside the sequence is the verse that says nobody who shelters there is condemned.
