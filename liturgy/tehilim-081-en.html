Tehilim 81: Blow the horn at the new moon

תְּהִלִּים פ״א · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 81
Seventeen verses that open as a summons to a festival and then, halfway through verse 6, hand the microphone to God. The instruments are named one by one, the horn is blown, and a voice that was never introduced says I removed his shoulder from the burden and does not stop until the psalm ends. This is the psalm of the day for Thursday, and verses 4 and 5 are why it is tied to Rosh Hashanah; in many rites they stand among the shofar verses of the additional service. Watch the pronouns: from verse 9 the whole people is addressed as a single you.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two movements with no join. Verses 2 to the first half of 6 are addressed to a congregation: sing, shout, lift the melody, strike the timbrel, blow the horn, because the day is a statute. From the second half of verse 6 to the end, God speaks and nobody answers. That speech runs through Egypt, the thunder at Sinai and the waters of Meribah, states the first commandment, reports that it was refused, and then spends four verses on the good a listening people would have had. Nothing returns to the festival that was being called for at the start, and the final word is singular, addressed to one person.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The dated word at בַּכֵּסֶה (ba-ke-se, v.4) is the crux of the psalm. Read against the Aramaic and Akkadian terms for the full moon, it puts the horn at mid month, where the pilgrimage festivals fall. Read from the Hebrew root for covering, it puts the horn on the day the moon is hidden, the first of the month, and the Talmud in tractate Rosh Hashanah takes it that way and names the festival as Rosh Hashanah. Both readings are live and the choice decides which day the psalm belongs to. Two more knots sit in verse 6: the name at בִּיהוֹסֵף (bi-ho-sef, v.6) is spelled with an extra letter, Yehosef rather than Yosef, and the clause running from שְׂפַת (sfat, v.6) to אֶשְׁמָע (esh-ma, v.6) has no stated subject, so it is read either as Israel hearing an unknown language in slavery or as the psalmist hearing the voice that then takes over the poem. At עִתָּם (i-tam, v.16) the possessive has no unambiguous owner, and their time forever is read both as the enemies' sentence lasting and as Israel's own time lasting.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The extra letter at בִּיהוֹסֵף (bi-ho-sef, v.6) was not treated as a spelling variant. The Talmud in tractate Sotah reads it as a letter of the divine name added to Joseph when he was taught the languages of the nations, which ties it directly to the unknown speech four words later in the same verse. The invitation at הַרְחֶב (har-chev, v.11) and פִּיךָ (pi-cha, v.11), open your mouth wide and I will fill it, was read off food and onto study and prayer: what a mouth is opened for is words, and the measure of the asking sets the measure of the answer. On that reading verse 11 is not a change of subject after the commandment of verse 10 but its reward, and the wheat and the honey of the last verse are the same gift seen from the other end.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm turns on a voice that arrives unannounced. Someone reports hearing a speech he did not know, and from that half verse onward the poem is that speech. Mystical readings take verse 6 as a description of how revelation is received rather than as a note about foreign languages: what is unfamiliar is not the vocabulary but the direction it comes from, and the hearer cannot place it because it does not originate on his side. The horn of verse 4 belongs in the same reading. It is the one instrument with no words and no melody, sounded on a day when the moon shows nothing, and it stands at the head of a psalm that ends with a mouth held open and filled.
