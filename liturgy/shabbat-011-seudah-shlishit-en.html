Seudah Shlishit: The Third Meal

סְעוּדָה שְׁלִישִׁית · Saturday twilight · between Mincha and Maariv · Psalm 23
HomeSeudah Shlishit
Seudah Shlishit, the third Shabbat meal, eaten in the late afternoon, between Mincha and Maariv, as Shabbat begins to fade. Less formal than the Friday-night and Saturday-day meals: typically lighter food (tuna, salad, bread), often eaten at the synagogue itself or at a friend's house. No Kiddush is said (it was already said at the previous meals). The texture is meditative, twilight on Shabbat, last hours, slow conversation. The universal song at Seudah Shlishit is Psalm 23 (Mizmor LeDavid), "the LORD is my shepherd". The psalm is sung over and over in many communities, with different melodies, sometimes for 30-40 minutes. Also common: Yedid Nefesh, Y'did Nefesh, mystical Hasidic niggunim (wordless melodies), and personal divrei Torah. The mood: bittersweet, Shabbat is ending; absorb it.

Read Psalm 23 on Sefaria for the surrounding biblical/liturgical context.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Saturday afternoon, between Mincha and Maariv, ~90 minutes before Havdalah. Some families do it at home; many synagogues hold a communal Seudah Shlishit in the social hall after Mincha.
  • What to eat, minimum: bread, with the standard meal blessings (HaMotzi + Birkat HaMazon). Lighter food than the previous two meals: salads, tuna, fish, olives, cheese, fruit. The third meal is symbolic of "three meals on Shabbat", a Torah-mandated number, so its presence matters more than its size.
  • Why three meals on Shabbat, the rabbis derive this from a triple repetition of the word hayom ("today") in Exodus 16:25 (the manna passage about Shabbat). Each "today" obligates one Shabbat meal. Two are easy (Friday night, Saturday day); the third requires the deliberate gathering at twilight.
  • The Psalm 23 tradition, sung at every Seudah Shlishit, often the only text. Many communities sing each verse multiple times, with different melodies, fading into wordless niggunim. The mood of Psalm 23, the Shepherd guiding through the valley, fits the mood of twilight on Shabbat, when the day is ending and the worshiper trusts what comes next.
  • Vocabulary, Psalm 23:
    • ro'i (רֹעִי), "my shepherd". Ro'eh (shepherd) + 1cs possessive. Same root as re'a (friend, companion), the shepherd is the close companion.
    • echsar (אֶחְסָר), "I will lack". Qal imperfect 1cs of ח.ס.ר (to lack). Same root as chaser (lacking, missing).
    • n'ot deshe (נְאוֹת דֶּשֶׁא), "pastures of tender grass". Naot = pastures/abodes; deshe = young/new grass. The most peaceful possible setting.
    • mei m'nuchot (מֵי מְנוּחוֹת), "waters of rest". Stillness. M'nuchot is plural of m'nuchah (rest), same root as menuchah in Baruch El Elyon.
    • tzalmavet (צַלְמָוֶת), "deathshade". A compound word: tzel (shadow) + mavet (death). Means literally "the shadow of death" or, in context, deep darkness/extreme danger. King James English settled on "the valley of the shadow of death".
    • shevet u-mish'anet (שֵׁבֶט וּמִשְׁעַנְתֶּךָ), "rod and staff". A shepherd's two tools: the rod (for fending off predators) and the staff (for guiding the flock). Same hand, two functions.
  • The Hasidic dimension, Hasidic communities (Chabad, Belz, Ger, etc.) particularly love Seudah Shlishit. The Rebbe's table at Seudah Shlishit (called the tisch) became a high spiritual moment, with the Rebbe teaching, storytelling, and singing wordless niggunim into the deepening twilight. Many recorded Hasidic niggunim originated at Seudah Shlishit tables.

♦ About this prayer

The quietest, sweetest, most reluctant meal of the Jewish week. The light is gone. Shabbat is ending. Psalm 23 is sung again and again, melting into wordless melody. The hardest meal to leave.

The text: Psalm 23 attributed to King David

Six verses. One of the shortest and most universally beloved psalms. Traditionally attributed to King David (~10th c. BCE), though scholarly consensus places its final form somewhat later. The psalm is recited at funerals, hospital bedsides, before journeys, and every Saturday afternoon at Seudah Shlishit. It is one of the most-quoted biblical texts in all of Western literature, with translations and adaptations in every European language.

The psalm's emotional power comes from a single grammatical move: the speaker shifts from third-person address ("He leads me", "He restores my soul") to second-person intimacy ("You are with me", "Your rod and Your staff") at the moment of greatest danger, when walking through the valley of the shadow of death. The shepherd image becomes a present, addressed companion at exactly the point when the speaker is most afraid. This is the psalm's pivot, and it's why so many people memorize it for their hardest moments.

Why Seudah Shlishit feels different

By Saturday afternoon, the Shabbat-soul is full. The first meal was excitement; the second was ease; the third is reluctance. Everyone at the table is starting to think about the work-week ahead: emails, the school run, the deadline on Tuesday. Seudah Shlishit is the meal that refuses to let go. The lights are still off (no electricity used on Shabbat); the conversation gets softer; the singing gets slower; sometimes there are long silences. Then someone starts Psalm 23 again, and the table joins in.

In observant homes, Seudah Shlishit is often the moment when Shabbat is most felt as a real thing, the moment of holding-on. The Talmud (Shabbat 118a) says that whoever fulfills the obligation of three Shabbat meals will be saved from the suffering preceding the Messiah's arrival, the punishment of Gehenna, and the war of Gog and Magog. The third meal is, in some traditions, the most spiritually significant of the three.

How to participate

1. If you're invited to a Seudah Shlishit, accept. It's the easiest Shabbat meal to attend, informal dress, low expectations on small talk, no pressure to perform anything.

2. Bring bread or a fruit/cheese plate. The standard contribution.

3. Learn the first verse of Psalm 23 in Hebrew (Mizmor leDavid, Adonai ro'i lo echsar), that's enough to join in. The melody is simple, often a slow 6/8 in a minor key.

4. Sit quietly when there are silences. The silence is part of the meal.

5. Stay until Maariv. The meal ends when the evening service begins, the moment Shabbat itself is technically over.
