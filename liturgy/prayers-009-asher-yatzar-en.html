Asher Yatzar: Who Formed the Body

אֲשֶׁר יָצַר · after every bathroom visit · the medical blessing · Talmud Berachot 60b
HomeAsher Yatzar
The blessing said after using the bathroom, thanking God for the proper functioning of the body. One of the most ancient blessings in Jewish liturgy (Talmudic, Berachot 60b) and one of the most loved by physicians: it acknowledges the medical miracle of a healthy body, orifices that open and close correctly, organs that function in their precise alignment. Said after every bathroom visit (after washing the hands first, no formal blessing on the water, see Netilat Yadayim). Many hospitals in Israel display this blessing in restrooms; it has become a beloved text in the secular medical world too, as a moment-by-moment reminder that the smooth operation of the human body is not guaranteed. The closing line, "rofei chol basar u'mafli la'asot" ("who heals all flesh and acts wondrously"), is one of the most quoted phrases in Jewish thought about medicine and embodiment.

Read Talmud Berachot 60b on Sefaria for the surrounding biblical/liturgical context.

♦ Lesson

  • When, every time after using the bathroom. After washing hands (no blessing on the water), then say Asher Yatzar, ideally before drying the hands or stepping out of the bathroom area. Said in addition to (not instead of) the standalone Netilat Yadayim done on waking.
  • How, say standing or seated, no special posture required. The full text takes ~30 seconds. Many people keep a small printed copy on the bathroom wall.
  • The doubled words, nekavim nekavim, chalulim chalulim ("orifices orifices, cavities cavities"). The doubling is deliberate. The rabbis explain it as praising the body's multiplicity: many openings of many kinds (mouth, nose, ears, eyes, lower openings) and many hollow organs (lungs, heart, stomach, intestines, bladder), each one is a separate engineering miracle. The repetition is a list-marker, like saying "so many, so many".
  • Why this blessing exists at all, most blessings thank God for active gifts (food, wine, rain). Asher Yatzar uniquely thanks God for a negative, for the fact that things didn't go wrong over the past few minutes. Anyone who has ever had a medical issue with the digestive or urinary system knows what is being thanked for here. The blessing is a moment of explicit health-awareness three to ten times a day.
  • Vocabulary:
    • yatzar (יָצַר), "formed / shaped / molded". Used in Genesis 2:7 for God's forming of Adam from dust. Same root as yetzira (formation) and yetzer (impulse / inclination).
    • chochmah (חָכְמָה), "wisdom". The pre-Socratic Hebrew word for skilled craft; same root as chacham (wise). "God formed the human with wisdom" = with the skilled craft of a master engineer.
    • nekev (נֶקֶב), "opening / orifice". Plural nekavim. Same root as nekeva (female), a euphemistic biblical reference.
    • chalul (חָלוּל), "hollow". Qal passive participle of ח.ל.ל (to be empty/hollow). Same root as cholin ("ordinary", non-sacred, that which is empty of holiness) and the place name Cholon.
    • rofei chol basar (רוֹפֵא כָּל בָּשָׂר), "Healer of all flesh". Rofei is the qal participle of ר.פ.א (to heal). Same root as refuah (healing) and refu'ah shleimah ("complete healing", the standard Jewish phrase for wishing someone well).
    • mafli la'asot (מַפְלִיא לַעֲשׂוֹת), "acts wondrously". Mafli is hiphil of פ.ל.א (to be wonderful), same root as peleh (miracle/wonder) and niflaot (wonders).

♦ About this prayer

The Jewish medical blessing. Recited after every bathroom visit. The 2,000-year-old text that hospitals frame on restroom walls because nothing better has been written about the gift of a working body.

Talmudic origin: Berachot 60b

The blessing first appears in the Talmud, tractate Berachot 60b, in the form roughly identical to what is said today. The Talmud also gives the rationale: "For Rabbi Abahu said: One should not say a blessing over something that has not yet occurred, but one should say a blessing over something that has just occurred. So after a bathroom visit, one says Asher Yatzar." The blessing is for what just didn't go wrong.

The medical reading

Modern physicians, especially gastroenterologists and urologists, have come to love Asher Yatzar as a precise description of human physiology: the body is described as having nekavim (openings) and chalulim (hollow organs/cavities). Both kinds of structures are essential. If any of the openings (sphincters, valves) opens or closes wrongly, exactly the failure mode of incontinence, hernias, fistulas, intestinal obstruction, the body cannot function. "It would be impossible to exist and stand before You."

The closing line, "rofei chol basar u'mafli la'asot" ("Healer of all flesh, acting wondrously"), has become a phrase Jewish doctors invoke before surgery, before consultations, in medical research. It frames medicine as participation in God's healing work; it frames the body's daily functions as an ongoing miracle.

The theology of embodiment

Judaism, unlike some other religious traditions, does not regard the body as a lower or shameful counterpart to the soul. The body is the soul's partner, both created with chochmah (skilled wisdom), both essential to a person's standing before God. Asher Yatzar is one of the clearest liturgical articulations of this: the body's plumbing matters theologically. Forgive the body's needs; honor them; thank God for their proper functioning.

This is why some traditional Jewish texts treat the bathroom itself as a sacred space requiring care (modesty, certain blessings before/after) rather than a shameful one. The blessing of the body is just as important as the blessing of the soul.
