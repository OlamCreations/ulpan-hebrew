Tehilim 28: To You, O LORD, I call

תְּהִלִּים כ״ח · לְדָוִד אֵלֶיךָ
HomePs 28
Nine verses that break in half at verse 6 and never look back. The first five are a plea from someone whose fear is not defeat but silence, and they end by asking that the wicked be handed exactly what they built. Then, with no transition and no account of a rescue, the verbs move to the perfect and the psalm is thanksgiving: He has heard. The word to follow is the one for pleas in verse 2, which comes back whole in verse 6. It is the only phrase repeated across the break, and it is the psalm's own signal that the second half is an answer to the first.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A plea in verses 1 to 5 and a thanksgiving in verses 6 to 9, joined with no seam. The plea has three moves: do not be silent, do not sweep me up with the wicked, give them what they built. The thanksgiving has two: He heard me, and then, abruptly, save the whole people. What joins them is a word, not a logic. תַּחֲנוּנַי (ta-cha-nu-nai, v.2) in the plea comes back as תַּחֲנוּנָי (ta-cha-nu-nai, v.6) in the thanksgiving, the same noun for pleas, and the voice that asked to be heard is now said to have been heard. Nothing in the psalm describes a rescue. The change of tense is the entire event.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The sharpest move here is a repeated phrase. Verse 4 asks that the wicked be paid according to כְּמַעֲשֵׂה (kma-a-se, v.4), the work of their hands, and verse 5 convicts them for paying no attention to מַעֲשֵׂה (ma-a-se, v.5), the work of His hands. One noun, two owners, and the whole justification of the sentence sits in that repetition. Verse 8 carries a textual crux: the Masoretic text has לָמוֹ (la-mo, v.8), for them, with no clear antecedent in the line, and translations split between for them and for His people, a difference of a single letter. The choice decides whether the psalm turns communal at verse 8 or only at verse 9. And בוֹר (vor, v.1) is not a specific hole in the ground, it is the standard idiom for the grave across the Psalter.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 4 is the verse readers flinch at, and the traditional handling of it is not to soften the request but to bound it. Nothing is asked for beyond measure: their deed, their doings, the work of their own hands, what they have earned, four statements of the same limit and none of them asking for more than was made. Verse 9 does something else with the discomfort. A psalm that spent five verses on one man's fear ends with four imperatives for a whole people, and the last of them, וּרְעֵם (ur-em, v.9), is the shepherd's verb, the one Psalm 23 opens with. The prayer that enemies be repaid and the prayer that a nation be carried are put in the same mouth, and the psalm makes no attempt to reconcile them.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two nouns of verse 7 are read as a pair rather than a doubling: strength is what holds a person up from inside, the shield is what stands between him and what is outside, and both are named before the heart is allowed to move from trust to exultation. דְּבִיר (dvir, v.2) in verse 2 is the innermost room of the sanctuary, the point with nothing further behind it, and hands lifted toward it are the standard image for raising what is below back toward its root. The closing verb belongs to the same reading: what can no longer walk is not abandoned but carried, and the psalm ends on the word for without limit in time.
