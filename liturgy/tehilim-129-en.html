Tehilim 129: Much have they harassed me from my youth

תְּהִלִּים קכ״ט · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 129
A Song of Ascents in two halves that do not match. Verses 1 to 4 are a survivor's report, spoken by Israel in the first person singular: they harassed me, they plowed my back, and they did not win. Verses 5 to 8 turn to those who hate Zion and ask that they come to nothing, and the way the psalm does that is by describing a harvest that never happens. Watch the last verse. The greeting that reapers and passers by exchanged in a field is quoted in full, inside a sentence saying that nobody will say it, so the psalm ends on a blessing it has just withheld. Grammatically the first half drills the plural perfect, they did, and the second half the jussive, let them be.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two halves, joined at verse 4. The first half is testimony: a long persecution stated twice, an image of it as plowing, and then one flat sentence about God that ends it. The second half is a request, and it is made entirely out of farming that fails. Grass on a roof cannot root, so it dies standing; there is nothing for a reaper to grip; and because there is no harvest, the greeting people call across a field never gets called. The chain is tight. Verse 4 cuts the ropes of the plow, verses 6 to 8 remove the crop, so the enemy loses first the tool and then the yield. Note that צְרָרוּנִי (tze-ra-ru-ni, v.1) and the same word in verse 2 mean harassed or hemmed in rather than defeated: the psalm never claims the persecution failed to hurt.

רמז · Remez: allusion

רַבַּת (ra-bat, v.1) is a feminine form used adverbially, much or greatly, and it is the first word of the quotation both times. The verb after it is from the root tzade resh resh, whose other outputs are narrowness and distress, so being harassed and being squeezed into a tight place are one word in Hebrew. לְמַעֲנִיתָם (le-ma-a-ni-tam, v.3) is a rare noun for the strip a plow cuts in one pass, which is why the boast about length works. שֶׁקַּדְמַת (she-kad-mat, v.6) and שָׁלַף (sha-laf, v.6) are the hard place: the first is a construct form meaning before or prior to, the second a perfect verb usually taken as drawn out or plucked, and the resulting clause has been read as withers before it is pulled and as withers before it shoots up. Both are current and the difference matters, because one has the grass die after it grows and the other has it die first. וְחִצְנוֹ (ve-chitz-no, v.7) is the fold of a garment used as a carrying pouch.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

From my youth has been read as national rather than personal: not one man's childhood but Israel's, which puts Egypt at the start of the list. The instruction in יֹאמַר (yo-mar, v.1) נָא (na, v.1) יִשְׂרָאֵל (yis-ra-el, v.1), let Israel now say, uses the same three words that open the responses in Psalm 118, so the line is built for a crowd to repeat and the psalm expects to be said aloud by more than one person. The greeting of verse 8 is not invented for the curse; the book of Ruth records exactly that exchange between a landowner and his reapers in a barley field, which is how we know it was ordinary speech. Withholding it is therefore not a curse in words but the removal of an occasion for words, and that is the psalm's harshest move: the wicked are not cursed, they are left out of a conversation.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read for depth rather than for events, the psalm is about rooting. The speaker has been cut at from youth and is still standing, which means the roots go under the damage; the enemy is asked to become the opposite, growth with no soil under it, tall for a morning and dry by noon. The ropes of עֲבוֹת (a-vot, v.4) are read as the bindings that hold a person to a work not their own, and the one act attributed to God in the whole psalm is cutting them, not lifting the plowman off the field. Verse 8 then closes the circle: a blessing said in the name has to pass through someone, and where there is no harvest there is no one to pass it through. The last two words of the psalm are the name, and they are on the wrong side of a negative, which is a way of ending on it without granting it.
