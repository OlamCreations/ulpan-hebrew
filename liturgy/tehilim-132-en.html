Tehilim 132: LORD, remember David

תְּהִלִּים קל״ב · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 132
Eighteen verses, the longest of the fifteen Songs of Ascents, and the only one built on two oaths that answer each other. In the first half a man swears he will not sleep until he has found God a place; in the second half God swears back, to David, about a throne. Verses 8 to 10 turn up almost word for word at the close of Solomon's dedication prayer in 2 Chronicles 6, which is the clearest sign of where the psalm belonged. Watch the verb for clothing: it is asked for in verse 9, granted in a changed form in verse 16, and turned against the enemies in verse 18.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two halves, hinged on one verb. Verses 1 to 10 are the human side: a vow not to rest until God has a place, a search at בְאֶפְרָתָה (ve-ef-ra-ta, v.6) and יָעַר (ya-ar, v.6), a procession, and a plea that leans on a dead king's name. Verses 11 to 18 are the reply, spoken by God, and each request of the first half comes back altered. The prayer at קוּמָה (ku-ma, v.8) takes the words said whenever the ark set out in the wilderness and swaps the destination from marching to rest. Verses 8 to 10 appear again at the close of Solomon's dedication prayer in 2 Chronicles 6, which places this psalm in the world of the Temple rather than the road.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three grammatical details carry the argument. First, the oaths at אִם (im, v.3) and אִם (im, v.12) open with the word if and never finish the sentence: a biblical oath cuts itself off before the self curse, so if I enter my house means I certainly will not. Second, the verb for clothing runs at יִלְבְּשׁוּ (yil-be-shu, v.9), אַלְבִּישׁ (al-bish, v.16) and אַלְבִּישׁ (al-bish, v.18), and the garment differs each time, so the request is granted, upgraded, then turned on the enemies. Third, בָּרֵךְ (ba-rech, v.15) sets an infinitive beside its own finite verb, the ordinary Hebrew way of saying that the thing will happen and there is no arguing about it. The two place names of verse 6 are disputed: Ephrathah is usually read as the region of Bethlehem and the fields of יָעַר (ya-ar, v.6) as Kiriath-Jearim, where the ark stood before David moved it, but that identification is an inference from the names and is not stated in the psalm.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The tension readers keep returning to is between נִשְׁבַּע (nish-ba, v.11), an oath God says He will not turn back from, and the condition in verse 12 that hangs the throne on whether David's sons keep the covenant. One line of reading takes the promise as unconditional and the condition as governing only unbroken rule; another takes the whole grant as conditional and the exile as its proof. Both readings are old, and this psalm does not decide between them. Verse 10 is the classic text for arguing from the merit of someone already dead: the petitioner offers nothing of his own and asks only that an old commitment be honoured. Chronicles puts that same sentence in Solomon's mouth at the moment the Temple is dedicated, which is a use of the psalm rather than a comment on it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The controlling word is rest. It is the destination in קוּמָה (ku-ma, v.8) and God's own claim in verse 14, and in the mystical reading rest is not the absence of work but the state in which presence settles into a place and stops moving, which is why the same root names what the Sabbath is for. Zion is chosen by desire at אִוָּהּ (i-vah, v.13) and desired again at אִוִּתִיהָ (i-vi-ti-a, v.14), so the longing runs in both directions and the dwelling is something wanted before it is something built. The crown of the last verse blossoms rather than shines, which keeps the king inside the same vocabulary of growth already used for the horn and the lamp.
