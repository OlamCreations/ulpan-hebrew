Tehilim 113: He raises the poor from the dust

תְּהִלִּים קי״ג · הַלְלוּ יָהּ
HomePs 113
Nine verses, and the opening of Hallel, the run of Psalms 113 to 118 said on festivals, on Rosh Chodesh and at the seder, where this psalm and 114 come before the meal. It turns on one move: God is higher than everything there is, and what He does with that height is look downward. Two things for a learner. The imperative praise appears three times in verse 1 alone, and the psalm is closed at both ends by Hallelujah, so the first two words and the last two are the same. And five words here end in an extra yod that carries no meaning: the one who sits high, the one who bends low, the one who raises, the one who seats, the one who settles. It is an old poetic ending that Hebrew later dropped, so it looks like an error and is not.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements of three verses. Verses 1 to 3 are about the name, praised by servants, blessed through all time, praised across the whole width of the day. Verses 4 to 6 are about height, and they turn in the middle: הַמַּגְבִּיהִי (ha-mag-bi-hi, v.5) and הַמַּשְׁפִּילִי (ha-mash-pi-li, v.6) are two participles in the same unusual form, one going up and one going down, and that pair is the psalm's whole argument. Verses 7 to 9 are what the downward look reaches, three people at the bottom, the poor, the needy and the barren woman, each of them moved. Note that the verb for sitting runs through all three movements, from the one seated on high in verse 5 to the man seated with nobles in verse 8 to the woman settled in a house in verse 9.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Five words in this psalm carry a yod on the end that adds no meaning and changes no grammar: הַמַּגְבִּיהִי (ha-mag-bi-hi, v.5), הַמַּשְׁפִּילִי (ha-mash-pi-li, v.6), מְקִימִי (me-ki-mi, v.7), לְהוֹשִׁיבִי (le-ho-shi-vi, v.8) and מוֹשִׁיבִי (mo-shi-vi, v.9). It is an old ending, found mostly in poetry, and its density here is unusual even for the Psalms. The second thing to notice is that verses 7 and 8 are close to a line in Hannah's song at 1 Samuel 2:8, which also raises the poor from the dust and the needy from the ash heap and seats them with nobles, and verse 9 is close to 1 Samuel 2:5 on the barren woman. Whether the psalm quotes the song, the song quotes the psalm, or both draw on a formula older than either is disputed and the evidence does not settle it. One last thing: עֲקֶרֶת (a-ke-ret, v.9) literally means the barren one of the house, and modern Hebrew took the same two words and made them mean the woman who runs the household, which reverses the sense the psalm gives them.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The last line of this psalm is the subject of a recorded dispute. Mishnah Pesachim 10:6 asks how far Hallel is recited before the meal at the seder: the school of Shammai says as far as אֵם (em, v.9) to שְׂמֵחָה (sme-cha, v.9), a glad mother of children, which is the end of Psalm 113, and the school of Hillel says as far as the end of Psalm 114. The practice follows Hillel, so the psalm is not read alone there. The reason the question is live is that the barren woman made a mother is the standard image of a reversal that nobody could have arranged, which makes it either a fitting place to stop before the meal or an incomplete thought that needs the exodus of Psalm 114 to finish it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two participles at הַמַּגְבִּיהִי (ha-mag-bi-hi, v.5) and הַמַּשְׁפִּילִי (ha-mash-pi-li, v.6) are the verse the mystical reading works on. A God defined by being high who is then defined by bending down is a contradiction only if height means distance, and the kabbalistic answer is contraction: the infinite makes room by withdrawing, and what looks like descent is the condition for anything small existing at all. On that reading verses 7 to 9 are not three examples of charity but three demonstrations of the same mechanism, since dust, ash heap and barrenness are all names for a place with nothing in it. The barren woman at עֲקֶרֶת (a-ke-ret, v.9) is read in this tradition as the exiled community, on the strength of Isaiah 54:1, where Zion is addressed as the barren one and told to sing.
