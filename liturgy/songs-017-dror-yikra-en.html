Dror Yikra: He will proclaim freedom

Shabbat day, at the second meal · דְּרוֹר יִקְרָא
HomeDror Yikra
The oldest song on this site: Dunash ben Labrat wrote it in tenth-century Spain, and he is the man who introduced Arabic quantitative metre into Hebrew poetry, which is why it scans the way it does. Six stanzas of four short lines, each stanza rhyming on one sound throughout, and the first letters spell the poet's name. It is sung at Shabbat lunch, quickly, and most tables get through three stanzas before the food arrives.

Four lines, one rhyme: בַּת (bat), בָבַת (va-vat), יֻשְׁבַּת (yush-bat), שַׁבָּת (sha-bat) all end in -at. That is the whole formal principle of the poem, and it is why it is so easy to learn — if a line does not rhyme, you have the wrong line. בָבַת (va-vat) is literally "like the pupil of the eye", the same image as in Deuteronomy 32.

The last stanza is the only one addressed to the singer rather than to God, and it is the only one that mentions the Sabbath again after the first. The poem opens with rest commanded and closes with rest kept.

♫ Singing notes

  • Sung at Shabbat lunch, briskly, and often not to the end. Three stanzas is a normal amount.
  • Each stanza has its own rhyme sound and it never varies within the stanza. If you are learning by ear, that rhyme is your handrail.
  • The lines are short — three or four words — so the melody turns around quickly. Do not stretch them.
  • There are several tunes in circulation, including one that borrows a well-known secular melody. Whichever the table starts, join it.

♦ About this song

Dunash ben Labrat

Tenth century, North Africa and then Córdoba, and the poet who brought Arabic quantitative metre into Hebrew verse. That importation is the reason Hebrew poetry sounds the way it does for the next five hundred years, and this table song is one of the few pieces of his that everyone still sings.

The acrostic

The first letters of the stanzas spell the poet's name. The sixth stanza is generally taken to be a later addition, since it falls outside that acrostic — the same situation as the last stanza of Maoz Tzur, and it is printed here for the same reason.

Almost every line is a quotation

Deuteronomy, Isaiah twice, Psalms. None of them is marked. A piyyut of this period assumes a listener who has the Tanakh by heart, and it builds a new poem out of half-lines the audience will recognise as they pass.

Text and licence

The Hebrew here is Daat Siddur Ashkenaz, public domain, via Sefaria and pinned in this repository. That edition marks four alternative readings and closes with an attribution to the poet; the scaffold removed all five rather than printing them as lines to be sung, and lists each one when it runs.
