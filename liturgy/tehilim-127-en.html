Tehilim 127: Unless the LORD builds the house

תְּהִלִּים קכ״ז · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 127
Five verses, the only Song of Ascents attached to Solomon, and the only one in the group that argues instead of praising. The first two verses are about effort that comes to nothing, and they turn on one word repeated three times that means emptiness or futility. The last three are about children, which is what the psalm offers as the thing that does get built. Whether the two halves began as one poem is disputed; they are joined by the ordinary Hebrew word for a house, which means a building in verse 1 and a household everywhere after. Verse 2 ends with one of the more contested lines in the book: it can be read as He gives His beloved sleep, or as He gives to His beloved while he sleeps, and the difference decides what kind of consolation the psalm is offering.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two halves that share a subject. Verses 1 and 2 say that building, guarding and overwork produce nothing on their own; verses 3 to 5 say what does arrive, and it arrives as a gift rather than as a wage earned. The bridge between them is the word house, which in verse 1 is a structure with builders and in verse 3 becomes a family, since Hebrew uses the same noun for both. Read that way the psalm is not two poems awkwardly joined but one argument that changes what it means by the word it started with. The word for emptiness appears three times, twice in verse 1 and once at the head of verse 2, and each time it is placed first in its clause, before the work it is about to cancel.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 2 is the crux. The last word, שֵׁנָא (she-na, v.2), is spelled with a final aleph, a spelling found nowhere else for the ordinary noun meaning sleep, and how it functions in the sentence is disputed: as a direct object it gives He grants sleep to His beloved, and read adverbially it gives He grants to His beloved while asleep. The two readings turn the verse into different arguments, one about rest and one about effortless provision, and both are current. There is also a name buried in the line. The word לִידִידוֹ (li-di-do, v.2), His beloved, is built on the same root as Yedidyah, the name Nathan gives Solomon in 2 Samuel 12:25, and the heading of this psalm names Solomon. In verse 5 note that the particle אֶת (et, v.5) looks identical to the object marker at אֶת (et, v.5) but is the preposition meaning with, which is why the sons speak with enemies rather than speaking them.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Solomon built the house in Jerusalem, and the tradition has generally read the house of verse 1 as that one, which makes the opening line something close to self criticism attributed to the builder himself. The word carries a second sense in the same breath, since a house in Hebrew is also a dynasty, and both of Solomon's houses were promised to him rather than achieved. Verse 2 has had a long life outside the psalm as an argument against anxious labour, and the emphasis it usually receives falls on the futility rather than on the sleep. The psalm never says that work is worthless, only that work without the condition in verse 1 is empty, and the difference between those two claims is where most of the commentary lives.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading works from the two verbs of verse 1, building and keeping, and treats them as the two operations that any vessel requires: it has to be made, and then it has to be held. Human effort can perform both and neither will hold without what is above them, which is why the verse gives the same verdict twice. Sleep in verse 2 is then not an image of laziness but of the one state in which a person stops adding to himself, and the reading takes it as the condition for receiving anything at all. Children in verse 3 are the plainest case of that, since nothing in the process is earned by the one who receives them, and the arrows of verse 4 make the point again from the other side: the value of a thing you are given is that it leaves.
