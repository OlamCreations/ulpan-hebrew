Tehilim 83: God, keep no silence

תְּהִלִּים פ״ג · שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 83
Nineteen verses, the last of the psalms attributed to Asaph, and one of the most direct: a nation names the alliance forming against it, then asks God to break that alliance the way he broke earlier ones. It is widely said in Israel in times of war, and it is the psalm people reach for when the threat is collective rather than personal. For a learner it is a compact drill in two things: the plural enemy verbs of verses 3 to 6, which all arrive in the same shape, and the comparative prefix that runs through verses 10 to 15, one small letter doing all the work of the word like. Notice that the psalm never asks for victory. It asks that a name be known.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A national emergency prayer in three movements. Verses 2 to 6 report a coalition forming and quote its intention. Verses 7 to 9 name ten peoples, arranged roughly by distance, with the empire last. Verses 10 to 19 ask God to act, first by precedent, using two victories from the book of Judges, then by image, using wind and fire, and finally by stating the purpose. No battle is described and no army of Israel appears. The speaker has no force to report on, which is why the whole poem is addressed upward.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm is built on hiddenness. In verse 4 the plan the enemies lay, סוֹד (sod, v.4), and the people they lay it against, צְפוּנֶיךָ (tze-fu-nei-cha, v.4), come from adjacent ideas of what is kept out of sight: a closed council on one side, a stored treasure on the other. The same root behind צְפוּנֶיךָ (tze-fu-nei-cha, v.4) is the ordinary verb for putting something away for safekeeping. Then there is the frame: what the enemies want abolished in verse 5 is שֵׁם (shem, v.5), a name, and what the psalm wants established in verse 19 is שִׁמְךָ (shim-cha, v.19), a name. כַגַּלְגַּל (cha-gal-gal, v.14) is a real crux, since the noun means a wheel and is used elsewhere of something that rolls, so translators give whirling dust, tumbleweed, or a wheel driven along, and the choice is not settled.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Two questions have kept this psalm in discussion. The first is historical: no recorded war matches all ten nations at once, and proposals range from the coalition of Moab, Ammon and Seir faced by Jehoshaphat in Chronicles, where the levite who answers is from the sons of Asaph, to much later settings. None of the proposals is established, and the psalm gives no date. The second is moral: וִיבַקְשׁוּ (vi-vak-shu, v.17), let them seek Your name, reads as a request for the enemies to turn, and the verse straight after asks for them to perish. Some readers take verse 17 as the real goal and verse 18 as what happens to those who refuse it. Others take the pair as unresolved on purpose. The disagreement is genuine and this page does not settle it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes the silence of verse 2 seriously as a condition rather than a mood. דֳּמִי (do-mi, v.2) asks God not to be still, and the request is for a concealed presence to become an active one, which is the same movement the psalm asks for in its own people, described in verse 4 as the hidden ones. Enemy and covenant appear as mirrored structures: they cut a covenant in verse 6 using the exact vocabulary of the covenant at Sinai, so what is fought is a counterfeit of the real bond rather than a mere threat. סֶלָה (se-la, v.9), the pause marker at the end of the list, falls exactly between the naming and the asking, and readers of this tradition treat that gap as the point where a person stops counting the enemy and starts addressing God.
