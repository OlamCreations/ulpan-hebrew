Tehilim 55: Cast your burden on the LORD

תְּהִלִּים נ״ה · לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת
HomePs 55
Twenty-four verses of a man being destroyed by someone he used to pray beside. The first movement is panic and a wish for wings; the second is a city patrolled by its own corruption; the hinge is verse 14, where the enemy is finally identified as a friend; the last third is a decision to trust taken without any report that the situation improved. In the cycle that reads the whole book across thirty days it falls on the tenth. Grammatically it is a good place to meet the cohortative, the I would form ending in -a: verses 7 to 9 give you I would fly, I would settle, I would hurry.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements. Verses 2 to 9 are distress and end in a fantasy of escape: wings, wilderness, a shelter from a storm. Verses 10 to 16 turn to the city and find the danger inside the walls, where it has a face and a name. Verses 17 to 24 are a decision to trust, taken with no report that anything outside the speaker got better. The hinge is וְאַתָּה (ve-a-ta, v.14), an emphatic pronoun put first in its clause: up to that point the trouble is an enemy, from there it is a friend. Note that the escape is never taken. The dove flight is stated as a wish and then dropped without comment.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The consonants q-r-b run through the psalm in two senses the ear cannot separate. Inside: my heart writhes inside me in verse 5, inside her in verses 11 and 12, inside them in verse 16. Combat: וּקְרָב (uk-rav, v.22), his heart was war, and מִקְּרׇב (mik-rov, v.19), from the battle against me. A psalm about what is inside a person is built on the word for close fighting. Two readings stay unsettled and this page does not settle them. The first word of verse 16, יַשִּׁי (ya-shi, v.16), is written one way and read another in the Masoretic tradition, and translations divide between a verb, may He incite death against them, and a reading built on a noun for desolation; it is disputed. The last word of verse 19, עִמָּדִי (i-ma-di, v.19), plainly means with me, which is why some render for they were many against me and others for many were on my side, and the verse can be read either as rescue from a crowd or as rescue by one.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Traditional commentary identifies the friend of verses 14 and 15 with Ahithophel, David's counsellor, who went over to Absalom's revolt in 2 Samuel 15 to 17 and whose advice the book says was treated like an oracle. On that reading the shared walk to the house of God is not a general memory of friendship but a working relationship inside the court. Verse 18 names three times of prayer, evening and morning and noon, in that order, and the same three times appear in Daniel 6:11; the pair is a fixture in discussions of why the day carries three fixed prayers. One structural detail is worth holding onto: הַשְׁלֵךְ (hash-lech, v.23) is the only imperative in twenty-four verses that is addressed to a person rather than to God.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm uses two divine names and does not scatter them at random. The name read as God carries the appeals of verses 2 and 17 and the judgment of verse 24; the four letter name appears at the moment of rescue in verse 17 and in the instruction of verse 23. In the standard reading of the two names, one is strict measure and the other is mercy, so the psalm crosses from the first to the second while the circumstances stay exactly as they were. The wilderness of verse 8 is read in this key not as flight from the world but as the place where nothing stands between a person and the source, which is why the wish is answered in a form the speaker did not ask for: the shelter arrives, the wings do not.
