Tehilim 68: Let God arise

תְּהִלִּים ס״ח · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד
HomePs 68
Thirty-six verses of victory procession, and the hardest Hebrew in the book. Something like a dozen words here occur nowhere else in the Bible, several more occur only in forms nobody can parse with confidence, and the ancient translators were already guessing at them. Read it as a march: God goes out from Sinai, crosses the wilderness, scatters kings, chooses a mountain over the taller ones and sits down on it, and the last third is the parade arriving at the sanctuary with singers in front, drummers in the middle and foreign envoys bringing tribute at the back. Many rites say it on Shavuot, on the strength of verse 19. The one thing to notice while learning it: how often these notes have to say that a word is uncertain. That is not caution on this page, it is the condition of the text.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A march in three movements. Verses 2 to 11 are the departure: God rises, the enemy dissolves, the column goes out through the wilderness and the mountain shakes. Verses 12 to 24 are the campaign: kings run, spoil is divided, a mountain is chosen and occupied, captives and tribute are taken. Verses 25 to 36 are the arrival, and the pace changes completely, because now the poem is describing something being watched rather than something being done. Singers in front, instrumentalists behind, drummers in the middle, four tribes named, then envoys from Egypt and Cush, then the kingdoms of the earth told to sing the same two verbs Israel was told to sing in verse 5. Read the geography and the argument is plain: the movement is from Sinai to Jerusalem, from a mountain in the desert to a smaller hill that was chosen over taller ones, and the whole psalm exists to say that the God of the wilderness now has an address.

רמז · Remez: allusion

This is the hardest text in the Psalter and the honest report is a list of unsolved words. The largest is בָּעֲרָבוֹת (ba-a-ra-vot, v.5) in the call to prepare a road for the rider. The Masoretic pointing gives the plural of the noun for desert plain, and the sense is then the One who rides through the steppes, which fits a psalm about crossing a wilderness. A second reading takes it as a cognate of the word for cloud found in Ugaritic poetry, where the storm god carries the standing title rider of the clouds, and reads the One who rides the clouds. Verse 34 gives the same participle with an unambiguous sky word, which is evidence for the cloud reading; verse 8 gives the desert with an unambiguous word, which is evidence for the other. Neither is decisive. Around it sit words that occur once and are guessed from context: בַּכּוֹשָׁרוֹת (ba-ko-sha-rot, v.7), תְּרַצְּדוּן (te-ratz-dun, v.17), שִׁנְאָן (shin-an, v.18), רִגְמָתָם (rig-ma-tam, v.28), חַשְׁמַנִּים (chash-ma-nim, v.32). And זֶה (ze, v.9) with סִינַי (si-nai, v.9) may be a demonstrative, this Sinai, or an archaic relative particle of the kind Arabic still has, giving the One of Sinai as a title. When a translation of this psalm reads smoothly, that fluency was added by the translator.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 19 carries most of the later reading. You went up to the height, you took captive, you took gifts among men: the Talmud reads the ascent as Moses going up Sinai and the gifts as the Torah, brought down to human beings over the objection of the angels, and this is why the psalm is attached to Shavuot in the rites that assign it there. Note what the reading does with the grammar. The Hebrew says gifts were received, and the midrashic reading turns the receiving into a delivery, on the argument that what Moses took he took in order to give. Verse 28 has its own history: the Talmud brings it into the dispute about which tribe went into the Reed Sea first, dividing רֹדֵם (ro-dem, v.28) into two words so that Benjamin the youngest is not ruling them but going down to the sea. Verse 6, father of orphans and judge of widows, is the verse most often quoted from this psalm outside of any of that, and it is quoted for itself.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The Talmud's list of the seven heavens takes the name of the highest, Aravot, from בָּעֲרָבוֹת (ba-a-ra-vot, v.5) of this psalm, reading the rider not as crossing a desert but as enthroned above the outermost firmament, where justice and righteousness and the treasuries of life are stored. That is the mystical resolution of the philological problem in the remez: the obscure word is not decided between desert and cloud, it is read as the proper name of a place that is neither. On that reading the psalm's whole geography becomes vertical. The going out of verse 8 and the going up of verse 19 are one movement seen from two ends, and the mountain God desired for a seat in verse 17 is the point where the highest heaven touches a specific hill that is not the tallest one available. The chosen thing being smaller than its rivals is the recurring shape here, and the kabbalistic tradition treats it as the rule rather than the exception: a vessel receives in proportion to how little it insists on its own height.
