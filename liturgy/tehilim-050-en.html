Tehilim 50: God has spoken and summoned the earth

תְּהִלִּים נ׳ · מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף
HomePs 50
Twenty-three verses of courtroom. Asaph opens with God summoning heaven and earth as witnesses, then puts two parties on the stand: the people who bring offerings and think the account is settled, and the man who recites the covenant out loud while breaking it. Nothing here abolishes the sacrifices, and verse 8 says so in as many words. What is refused is the accounting behind them, the idea that an animal is a transfer of goods to a God who needs goods. In the printed monthly cycle of Tehilim the psalm falls on the ninth day. For a learner it is a long drill in the second person possessive suffix: your sacrifices, your burnt offerings, your house, your pens, your vows, your mouth, your tongue, your brother.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A lawsuit, start to finish. Verse 1 is the summons, verse 4 calls heaven and earth as witnesses in the idiom Deuteronomy uses when the covenant is enforced, and verses 5 and 6 seat the court. Then two speeches: verses 7 to 15 to the people who bring offerings, verses 16 to 21 to the man who recites the covenant while breaking it. Verse 8 carries the whole reading, because it states outright that the sacrifices are not the charge. What is on trial is a theory about them, the one in which the animal is a delivery of goods to a God who needs goods. Verse 23 says what the offering was actually for.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The frame is a pun on silence. Verse 3 asks that God not stay silent, יֶחֱרַשׁ (ye-che-rash, v.3), and verse 21 discloses that He had been silent all along, וְהֶחֱרַשְׁתִּי (ve-he-che-rash-ti, v.21), the same root, and that the accused took the silence for consent. A second pun carries the resolution: תּוֹדָה (to-da, v.14) and תּוֹדָה (to-da, v.23) are the word todah, which means both thanks and the thanksgiving offering, so verse 14 can be heard as bring a thanksgiving sacrifice or as let thanks be your sacrifice, and the psalm seems to want both readings at once. וְזִיז (ve-ziz, v.11) is a rare noun, occurring only here and in one other psalm, usually taken as the teeming or moving life of the open country. וַתִּרֶץ (va-ti-retz, v.18) is unsettled too: the consonants can be traced to the root meaning to run or to the root meaning to be pleased with, so the man either ran along with the thief or simply approved of him.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 2 became a statement about Jerusalem in rabbinic hands: the Talmud has ten measures of beauty coming down to the world with nine of them taken by Jerusalem, and מִכְלַל (mich-lal, v.2) is the phrase it hangs on. Verse 5 gave the tradition חֲסִידָי (cha-si-dai, v.5), the faithful gathered in as those who cut a covenant over a sacrifice, and the line has been read both as the people who keep the covenant and as the people who give themselves up for it, since the sacrifice in the phrase has no stated object. On verse 14 there is a widely quoted midrashic saying that in the world to come every offering will cease except the thanksgiving offering. That reading turns the psalm from a critique of the sacrificial system into its distillation: what survives is the part that was never a payment.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Verse 1 stacks three divine names in three consecutive words, beginning at אֵל (el, v.1), and the mystical reading treats such a stack as the measures under which God is acting, strict judgement and compassion appearing together the moment a court convenes. The rest follows from the psalm's own argument. If nothing can be given to God that was not already His, then the only thing a person genuinely owns and can therefore offer is his intention, and וְשָׂם (ve-sam, v.23), the deliberate setting of a way, is the form that intention takes over time. The fire before Him and the storm around Him in verse 3 are the same apparatus as the mountain in Exodus, and hearing the two scenes together makes this trial a renewal of the covenant rather than its cancellation.
