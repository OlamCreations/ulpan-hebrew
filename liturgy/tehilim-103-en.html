Tehilim 103: Bless the LORD, O my soul

תְּהִלִּים ק״ג · לְדָוִד בָּרְכִי
HomePs 103
Twenty two verses of blessing with no request anywhere in them, which is rare in this book. David addresses his own soul in the first line and again in the last, and between the two the frame keeps widening: from one body to Israel under Moses, to everyone who fears Him, to the angels, to the hosts, to every work of God in every place. The grammar to watch is the run of participles in verses 3 to 5, the One forgiving, the One healing, the One redeeming, the One crowning, the One satisfying, each carrying a second person feminine suffix in an old form, because the soul being addressed is feminine in Hebrew.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Five participles in verses 3 to 5 describe what God does for one person. Verse 6 turns the same activity outward and verse 7 gives it a history. Verse 8 quotes Exodus. Verses 9 to 14 argue from that quotation in four negations and three comparisons of scale. Verses 15 and 16 state the human problem in the plainest terms the psalm uses, verse 17 answers it, and verses 20 to 22 hand the blessing over to the angels, the hosts and everything that exists, before returning it to the soul that began. There is no request anywhere in the psalm, which is unusual enough to be the first thing to notice.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The possessive endings in verses 3 to 5, on עֲוֺנֵכִי (a-o-ne-chi, v.3) and on the words around it, are second person feminine singular in a long form that the Bible uses only in a handful of places and that is generally treated as archaic. The addressee is the soul, which is feminine in Hebrew, so a reader who expects masculine endings will misparse the whole passage. עֶדְיֵךְ (ed-yech, v.5) is the crux: the consonants give a word for adornment, the ancient versions do not agree on what it means here, and printed translations range from your old age to your desire to your prime. It is disputed, and no reading has settled it. רַחוּם (ra-chum, v.8) and what follows is a direct quotation of Exodus 34:6.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

One reading takes מִזְרָח (miz-rach, v.12) and its pair to be chosen deliberately rather than for variety: north and south meet at a pole, east and west never meet at all, so the removal of transgression is stated as unbounded and not merely large. יִצְרֵנוּ (yitz-re-nu, v.14) is the noun yetzer, from the verb a potter's work uses, and it already carries a moral sense in Genesis 6:5. Rabbinic Hebrew then makes yetzer the standard word for the inclination that pulls a person toward wrongdoing, and verse 14 read through that later usage becomes God taking into account the very thing He installed. That last step belongs to the drash and not to the plain sense of the verse.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Verse 4 crowns the redeemed by way of הַמְעַטְּרֵכִי (ham-at-re-chi, v.4), a participle built on the word for a crown, and it pairs kindness with compassion. In the kabbalistic vocabulary those two are names of divine attributes rather than descriptions of mood, so the verse reads as an account of what descends onto a person rather than of how God feels about them. The same logic runs through the frame: בָּרְכִי (bar-chi, v.1) and בָּרְכִי (bar-chi, v.22) are the same word in the same form, and the movement out to the angels and back in to one soul makes that soul the smallest and the last of the places of dominion named in verse 22.
