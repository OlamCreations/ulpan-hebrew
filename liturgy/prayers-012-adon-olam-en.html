Adon Olam: Master of the Universe

אֲדוֹן עוֹלָם · closes Shacharit + Maariv · medieval hymn (ibn Gabirol?)
HomeAdon Olam
The closing hymn of Shacharit (and many other services), a 10-stanza meditation on God's transcendence over time. The opening four stanzas describe God before creation, during creation, after creation, and beyond the end of creation: the same one God across every state of the universe. The hymn is medieval (~11th c. Spain, often attributed to Rabbi Shlomo ibn Gabirol, the great Andalusi philosopher-poet). Its meter is unusual for Hebrew liturgy, strict syllabic, like Arabic poetry, which makes it ideal for melody. Hundreds of melodies exist; the most famous Ashkenazi tune is bouncing, the Sephardic Andalusian setting is slow and ornate. The closing stanza ("In His hand I deposit my spirit") is sometimes said as a bedtime prayer.

♦ Lesson

  • When, closing Shacharit (morning service), closing Maariv (evening service) on Shabbat and festivals. Some say the last stanza alone before sleep.
  • Structure, 10 stanzas total, but the first 4 are the most learned. Each stanza is a 4-line couplet with strict syllabic meter (typically 4+4+4+4 syllables).
  • The four phases of God:
    • St. 1: before creation, God exists alone, no creatures.
    • St. 2: at creation, the name "King" becomes meaningful when there is something to be King over.
    • St. 3: after the world ends, God remains, alone again.
    • St. 4: beyond comparison, "He was, He is, He will be in glory".
  • Vocabulary: olam = world/universe/eternity (one word for both space and time). be-terem = "before". az/azai = "then". nora = "awesome / inspiring awe" (niphal of י.ר.א, same root as yirah).
  • Grammar pattern, niphal passive/middle: nivra (was created), na'asah (was made), nikra (was called), all niphal perfects of weak roots. Notice the na-/ni- prefix and the -a ending; this is the niphal stem signature.

♦ About this prayer

A 10-stanza medieval hymn that sounds like it was written for melody, and was. Sung weekly in every Jewish community, in hundreds of tunes, with one of the warmest places in Jewish life.

Authorship: disputed, almost certainly Spanish

The hymn is traditionally attributed to Rabbi Shlomo ibn Gabirol (Avicebron, ~1021-1058 CE), the Cordoba-born poet-philosopher who wrote both massive philosophical works (Mekor Chayyim / Fons Vitae) and exquisitely metrical Hebrew poetry. The attribution is uncertain, some scholars argue for Rav Hai Gaon (Baghdad, ~10th c.) or for an anonymous Sephardic poet of the same era. What's certain: the meter and theology fit the Andalusi-Hebrew tradition.

Why the metrical novelty matters

Classical Hebrew poetry (the Psalms) uses parallel meaning-units, not syllable counts. Hebrew liturgy until ~10th c. mostly continued that tradition. The Andalusi poets, Shmuel HaNagid, ibn Gabirol, Yehuda Halevi, invented Hebrew metrical poetry under Arabic influence: strict syllable counts, classical Arabic meters (the qasida patterns) transposed into Hebrew. Adon Olam is one of the small handful of metrical-Hebrew liturgical poems that survived in mainstream prayer. Its predictable meter is what makes the hundreds of melodies possible.

The last stanza: bedtime use

The final stanza ends: "In His hand I deposit my spirit when I sleep and when I wake; and with my spirit, my body too, the LORD is with me, I will not fear." This stanza is recited as the closing line of the Bedtime Shema. Some communities say only this single stanza before sleep; others say the whole hymn.

It is one of the few prayers traditionally sung even by people who don't pray regularly, its melodies are loved enough that secular Israelis at funerals, weddings, and bar mitzvahs will know at least one tune.
