Tehilim 105: He remembers His covenant forever

תְּהִלִּים ק״ה · הוֹדוּ לַיהֹוָה
HomePs 105
Forty five verses of history turned into a hymn. It opens with ten plural imperatives, give thanks, call, make known, sing, seek, remember, then drops the commands and runs the rest of the story on the narrative tense, one vav-consecutive verb after another. That is the most common verb form in biblical prose, and this is a good place to learn it because the sentences are short and the vocabulary repeats. Verses 1 to 15 appear almost word for word in 1 Chronicles 16, in the account of the ark coming up to Jerusalem, and the Hodu passage of the morning service is taken from that version. Notice what the psalm leaves out: from Abraham to the land, not one Israelite sin. Psalm 106, its neighbour, tells the same history with almost nothing else.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Seven blocks. A call to praise (1 to 6), the covenant sworn to the fathers (7 to 11), the protection of a handful of migrants (12 to 15), Joseph (16 to 22), Egypt and the plagues (23 to 36), the exit and the desert (37 to 41), and the reason for all of it (42 to 45). One verb frames the narrative: זָכַר (za-char, v.8) where it begins and זָכַר (za-char, v.42) where it ends, both meaning He remembered. The plague list is a selection, not a chronicle. Eight appear rather than ten, with no cattle plague and no boils, and darkness is moved from ninth place to first. The psalm also runs out of story before Sinai: the sequence goes promise, danger, deliverance, land, and never passes through a mountain.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The hardest line in the psalm is וְלֹא (ve-lo, v.28) מָרוּ (ma-ru, v.28), and they did not rebel against His word. Who is not rebelling? Taken of the Egyptians it is odd, since resistance is the whole plot. Taken of Moses and Aaron it follows verse 27 well. The Greek version lacks the negative and reads that they rebelled, and modern translations still divide over which text to follow, so this is genuinely disputed rather than settled. Two smaller oddities reward attention. שָׁאַל (sha-al, v.40) is a singular verb, he asked, where the sense wants the people, and the old versions quietly make it plural. And חֶבֶל (che-vel, v.11), rendered portion, is literally a measured rope: the surveyor's line became the ordinary word for an allotment of land.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 15 is the psalm's only divine speech apart from the land grant, and it gives the patriarchs two titles, anointed and prophets. The second has a hook in the text: in Genesis 20 God tells Abimelech that Abraham is a prophet who will pray for him. Read this way the psalm functions as a charter. The claim to the land rests on an oath sworn to three individuals who owned nothing at the time, which is why the fathers are named and the generation at Sinai is not mentioned at all. The Joseph section carries the same logic on a small scale: the man thrown into a pit ends up holding the granary that keeps his family alive, so the descent gets read as provision arranged in advance rather than as an accident survived.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Remembering, in this psalm, is not a state of mind but an act that changes the world, and the two occurrences of the verb hold the whole narrative between them like brackets. The mystical reading takes the small numbers seriously: מְתֵי (me-tei, v.12) מִסְפָּר (mis-par, v.12), men of number, few enough to be counted, is the narrow point through which the entire promise has to pass, and the smallness is a condition of the thing rather than an obstacle to it. Joseph goes down first so that what comes after can be gathered up, the pattern later kabbalah generalises as descent for the sake of ascent. The psalm then ends on הַלְלוּ (hal-lu, v.45) יָהּ (yah, v.45), the shortest praise built on the shortest form of the Name, as though four hundred years of history compressed back down into two syllables.
