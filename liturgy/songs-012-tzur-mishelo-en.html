Tzur Mishelo: The Rock whose food we have eaten

Friday night, after the meal and before Birkat HaMazon · צוּר מִשֶּׁלּוֹ
HomeTzur Mishelo
The zemer that ends the Friday night meal, sung immediately before the grace after meals — and in fact it is a compressed version of that grace, saying the same four things in four stanzas. Everyone at the table knows the refrain, which returns after each stanza and is the same nine words every time. That is not laziness: the refrain is what lets a table of people who do not read Hebrew sing along for five minutes, and it is why this is the first zemer most guests learn.

Two lines that will come back four times. מִשֶּׁלּוֹ (mi-she-lo) literally means "of that which is His": what was eaten was never the host's. And וְהוֹתַרְנוּ (ve-ho-tar-nu) — leaving food over — is not politeness but a quotation, from the story of Elisha and the twenty loaves in Kings, where the miracle is the leftovers.

The last stanza brings the poem back to the table it is being sung at: כּוֹס (kos), יַיִן (ya-yin), מָלֵא (ma-le) — a full cup of wine, which is the cup of the grace after meals, sitting in front of whoever is leading. The poem ends by pointing at the object in the room.

♫ Singing notes

  • The refrain is everything. If you learn nine words of this song, learn צוּר (tzur) through יְהֹוָה (a-do-nay), and you can join any Friday night table in the country.
  • The verses are usually taken by one or two people who know them; the whole table comes in on the refrain. Leave a clear breath before it so people know it is coming.
  • Around 92 BPM, and it does not accelerate. This one is sung sitting down, at the end of a long meal.
  • Three chords carry it. Am for the openings, Dm and E for the turns, back to Am for every refrain.

♦ About this song

It is the grace after meals in miniature

The four stanzas match the four blessings of Birkat HaMazon in order: the one who feeds, the land, Jerusalem, and the closing blessing. It is sung immediately before the real thing, which makes it a rehearsal and a summary at once.

Anonymous

The poem carries no acrostic naming its author and no attribution in the manuscripts, which is unusual for a zemer of this period — Dror Yikra names Dunash, Yah Ribon names Najara. This one arrived without a signature and is generally dated to the fourteenth century or thereabouts.

Quotation as method

Almost every line closes on a phrase from the Tanakh, and the phrases are not marked as quotations. Elisha's leftovers, Hannah's prayer, Psalm 117, Lamentations. A listener who knows the sources hears a second poem underneath the first, and a listener who does not still gets a complete one.
