Tehilim 139: You have searched me and known me

תְּהִלִּים קל״ט · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד
HomePs 139
Twenty four verses in four blocks of six: you know me, I cannot get away from you, you built me, and then a sudden turn on the wicked before the last two verses hand the searching back to God as a request. The Hebrew is late and carries Aramaic forms, and several of its words occur nowhere else in the Bible. It is not part of the fixed daily order of prayer; it is one of the psalms people take up on their own. Watch the first verb and the last: the psalm ends by asking for exactly what it opens by stating as a fact.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements of six verses. Knowledge in 1 to 6, presence in 7 to 12, formation in 13 to 18, and then 19 to 22 turn on the wicked before 23 and 24 close the frame. The frame is exact: חֲקַרְתַּנִי (cha-kar-ta-ni, v.1) states that God has searched, חׇקְרֵנִי (chok-re-ni, v.23) asks Him to search, and the two are the same verb with the same object suffix. Everything in between is the reason the request costs something. The speaker has spent eighteen verses saying that nothing about him is hidden, so asking to be examined is not a formality, and the four verses of hatred immediately before it are what he is handing over.

רמז · Remez: allusion

מִלָּה (mi-la, v.4) is an Aramaic word for speech and one of the signs that this psalm is late. גׇּלְמִי (gol-mi, v.16) occurs nowhere else in the Bible: it names something rolled up and not yet shaped, and later Hebrew keeps it for anything unformed. רֻקַּמְתִּי (ru-kam-ti, v.15) is the verb of the embroiderer, the same root used for the woven screens of the tabernacle, so the body is made the way the sanctuary was made. עָרֶיךָ (a-rei-cha, v.20) is the standing crux: as it is pointed it reads Your cities, and translators arrive at Your adversaries either by attaching it to a root meaning hostility or by reading a similar looking word, and neither solution is agreed. In the same verse the pair of verbs, lifting up and in vain, is the exact wording of the commandment against taking the name in vain.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 16 is where the psalm was most worked over: a book in which days are written before the days exist runs straight into the question of what is decreed and what is chosen, and the ambiguity of the last clause, whether it says none of them existed yet or one of them did not, has kept the argument open. The turn at verse 19 is generally treated as a problem rather than a model, and the common reading takes 23 and 24 as the answer to it, since the man who has just declared a complete hatred immediately asks to have his heart examined, which is a confession that he does not trust his own verdict. עֹצֶב (o-tzev, v.24) is read both as pain, a way that hurts, and as idolatry, because the same consonants spell the word for an idol.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two directions of verse 8 are the standard vertical pair, and the point of the answer is that the descent is not somewhere outside. In the kabbalistic reading the light and the dark of verse 12 are not opposites reconciled but one thing seen from two distances, which is why the verse ends by equating them instead of saying the light wins. The book of סִפְרְךָ (sif-re-cha, v.16) is read as the record that precedes the world it describes, and being formed in the depths of the earth in verse 15 is taken as the descent a soul makes into a body, so the middle movement is the story of coming down and the last verse asks for the way back, the way with no end.
