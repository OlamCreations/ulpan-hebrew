Tehilim 32: Happy is the one whose transgression is lifted away

תְּהִלִּים ל״ב · לְדָוִד מַשְׂכִּיל
HomePs 32
Eleven verses on what silence costs and what saying it out loud is worth. The psalm names wrongdoing with three different nouns and uses all three twice, which makes it the cleanest place to learn the vocabulary of the confession: transgression as revolt, sin as missing the mark, iniquity as something bent. Watch the tenses turn at verse five, where the whole poem changes from past-tense illness to present-tense instruction, and watch who is speaking at verse eight, because it is not obvious.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements. Verses 1 and 2 state the conclusion as a blessing on someone else; verses 3 to 5 are the case history, silence and drought broken by one sentence spoken out loud; verses 6 to 9 turn the case into instruction; verses 10 and 11 close with a proverb and a call to the community. The physical detail is the argument: nothing is said about guilt as a feeling, only about bones wearing out and sap drying up, so that speaking becomes a medical event as much as a moral one. Note that the covering he refuses in כִסִּיתִי (chi-si-ti, v.5) is exactly what he is granted in כְּסוּי (ksui, v.1).

רמז · Remez: allusion

The three nouns of this psalm, transgression, sin and iniquity, are the same three that stand together in the thirteen attributes of Exodus 34, where God is described as lifting away iniquity, transgression and sin. The verb there is the verb here: נְשׂוּי (ne-sui, v.1) and נָשָׂאתָ (na-sa-ta, v.5) are both built on the root for carrying and lifting off. That is why the traditional English forgiven flattens something, since the Hebrew is about a load being taken off a back. Two other words are genuinely hard. לְשַׁדִּי (le-sha-di, v.4), rendered here as my moisture, occurs only a couple of times in the Bible and translations range from sap to vigour to the marrow. עֶדְיוֹ (edi-o, v.9) is obscure enough that many versions do not translate it at all but rebuild the clause around it.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The unanswered question is who speaks in verse 8. If the voice is God's, the psalm is a dialogue and the instruction is the reward for confessing. If it is the psalmist's, then a man who has been forgiven immediately becomes a teacher, and the title מַשְׂכִּיל (mas-kil, v.1) names what he is now qualified to produce. Both readings are old, and the Hebrew supports either, since nothing marks a change of speaker. The wordplay favours the second: אַשְׂכִּילְךָ (as-kil-cha, v.8), I will instruct you, is built on the same root as the maskil of the heading. Read that way, the whole psalm is the lesson the heading promises, and the man himself is the syllabus.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading works from the encircling. In תְּסוֹבְבֵנִי (te-sov-ve-ni, v.7) deliverance surrounds the speaker and in יְסוֹבְבֶנּוּ (ye-sov-ve-nu, v.10) kindness surrounds whoever trusts, both from the same root, so protection in this psalm is never a shield placed between two parties but an environment closed around one. Kabbalistic vocabulary later distinguished light that fills a vessel from light that surrounds one too large to enter, and this psalm is a natural home for that distinction: the man who could not speak was full of nothing but his own roaring, and what replaces it is not a filling but a circle. On this reading the deceit of verse 2 is the only real obstacle, because a spirit divided against itself has no outside for anything to encircle.
