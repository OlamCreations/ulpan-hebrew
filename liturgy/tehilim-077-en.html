Tehilim 77: In the day of my distress I sought the Lord

תְּהִלִּים ע״ז · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 77
Twenty one verses in two unequal halves. The first ten are a sleepless man asking six questions about whether God has changed; the last ten are the sea splitting, told as if the speaker were watching it happen. Verse 11 is the hinge and the hardest line in the psalm to construe. For a learner it is a good place to meet the cohortative, the first person form ending in a long vowel that means let me remember or I would remember: it appears four times, and each time the same act of memory does something different to the speaker. The heading assigns the psalm to Jeduthun, named in Chronicles as one of the levitical singers, and to Asaph.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Verses 2 to 11 are one man at night; verses 12 to 21 are the sea at the exodus. The join is אֶזְכּוֹר (ez-kor, v.12), a plain future verb, I will call to mind. Read the two halves as one argument and the logic is this: the speaker has asked whether God has changed, and instead of an answer he sets a piece of the past in front of himself and looks at it until the question loses its force. Notice what the second half does not do. It never mentions the speaker's distress, never says he slept, never claims the questions were wrong. The last verse ends on Moses and Aaron and simply stops.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 11 is the crux and it is genuinely disputed. חַלּוֹתִי (cha-lo-ti, v.11) is read by some as a noun from the root for sickness, giving this is my sickness, and by others as a form of the verb for entreating, giving this is my plea. שְׁנוֹת (shnot, v.11) is equally open: the same consonants give either the plural construct of year, hence the years of the right hand, or an infinitive of the verb to change, hence the changing of the right hand. Translations pick one silently; the Hebrew does not. A second ambiguity sits in וְהַאֲזִין (ve-ha-a-zin, v.2), whose form can be read as a past tense, He gave ear, or as an infinitive continuing the cry. The storm of verses 18 to 20 shares vocabulary and images with the theophany in Habakkuk chapter 3, closely enough that the two texts are usually discussed together.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

One reading takes the psalm as a description of how prayer actually works when nothing is going well. The four cohortatives are the mechanism: the first two, in verses 4 and 7, are memory turned on the self and they make things worse; the second two, in verses 12 and 13, are memory turned on an event outside the self and they hold. On this reading the cure is not new information but a change of object. A second reading works on the last two verses. וְעִקְּבוֹתֶיךָ (ve-ik-vo-tei-cha, v.20) says the footprints were not known and בְּיַד (byad, v.21) says it happened by the hand of Moses and Aaron, so the sentence that denies any visible trace is immediately followed by the two visible men who led the march. Where a path leaves no mark, the people who walked at the front are the only evidence there was one.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In kabbalistic vocabulary the right hand is the side of chesed, expansive kindness, and the left is the side of judgment and restraint. Verse 11 as usually vocalized asks about the years of the right hand, which makes the whole complaint a complaint about kindness that has stopped flowing, and verse 9 has already named chesed explicitly. The second half then answers in the register of concealment rather than of supply: the way exists, it runs through the sea, and it leaves no footprints. Water in this reading is the covering itself, and the split at the exodus is the one moment when the covering opened and the road underneath became briefly visible to everyone standing on the shore.
