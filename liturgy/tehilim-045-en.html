Tehilim 45: My heart is stirred by a good word

תְּהִלִּים מ״ה · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 45
A wedding song for a king, and the only psalm in the book that announces itself as one. Eighteen verses in two halves: the poet addresses the groom, then turns and addresses the bride. Grammatically it is the best place in Tehilim to learn the two second-person sets side by side, because the same suffix appears in its masculine form all through verses 3 to 10 and in its feminine form all through verses 11 to 16. It has no fixed slot in the daily service and most people meet it in the monthly cycle of reading the whole book. Watch the poet keep intruding: he opens by talking about his own tongue and closes by promising to keep the name alive.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A court poet writes a wedding song and performs it at the ceremony. Verses 2 to 10 face the groom: he is handsome, well spoken, armed, enthroned, perfumed, attended. Verses 11 to 16 face the bride: she is told to leave her family behind, and then she is described arriving. Verses 17 and 18 turn back to the king with a promise about sons and a promise about the song. The two halves are marked grammatically, not by a heading, since every possessive suffix switches gender at verse 11. The king is not named and the bride is not named, which is why the psalm has been reused for so many weddings and so many kings.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three cruxes worth knowing. כִּסְאֲךָ (kis-a-cha, v.7) followed by אֱלֹהִים (e-lo-him, v.7) can be read as an address to the king with the divine word as a vocative, as a statement that the throne belongs to God, or as a description meaning a divine throne, and translations still divide over which; the dispute is live and old. מִנִּי (mi-ni, v.9) is a single short word read either as a preposition meaning from, or as a clipped form of the noun for strings, giving stringed instruments, and there is no way to settle it from the consonants alone. שֵׁגַל (she-gal, v.10) is a rare word for a royal consort that turns up only a couple of times elsewhere in the Bible, once in Nehemiah of a Persian queen, which is part of why some place this psalm late. Also notice וְשִׁכְחִי (ve-shich-chi, v.11): the command to forget people and father's house uses the vocabulary of Abraham's call in Genesis, applied to a bride.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The tradition of reading this king as the messianic king is very old and remains the standard homiletical use of the psalm. Once the king is read that way, the bride is read as Israel or as the assembly, and וְשִׁכְחִי (ve-shich-chi, v.11), forget your people, stops being advice to a foreign princess and becomes a demand to leave an old allegiance. A second line of reading takes מָהִיר (ma-hir, v.2), the skilled scribe, as the psalm's own self-portrait and treats the whole poem as a statement about what praise is for. Both readings are widespread; neither is the plain sense, and the plain sense of a royal wedding has never been abandoned.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read mystically the psalm is about union and the preparation each side does for it. The groom's half is all outward equipment: sword, throne, oil, spice, the gold of a foreign trade route. The bride's half is all subtraction: forget, incline, leave. In the kabbalistic vocabulary the right hand of verse 10 and the sceptre of verse 7 belong to the giving side and the standing, listening, receiving figure to the other, and the wedding is the standard image for the two being brought into one. The verse the readers of that tradition dwell on is verse 14, glory within, because it locates the bride's worth in the place no procession can display.
