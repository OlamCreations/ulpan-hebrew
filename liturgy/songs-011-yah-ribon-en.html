Yah Ribon: God, Master of all worlds

Friday night, at the table · יָהּ רִבּוֹן
HomeYah Ribon
The one zemer at the Friday night table that is not in Hebrew. Israel Najara wrote it in Aramaic in sixteenth-century Safed, and its first letters spell his name. Aramaic is close enough to Hebrew that a learner can follow most of it and far enough that the differences jump out: the definite article is a final vowel instead of a prefix, the relative particle is a single syllable, and the word for "you" is not the one you have been taught. Singing it once teaches you more about the relation between the two languages than a chapter would.

Four features of Aramaic in nine words. וְעָלְמַיָּא (ve-al-ma-ya) ends in the vowel that means "the" — the article is a suffix, not a prefix. אַנְתְּ (an-te) is "you", where Hebrew says atah. מַלְכָּא (mal-ka) and מַלְכַיָּא (mal-cha-ya) are king and kings with the same final vowel doing the same work. Learn this refrain and the rest of the poem stops being foreign.

The lions are Daniel's, and the exile named in גָלוּתָא (ga-lu-ta) is the one Najara was living in — he wrote this in Safed, in an Ottoman province, four hundred years after the poem's Hebrew cousins. יַת (yat) and יַת (yat) are the Aramaic object marker, the equivalent of Hebrew et, and this is the clearest place in the poem to see the two languages side by side.

שִׁירִין (shi-rin) is songs and וְרַחֲשִׁין (ve-ra-cha-shin) is whispers, and pairing them is the poem's last move: the loud and the almost inaudible, both counted as singing. Then one place name and it stops.

♫ Singing notes

  • Slower than the other zemirot, and it stays slow. Around 84 BPM, sung sitting, usually after the faster ones have run their course.
  • The refrain is nine words and returns five times. Everyone sings it; the stanzas belong to whoever knows them.
  • The Aramaic will trip a Hebrew reader on the vowels rather than the letters. If you are learning it, learn the refrain by ear before you try to read it.
  • Many Sephardi and Mizrahi communities sing it to a maqam-based melody rather than the Ashkenazi one, and the two sound nothing alike. Both are correct.

♦ About this song

The acrostic

The first letters of the five stanzas after the refrain spell the poet's name, Israel. He signed his poems this way as a matter of course, which is how the authorship of a sixteenth-century table song is known at all.

Why Aramaic

Najara worked in Safed among kabbalists for whom Aramaic was the language of the Zohar, and writing a table song in it placed the Friday night meal in that register. It is also the language of the Kaddish, so the sound was familiar to everyone at the table even if the grammar was not.

A poem with no Sabbath in it

Nothing in Yah Ribon mentions the Sabbath. It is sung on Friday night by custom rather than by subject, which sets it apart from every other zemer on this site — those are about the day; this one is about the world.
