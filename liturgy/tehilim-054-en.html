Tehilim 54: God is my helper

תְּהִלִּים נ״ד · לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת
HomePs 54
Nine verses, two of them heading, and one of the shortest complete prayers in the book: three verses of plea, four of confidence, nothing in between. The heading pins it to a real betrayal, the men of Ziph telling Saul where David was hiding, and the psalm never names Saul again. Two things are worth watching. The first is the prefix be-, by or with: God saves by Your name, gives judgment by Your might, and puts an end to enemies by Your truth, three abstract nouns doing the work an army does elsewhere. The second is the ending -ni, me, glued to verb after verb: save me, judge me, delivered me.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two verses of heading, then two halves that mirror each other. Verses 3 to 5 ask and give the reason: strangers have risen, ruthless men want his life, and they do not keep God in view. Verses 6 to 9 answer without any new information having arrived: God is my helper, the harm will turn back, I will bring an offering and thank the name. The heading sends the reader to 1 Samuel 23, where the men of Ziph go up to Saul at Gibeah and tell him David is hiding in their hill country. Ziph is a town of Judah, so the men handing David over are of his own tribe, which makes the word strangers in verse 5 the hardest word on the page.

רמז · Remez: allusion

זָרִים (za-rim, v.5) means strangers, and that is the Masoretic reading. Some Hebrew manuscripts have instead a word meaning insolent or presumptuous, and the closely parallel opening at Psalm 86:14 has that second word where this verse has the first. Which of the two stood here originally is disputed, and the choice changes the psalm: foreigners attacking, or the arrogant of one's own people. The second crux is בְּסֹמְכֵי (be-som-chei, v.6), a plural participle with the preposition attached, literally among the upholders of my life. Hebrew also uses that preposition to say that someone is the thing itself rather than one of a group, which is why most translations read the Lord is the sustainer of my life. Both readings are defended.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Read against 1 Samuel, this is a psalm about betrayal by neighbours rather than attack by outsiders, and the word strangers becomes the reading itself: people of your own tribe who hand you over have made themselves strangers. Notice what the prayer does not do. It never asks for the informers to be punished by name, and the one aggressive line, יָשִׁיב (ya-shiv, v.7), asks only that the harm be turned back along the line it came. The vow of verse 8 is then the measure of the answer. An obligatory sacrifice would prove nothing about the person bringing it; a freewill offering is what gets given when nothing is owed.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The divine names move across the psalm. Verses 3, 4 and 5 say God, verse 6 says God and then the Lord, and only at verse 8, at the vow, does the four letter name appear. Psalm 54 sits inside the long block of psalms that prefer the name God throughout, so a single four letter name at the end carries weight it would not carry elsewhere in the book. Read mystically, the psalm walks from the name associated with strict judgment to the name associated with mercy while the circumstances stay exactly as they were, which means the only thing that moved was the one praying. That is also why the offering promised is freewill: the movement cannot be commanded.
