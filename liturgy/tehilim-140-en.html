Tehilim 140: Rescue me from an evil man

תְּהִלִּים ק״מ · לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 140
Fourteen verses against one kind of danger, and it is not an army. It is a person with a mouth: a tongue sharpened in verse 4, venom under lips, lips again in verse 10, a man of tongue in verse 12. Against that the psalm sets hands, a head, steps, ears, and in the last word a face. The first half is the best drill in the book for one piece of grammar, the object suffix -ni fixed to an imperative: rescue me, keep me, guard me, guard me again, four times before verse 6. Three words in this psalm occur nowhere else in the Bible, all of them in the lines about venom, pits and thrusts, which is why English versions of verses 4, 11 and 12 differ from each other more than anywhere else here.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements. Verses 2 to 6 ask for rescue and describe the danger, which is a mouth plus a set of traps. Verses 7 and 8 stop describing and speak to God directly, and they are the only calm lines in the psalm. Verses 9 to 12 ask for the harm to be sent back, escalating from do not let his plan work through coals, fire and pits. Verses 13 and 14 drop the speaker's own case entirely and state what God does for the poor and the needy as a class. The vocabulary tracks that shape exactly: the first half is full of things hidden, planned in the heart, held under lips, buried in the path, and the last verse is a face in the open.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three words here occur nowhere else in the Bible: the venomous creature of עַכְשׁוּב (ach-shuv, v.4), the pits of בְּמַהֲמֹרוֹת (be-ma-ha-mo-rot, v.11), and the thrusts of לְמַדְחֵפֹת (le-mad-che-fot, v.12). What each one names is disputed, which is why English versions swing between viper and spider, between watery pits and miry depths, and between thrust upon thrust and a headlong fall. A second signal of old poetry is the archaic third person plural ending on שְׂפָתֵימוֹ (sfa-tei-mo, v.4), שְׂפָתֵימוֹ (sfa-tei-mo, v.10) and יְכַסֵּימוֹ (ye-cha-sei-mo, v.10), a form that belongs to verse rather than to standard prose Hebrew. There is also a division crux at the end of verse 9: the two short words before Selah can close verse 9 or open the sentence that runs into verse 10, and translators split both ways.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The phrase man of tongue in verse 12 is the plainest statement in the book that speech belongs in the category of violence, and the psalm argues the case step by step: the tongue is given an edge in verse 4, it carries poison, and in verse 10 it is what finally comes down on the heads of the people who used it. Read that way the imprecation of verses 9 to 12 is measure rather than revenge, because nothing is asked for that the enemies did not first manufacture. The first request against them, in verse 9, is only that a scheme fail. The reversal completes in verse 12, where the men who hid the traps in verse 6 are the ones being run down.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm moves from concealment to appearance, and that movement is the reading. Everything in the first half is hidden: plans inside a heart, venom under lips, a trap covered over, a net laid beside a path where it will not be noticed. The hinge is verse 7, where the speaker says one sentence out loud, that You are my God, and after it the hiding stops working. The last verse names two things the upright receive, a name to thank and a face to sit before, which in this reading are the same presence met in two ways, one heard and one seen. That the psalm ends seated rather than rescued is deliberate: the danger is never reported as over.
