Eishet Chayil: A Woman of Valor

אֵשֶׁת חַיִל · Friday night, before the meal · Proverbs 31:10-31
HomeEishet Chayil
Proverbs 31:10-31, the closing 22-verse poem of the book of Proverbs, sung by the husband to the woman of the house on Friday night before Kiddush (or before the meal, customs vary). The poem is an acrostic: each of its 22 verses begins with a consecutive letter of the Hebrew alphabet (aleph → tav). Originally written ~5th-3rd century BCE; today many also read it as praise of the Shechinah (the feminine indwelling of the Divine) or of the Torah itself personified as a wise woman. In modern egalitarian homes, women sometimes sing it back to their husband, with the parallel verse Mizmor LeDavid sung by the husband. This page teaches the opening verse, a representative middle verse, and the climactic closing.

Read Proverbs 31:10-31 on Sefaria for the surrounding biblical/liturgical context.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Friday night after Birkat HaBanim, before sitting down to eat. Some families sing all 22 verses; others just the opening and closing. Traditional length: ~3-5 minutes if chanted slowly.
  • Who sings, traditionally the husband, addressing the wife. In contemporary egalitarian homes, often both sing together, or the wife sings to the husband (paired with Mizmor LeDavid), or the family sings to the household head of any gender.
  • The acrostic structure, 22 verses, one per letter of the Hebrew alphabet. The acrostic is a mnemonic structure (easier to memorize) and a completeness signal ("from A to Z"). The same structure is used in Psalm 119, Psalm 145 (Ashrei), and Lamentations chapters 1-4.
  • The four readings of the poem:
    • Plain (peshat), praise of a literal exemplary wife in an ancient agricultural household: she buys land, hires servants, runs a textile business, feeds the poor, runs the home.
    • Allegorical (remez), praise of the Torah personified as Wisdom, a tradition going back to Proverbs 8.
    • Mystical (sod), praise of the Shechinah, the indwelling/feminine aspect of the Divine, that descends each Friday night.
    • Historical, by some readings, an Israelite woman's hymn that survived from earlier times and was preserved in Proverbs.
  • Vocabulary:
    • eshet chayil (אֵשֶׁת חַיִל), a construct chain. Eshet is the construct form of ishah (woman/wife); chayil = strength/valor/competence. "A woman of valor" or, more accurately, "a competent / capable / strong woman".
    • peninim (פְּנִינִים), pearls or red coral. The most precious gemstone in the ancient Mediterranean before the discovery of diamonds. "Worth more than pearls" = worth more than the most valuable thing imaginable.
    • yirat Adonai (יִרְאַת יְהוָה), "awe of the LORD". The standard biblical phrase for religious reverence, paired throughout the Wisdom Literature with chochmah (wisdom).
    • tithallal (תִתְהַלָּל), hitpael of ה.ל.ל (to praise). Hitpael here = "to be praised / to make oneself worthy of praise". Same root as Hallel, tehillah, Halleluyah.

♦ About this prayer

The closing acrostic of Proverbs. Originally an ancient praise poem for a competent woman; absorbed into Friday-night liturgy in the late medieval period; now sung in millions of Jewish homes each week.

Authorship and date

The book of Proverbs traditionally is attributed to King Solomon (~10th c. BCE) but is in fact a compilation of wisdom literature edited over centuries. The closing acrostic of Proverbs 31 (verses 10-31) is generally dated to the post-exilic period (5th-3rd c. BCE) on linguistic grounds. The author is anonymous, though Proverbs 31:1 attributes the preceding section (vv. 1-9) to "King Lemuel," instructed by his mother. Some readers connect the Eshet Chayil to Lemuel's mother, who instructs him on what kind of woman to seek.

Why it became Friday-night liturgy

The custom of singing Eshet Chayil at the Friday-night table is medieval, attributed to Sephardic communities first and adopted widely after the 16th century. The rationale: Friday night is the moment when the woman of the house has prepared everything, candles lit, food cooked, table set, family gathered. Eshet Chayil is the household's public acknowledgment of all that labor.

Kabbalistically, Friday night is also when the Shechinah (the feminine indwelling presence of God) descends to dwell among the people. The same poem that praises the human woman of the house simultaneously praises the divine feminine; the two are reflections of each other.

Modern readings: feminist tension and recovery

Modern feminist scholarship has read Eshet Chayil in two opposing ways. One reading: the poem is a patriarchal projection, the wife is praised entirely in terms of how well she serves her husband and household. The other reading: the poem is one of the few biblical texts that describes a woman as an economic agent, she buys real estate (v.16), runs a business (v.18), employs servants (v.15), and engages in commerce (v.24). Far from being domesticated, she is a Bronze-Age CEO.

Many contemporary Jewish families have reframed the song: instead of (or alongside) the husband singing it to the wife, the family sings it together, or each member sings a verse, or it becomes a song of mutual recognition rather than one-way praise. Sephardic and Mizrahi traditions never quite read it as exclusively about the wife, for them it was always also about Torah, Shechinah, and the Sabbath itself.
