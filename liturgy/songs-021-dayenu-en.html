Dayenu: It would have been enough for us

Pesach seder, in the middle of the telling · כַּמָה מַעֲלוֹת
HomeDayenu
Fifteen steps out of Egypt, and after each one the table shouts the same word: it would have been enough. Enough for what, exactly, is left unsaid, and that is the argument. The song does not claim that any single step would really have sufficed — you cannot leave Egypt and stop at the edge of the sea. It claims that each one, taken on its own, would have deserved thanks. Grammatically it is the easiest long text in the Haggadah: the same two-clause frame fifteen times, and the second half of one verse becomes the first half of the next.

לַמָּקוֹם (la-ma-kom) is one of the old names for God, literally "the Place", and it is the one the Haggadah uses here. מַעֲלוֹת (ma-a-lot) means steps or degrees — the favours are being counted as a staircase, which is exactly what the next fifteen verses climb.

The last verse withdraws the whole argument, politely. Each step alone would have been enough — and He did all of them. Note the grammar shift: the fifteen verses used אִלּוּ (i-lu), "if", a hypothetical; this one uses שֶׁהוֹצִיאָנוּ (she-ho-tzi-a-nu), "that He did", a plain statement of fact. Fifteen verses of counterfactual, then one of indicative.

♫ Singing notes

  • The word דַּיֵּנוּ (da-ye-nu) is shouted, not sung, and it is shouted by everyone including the people who are not following the Hebrew. Leave a beat for it.
  • Many tables sing the word three times at the end of each verse. Some sing it once. Follow the house.
  • The first half of each verse is the second half of the one before, so a table that has lost its place can always rejoin at the next אִלּוּ (i-lu).
  • The final verse is not shouted. It is read or sung straight through, and it is noticeably longer than everything before it — do not let the room start clapping.

♦ About this song

Where it sits

In the Magid section, after the recounting of the plagues and before the explanation of the Passover offering, matzah and bitter herbs. It is the moment the seder stops being a lesson and becomes a party, which is roughly halfway through the evening.

The claim it is making

Read strictly, several steps could not have stood alone — the sea without the crossing, Sinai without the Torah. The song is not making a logical claim about sufficiency but a claim about gratitude: that each item deserves its own thanks rather than being absorbed into the total. The final verse then supplies the total anyway.

Fifteen

The number of steps matches other fifteens in the seder — the fifteen sections of the evening, the fifteen Songs of Ascents in Psalms, the fifteen steps of the Temple. Whether that is design or a pattern read in afterwards is not something this page can settle.
