Tehilim 118: Out of the narrow place I called

תְּהִלִּים קי״ח · הוֹדוּ לַיהֹוָה
HomePs 118
Twenty nine verses, the last psalm of Hallel and the loudest. It is a thanksgiving after a rescue, laid out as a procession: four calls to give thanks, a first person account of being surrounded and getting out, a request at the gates, and a crowd answering from inside. Said on the festivals, on Rosh Chodesh and on Hanukkah. In the common practice the opening four verses are call and response, with the congregation answering the refrain each time, and from verse 21 to the end each verse is said twice. The lulav is waved during verse 1 and again at verse 25. Watch two things. The short form of the divine name, Yah, stands here in six places where the long form would fit. And the pronouns move: one person is rescued, and a whole assembly walks in.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A procession, told in order. Verses 1 to 4 are the summons, a leader calling and three groups answering the same refrain. Verses 5 to 18 are the testimony: a narrow place, an answer, three tellings of being surrounded, and a chastening that stopped short of death. Verses 19 and 20 are the arrival at the gate and the reply from inside it. Verses 21 to 28 are the thanksgiving in the courtyard, with the crowd taking over at בְּעֵינֵינוּ (be-ei-nei-nu, v.23), in our eyes, and the single voice returning at אֵלִי (e-li, v.28). Verse 29 repeats verse 1 exactly, which closes the circle and hands the psalm back to the congregation. The change of number is the plot: one person is rescued in the first half, and by the second half an assembly is doing the talking.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The short name, Yah, is this psalm's signature. It stands where the long name would fit at יָּהּ (yah, v.5) and יָהּ (yah, v.5), and again at יָהּ (yah, v.14), יָהּ (yah, v.17), יָּהּ (yah, v.18) and יָהּ (yah, v.19), and it is the same form that closes the word halleluyah. Two other seams are worth seeing. עׇזִּי (o-zi, v.14) to לִישׁוּעָה (li-shu-a, v.14) is the Song at the Sea quoted word for word from Exodus 15, and the same line turns up again in Isaiah 12, so the psalm is reusing a phrase that was already old liturgy when it borrowed it. And two verses work by sound before they work by sense: הַמֵּצַר (ha-me-tzar, v.5), the narrow place, is answered by בַמֶּרְחָב (va-mer-chav, v.5), the wide place, built on opposite roots, while אֶבֶן (e-ven, v.22), a stone, and הַבּוֹנִים (ha-bo-nim, v.22), the builders, share the consonants bet and nun in that order, so the line rings as wordplay before it means anything.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 22 carries two long-standing readings and the psalm does not choose between them. One takes the rejected stone as David, passed over at home before he was anointed, which makes the psalm autobiography. The other takes it as Israel, discounted among larger nations and still standing, which makes it national. Both are old. Verse 25 has had the widest afterlife of any line here: hoshia na, save now, is the source of the word hoshana, which names both the willow of the seventh day of Sukkot and the day itself. Verse 24 travelled in the other direction, out of the procession and into ordinary speech, where it is now said about any good day at all. Verse 26 is the crowd's greeting to a pilgrim reaching the house, and it is more often met quoted elsewhere than read here in place.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two nouns at the centre of verse 5 give the mystical reading its whole vocabulary: a call out of constriction, and an answer that arrives as width. Nothing is taken away in that exchange. The space around the caller changes size. Read that way, the rest of the psalm is the same movement at larger and larger scale: from a single voice at קָרָאתִי (ka-ra-ti, v.5) to a crowd at נָגִילָה (na-gi-la, v.24), from a shut gate to an open one, from being surrounded on every side to standing at the horns of the altar with the offering roped in place. The gate of הַשַּׁעַר (ha-sha-ar, v.20) is the hinge, and the reply from inside is careful: it is not opened because the traveller earned it, it is what the gate is for. The last move of the psalm is to put the opening sentence back in the mouth of everyone now standing there.
