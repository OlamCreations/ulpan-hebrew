Tehilim 98: Sing to the LORD a new song

תְּהִלִּים צ״ח · מִזְמוֹר שִׁירוּ
HomePs 98
Nine verses in three blocks of three, and the grammar changes with each block. Verses 1 to 3 are past tense: He has done, He has made known, He has remembered. Verses 4 to 6 are plural imperatives aimed at people: shout, break out, sing, make music. Verses 7 to 9 are jussives aimed at things that cannot obey: let the sea thunder, let the rivers clap. It belongs to the run of enthronement psalms and is one of the six said at the start of Kabbalat Shabbat on Friday night. The one thing to watch is the last word of each block, because the psalm moves from a victory already won to a judgment still coming and treats both as reasons to sing.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three blocks of three verses, and the tense tells you which block you are in. Past in verses 1 to 3, imperative in 4 to 6, jussive in 7 to 9. The subject widens at the same rate: God alone acts in the first block, people are told to respond in the second, and in the third the response is handed to sea, rivers and mountains. The last verse closes the circuit by giving the reason, and the reason is not the victory of verse 1 but a judgment that has not happened yet. Note that בָא (va, v.9) is a participle, He is coming, an action already under way rather than scheduled.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The opening phrase, a new song, recurs across the psalms and the prophets, and it is never a song about the past: it marks something that has no precedent and therefore no existing setting. In הוֹשִׁיעָה (ho-shi-a, v.1) and לּוֹ (lo, v.1) the verb takes a dative of its own subject, saved for Him, an odd construction that keeps the victory from being anybody else's achievement. The strangest line is verse 5, where the word for lyre appears twice with the same prefix, at בְּכִנּוֹר (be-chi-nor, v.5) and בְּכִנּוֹר (be-chi-nor, v.5). The repetition sits in the Masoretic text and readers split over it: either an emphatic doubling or a copyist writing the phrase twice, and nothing in the text settles the question. In כָף (chaf, v.8) the noun is singular, a hand, where English needs a plural.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

This is one of the psalms that describes God becoming king, and the group it belongs to was read as being about a future enthronement rather than a past one, which is why the song is called new. The move that gets the most attention is the last: a judgment on the earth treated as good news. The reading usually offered is that judgment here means a case finally being heard, and that the parties cheering in verses 7 and 8 are the ones who have been waiting for a verdict rather than fearing one. Its place at the opening of Friday night, among the psalms said before the Sabbath is welcomed in, puts that future judgment at the start of every week's rest.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The kabbalistic reading hears the three blocks as three levels of one act sounding downward: the deed above in verses 1 to 3, its report in human voices and instruments in 4 to 6, and its arrival in the material world in 7 to 9, where water and rock resonate because they are made of the same thing. On this reading the instruments of verses 5 and 6 are not decoration, they are the point at which breath becomes audible matter, which is why the ram's horn and not the lyre is the one that stands before the King. The judgment of בְּמֵישָׁרִים (be-mei-sha-rim, v.9), translated with fairness, is a word about level and straightness, and the mystical reading takes it as the restoration of proportion rather than a sentence handed down.
