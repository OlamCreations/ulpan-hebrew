Tehilim 121: My help is from the LORD

תְּהִלִּים קכ״א · שִׁיר לַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 121
Eight verses built on one verb. The root that means to keep, guard or watch over appears six times: as a participle in verses 3, 4 and 5, then as a future in verse 7 twice and in verse 8. By the end it has been attached to a foot, to Israel, to a soul, and to going out and coming in. The psalm opens in the first person with a question and answers it in verse 2, then switches for the rest to a second person masculine singular addressee, so verses 3 to 8 are spoken to someone rather than by someone. Commonly said before setting out on a journey and at the bedside of the sick. The heading is worth a second look as well: of the fifteen Songs of Ascents this is the only one that reads a song for the ascents instead of a song of the ascents.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two voices, or one voice in two positions. Verses 1 and 2 ask and answer in the first person; from verse 3 to the end someone is being addressed as you, and nothing is asked, only promised. The promises widen in order: a foot, a whole person, a nation, a soul, and finally every departure and return without end. The psalm has often been explained as a dialogue, a traveller asking at a gate and a priest answering, which would fit a song sung on the way up. Nothing in the text labels the speakers, so that remains a proposal about performance rather than a reading of the words.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two grammatical details carry most of the argument. First, verse 3 negates with the particle used for wishes and prohibitions, אַל (al, v.3) and אַל (al, v.3), while verse 4 negates the same verb with the ordinary particle of statement, לֹא (lo, v.4) and וְלֹא (ve-lo, v.4). So verse 3 says may He not and verse 4 answers He does not, which is exactly the shape of a question being settled. Second, מֵאַיִן (me-a-yin, v.1) can be read as a question, from where, or as a relative, from the place where, and the two readings put the help in different places: either the mountains raise the question and verse 2 answers it, or verse 1 already points at the answer. The dispute is old and the accents do not settle it for everyone. Note also that the heading here, לַמַּעֲלוֹת (la-ma-a-lot, v.1), has the preposition where the other fourteen Songs of Ascents have the definite article.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The insistence in verse 4 that the keeper of Israel does not sleep reads differently once you notice how often sleeping gods appear in the surrounding literature; Elijah taunts the prophets of Baal on Carmel by suggesting their god may be asleep and needs waking. Against that, the doubled denial here is a claim about the kind of God being described rather than a piece of reassurance. The move from your keeper in verse 3 to the keeper of Israel in verse 4 and back to your keeper in verse 5 has been read as the reason the promise holds for an individual at all: the private guard is a share in the national one. The evils named are the ones a traveller actually meets, a loose stone and the sun, which keeps the psalm concrete even while the last verse turns unlimited.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The word shade in verse 5 invites a reading it does not require. A shade copies whatever the body does, so the verse can be heard as describing a relation that returns what is put into it, which makes the promise conditional on the one receiving it without adding any condition to the words. Verse 6 is read in the same register: sun and moon become two kinds of exposure rather than two lights, the overwhelming and the reflected, and the addressee is guarded against both extremes of a single axis. The soul in verse 7 is nefesh, the lowest of the soul's levels and the one bound to the body, which is why the psalm can promise to keep it in the same breath as a foot.
