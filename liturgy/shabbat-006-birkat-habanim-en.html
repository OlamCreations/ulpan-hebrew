Birkat HaBanim: Blessing the Children

בִּרְכַּת הַבָּנִים · Friday night · hands on the head · Numbers 6:24-26
HomeBirkat HaBanim
Blessing the children on Friday night, the parent places both hands on the child's head and recites the appropriate verse. For boys: "May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh", Joseph's two sons, the only grandchildren Jacob explicitly blessed (Gen 48). For girls: "May God make you like Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel, and Leah", the four matriarchs. Then for either: the Priestly Blessing (Birkat Kohanim, Num 6:24-26), the same three-line blessing the kohanim recited in the Temple, now spoken parent-to-child every Friday night in homes around the world. Often the parent kisses the child's forehead afterward. One of the most emotionally weighted moments of the Jewish week.

Read Numbers 6:22-27 on Sefaria for the surrounding biblical/liturgical context.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Friday night, after returning home from synagogue (or before sitting down for Kiddush), before the meal. Some families do it after Kiddush instead.
  • How, parent places both hands on the child's head (one hand if the child is small). Says the gender-appropriate opening verse, then the three lines of Birkat Kohanim. Often ends with a kiss on the forehead.
  • Why Ephraim and Manasseh for boys, Jacob's grandsons (Joseph's sons), born and raised in Egypt, are the only grandchildren Jacob singled out for blessing (Gen 48:20). The verse says: "By you Israel will bless, saying: May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh." They preserved Jewish identity in a non-Jewish land, the model for every Diaspora child.
  • Why the four matriarchs for girls, Sarah, Rivkah, Rachel, Leah are the founders of the Jewish lineage. Note: Bilhah and Zilpah (Jacob's other two wives, mothers of 4 of the 12 tribes) are often added by some families. The standard list is the four.
  • Birkat Kohanim, the Priestly Blessing from Numbers 6:24-26 is one of the oldest texts in the Tanakh. It was uncovered in 1979 in a small silver scroll from the 7th century BCE, the oldest biblical text ever found, predating the Dead Sea Scrolls by ~500 years. The words a child hears Friday night are the same words used in the Temple 2,700 years ago.
  • Pronominal suffixes: notice how Birkat Kohanim addresses a single "you". Yevarech-cha (he will bless you, ms suffix), yishmer-cha (he will keep you), elei-cha (toward you), le-cha (for you). For a daughter, the suffix shifts to -ech: yevarech-ech, yishmer-ech, etc. Pick the right suffix for each child.

♦ About this prayer

Two hands on a child's head. Three lines of biblical Hebrew. The single most concrete transmission ritual in Jewish life.

The Priestly Blessing: three escalating gifts

Numbers 6:24-26 is structurally elegant. Each line has more words than the last (3, 5, 7 words in Hebrew), and each names a different mode of divine attention:

Line 1, protection: "May the LORD bless you and keep you." Bracha (blessing) and shmirah (guarding), outer-circle care.

Line 2, favor: "May the LORD shine His face on you and be gracious to you." Or panim (light of the face, engagement, attention) and chen (grace), middle-circle care.

Line 3, peace: "May the LORD lift His face to you and grant you peace." Nesi'at panim (lifting the face, full presence) and shalom (peace, integration), inner-circle care.

The blessing moves from outer protection → middle favor → inner peace. The Hebrew structure is a deliberate crescendo.

Why Ephraim and Manasseh, in detail

The Genesis 48 scene: Jacob is dying in Egypt. Joseph brings his two sons, Manasseh (the elder) and Ephraim (the younger). Jacob crosses his hands (deliberately, despite Joseph's protest) and gives the younger Ephraim the right-hand blessing. He says: "By you Israel will bless, saying, May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh."

The rabbis read this as a model for diaspora Jewish identity: Ephraim and Manasseh grew up in Egypt's most assimilated environment (Joseph was second-in-command to Pharaoh), they spoke Egyptian, they had Egyptian names, and yet Jacob saw enough Jewish identity in them to bless them as full inheritors. They are the proof that you can live in another culture and still raise Jewish children.

What parents add to the blessing

After the formal text, many parents whisper a private blessing, for the child's health, character, future. There is no fixed text for this; it is the moment when the parent says what's actually on their heart, eye-to-eye with the child, hands still resting on their head. Some children remember these private words for decades.

For very young children, the parent's voice is part of the early childhood soundscape of Jewish life. For teenagers, the blessing often becomes brief and slightly awkward, but they still come for it. For adult children who visit, the blessing returns, sometimes more emotional than ever.
