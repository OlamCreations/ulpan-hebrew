Tehilim 108: Through God we will do valiantly

תְּהִלִּים ק״ח · שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 108
Fourteen verses, and only the first of them is new. Verses 2 to 6 are the closing half of Psalm 57, verses 7 to 14 are the closing half of Psalm 60, and the three word heading at verse 1 replaces the two long headings those psalms carry. Nothing here needs to be learned twice: if you have worked through 57 and 60 you already have the vocabulary, and the exercise is different. Read this page for the seam. The two halves came from a song of confidence sung in hiding and a lament after a defeat, and putting them end to end produces a psalm that praises first and asks second, which is the reverse of both parents. The wording is not quite identical either, and the small differences are listed verse by verse below.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The plain fact about this psalm is its construction. Verse 1 is its own heading; verses 2 to 6 reproduce Psalm 57:8 to 12; verses 7 to 14 reproduce Psalm 60:7 to 14. Read as a single piece it has a shape neither parent has. Psalm 57 goes from danger to song, so its praise is an arrival. Psalm 60 goes from defeat to a request for help, so its plea is an ending. Psalm 108 puts the arrival first and then asks, which makes the questions of verses 11 and 12 land after the singer has already announced that God is exalted above the heavens. The seam is between verses 6 and 7, and it is audible: verse 6 is the last line of a refrain, verse 7 opens with a purpose word, so that the exaltation of verse 6 is retrospectively given a reason, that Your beloved may be rescued. Nothing was invented to join them. The join is the word at the start of verse 7 doing work it did not do in Psalm 60.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The differences from the parent psalms are small and worth counting, because they are the only places where this text is not simply quotation. Against Psalm 57: my heart is steady is said once instead of twice in verse 2, and the word my glory moves back from the next verse into this one, so that verse 3 loses one of its two imperatives to awake; the four letter name stands in verse 4 where Psalm 57 has my Lord, and the second verb of that verse gains a joining vav; verse 5 says the kindness is great above the heavens where Psalm 57 says as far as the heavens; verse 6 gains a vav on and over all the earth. Against Psalm 60: verse 9 loses the vav on the second mine; verse 11 uses one noun for a fortified city where Psalm 60 uses another; verse 12 lacks the pronoun You. There are also spelling differences that do not change a word, such as a vowel letter present in one text and absent in the other. The one difference that changes meaning is in verse 10: this psalm has a first person verb, over Philistia I raise a shout, while Psalm 60 has a feminine imperative addressed to Philistia herself. The direction of borrowing is disputed. The common view is that this psalm is the later compilation; it has also been argued that all three draw on older liturgical material, and the manuscripts settle neither.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

A psalm made of two other psalms is a statement about how scripture was used, not only about how it was written. Two songs from named crises, a cave and a lost battle, are stripped of their headings and rejoined under three neutral words, and what was autobiography becomes something a congregation can say on any morning. The reuse also performs an argument. Placing the confidence of Psalm 57 before the complaint of Psalm 60 means the complaint is spoken by someone who has already sung, so the questions in verse 11 are not the questions of a man who has lost faith but of a man who has kept it and still has no army. Read that way, הֲלֹא (ha-lo, v.12), is it not, is the pivot of the whole compilation: praise does not exempt anyone from asking why the help has not come. The absence of the pronoun You in that verse, compared with the parent text, has been read as softening the charge, though a single missing word is thin ground for a doctrine.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two halves can be read as two moments of the same movement rather than as two documents. The first half is upward: heart steady, instruments woken, the dawn woken after them, kindness measured above the heavens, glory over all the earth. The second half is downward and outward: the land parcelled out by name, the neighbouring kingdoms reduced to household objects, the armies, the foe. Between them stands the right hand of verse 7, which in kabbalistic idiom is the side of kindness and expansion, and it is asked to do the saving. On this reading the psalm is a description of how a thing sung in the small hours reaches the ground: it is woken privately at אָעִירָה (a-i-ra, v.3), published among the nations in verse 4, raised above the heavens in verse 6, and only then applied to a specific map with specific enemies. Praise is not a mood here. It is the first stage of a descent into the world where the help is actually needed.
