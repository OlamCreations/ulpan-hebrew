Tehilim 73: Until I came into the sanctuaries of God

תְּהִלִּים ע״ג · מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף
HomePs 73
Twenty eight verses by Asaph, and the psalm that opens Book Three of the Psalter. It states its conclusion in the first line and then spends fifteen verses arguing against it: the wicked are comfortable, they die without pain, and the speaker who kept his hands clean is beaten every morning. The argument does not get answered by an argument. It gets answered by a place, in verse 17. For a learner the thing to track is one small word, the particle at אַךְ (ach, v.1), which returns at אַךְ (ach, v.13) and אַךְ (ach, v.18): three times someone says surely, and each time it introduces a different certainty, the last two contradicting the first.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

One thesis, one long objection, one turn, one resolution. Verse 1 states the conclusion. Verses 2 to 16 dismantle it with a catalogue: the wicked die easily, dress in pride, speak against heaven, and get richer, while the man who kept himself clean is punished daily. Verse 17 is the turn, and what changes is not the evidence but the vantage point. From verse 18 the psalm speaks to God rather than about him, and it never returns to the third person. The last movement, verses 21 to 28, rereads the crisis: the speaker calls himself a beast, discovers that even the beast was being held by the hand, and ends by putting the word good from verse 1 back in place with a new meaning. Track the phrase but as for me at וַאֲנִי (va-a-ni, v.2) and again at וַאֲנִי (va-a-ni, v.28). It opens the complaint and it closes the answer, and the two sentences it introduces are opposites.

רמז · Remez: allusion

This psalm carries an unusual number of cruxes, and a learner should know which are real. In verse 1 the consonants of the word for to Israel can be divided differently to give to the upright, which yields God is good to the upright rather than to Israel. That division is a conjecture; the received text and the vocalisation both read Israel, and the reading is disputed. Verse 4 has the same kind of problem: לְמוֹתָם (le-mo-tam, v.4), at their death, can be re-divided to attach its last syllable to the following clause, giving they have no pangs, sound and fat is their body. Verse 10 is genuinely obscure, and published translations differ from each other more than they differ from the Hebrew. At בָּעִיר (ba-ir, v.20) the consonants are read by some as in the city and by others as when You rouse Yourself, from the root for waking, which is the reading the dream in the same verse supports. In verse 22 the word for beast is a plural form used for a single animal, which is why some render it a great brute. The verb at תִּקָּחֵנִי (ti-ka-che-ni, v.24) is the ordinary verb to take, and it is also the verb used of Enoch in Genesis 5 and of Elijah in 2 Kings 2, which is why the line has been read as more than a promise of guidance.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Psalm 73 opens Book Three of the Psalter and heads the block of Asaph psalms, so its placement puts a crisis of theodicy where a reader expects a hymn. Later readers took the plural at מִקְדְּשֵׁי (mik-de-shei, v.17), the sanctuaries, to mean more than a building, and the word for their end in the same verse carries in later Hebrew the sense of what comes after, so that the answer given in the sanctuary is understood as a view past the edge of a lifetime. On that reading verses 24 to 26 are not about consolation in old age but about what survives it, and the flesh failing in verse 26 is allowed to fail. There is a quieter reading that stays inside this life: what the sanctuary supplies is not information about the future but company in the present, and the proof is verse 23, where the hand was already being held during the years the speaker calls himself a beast. Both readings are current. Verse 15 is the ethical hinge of the whole thing: the speaker does not conclude that his complaint was wrong, only that broadcasting it would have injured people who had done nothing, and that judgement is made before he has any answer at all.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read inwardly, the psalm is about the two things a person can take as a measure. The wicked are measured from outside, by fat, clothing, wealth and the absence of pain, and the mystical reading takes the eye pushed forward by fat in verse 7 as the exact image of a consciousness that can no longer see anything but its own surface. Against that, verse 26 sets an inner measure and calls it the rock of my heart, a phrase in which the heart has a foundation that is not the heart. The beast of verse 22 is read as the animal soul, the part of a person that is entirely honest about wanting what the wicked have, and the psalm does not kill it; it discovers that it too was being held by the right hand, which is the side of kindness. The last word of the whole argument is nearness, at קִרְבַת (kir-vat, v.28), a noun built from the verb to draw close: not a reward for the pure of heart, but the definition of what good was in verse 1 all along.
