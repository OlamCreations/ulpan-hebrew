Tehilim 39: Let me know my end

תְּהִלִּים ל״ט · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִידוּתוּן
HomePs 39
Fourteen verses on how short a life is, spoken by someone who decided not to speak. The psalm opens with a vow of silence kept in front of an enemy, records what that silence did to the speaker, and then breaks it. What comes out is not the complaint he was holding in but a request to be told the length of his own days. The word for breath or vapour, the one that runs through Ecclesiastes, appears three times, and the psalm ends without a resolution: the last line asks God to look away. It is addressed to Jeduthun, one of the Levites David appointed over the music.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A vow, its cost, its collapse, and what came out. Verses 2 to 4 narrate: I decided to say nothing while the wicked man was watching, I said nothing, it burned, I spoke. Verses 5 to 14 are the speech, and it is not the complaint that was being held back. It asks for the length of a life, gets an answer that is worse than the question, and turns into a request first for forgiveness and then for distance. The refrain about every man being a breath lands twice, at הֶבֶל (he-vel, v.6) and הֶבֶל (he-vel, v.12), with a third instance inside verse 7 at הֶבֶל (he-vel, v.7). Nothing is resolved. The final word at וְאֵינֶנִּי (ve-ei-ne-ni, v.14) is a negation of existence, and the psalm stops there.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The word at בְּצֶלֶם (be-tze-lem, v.7) is the one used in Genesis for the image in which humanity was made, and here it stands for something insubstantial that a man walks around as. Translators split over it: some keep the sense of image or semblance, others read it as a shadow or phantom, and the choice changes whether the line is about being a copy or about being unreal. The verb at נֶאֱלַמְתִּי (ne-e-lam-ti, v.3) and נֶאֱלַמְתִּי (ne-e-lam-ti, v.10) is the same in both places, from the root for being mute, and it holds the psalm's two silences together. Two other words are worth stopping on: מַחְסוֹם (mach-som, v.2), a muzzle, which is an object put on an animal, and חָדֵל (cha-del, v.5), a rare adjective from the root meaning to cease, so the speaker asks not how long he will live but how much of a stopping he already is.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The claim at גֵר (ger, v.13) and תּוֹשָׁב (to-shav, v.13), that the speaker is a stranger and a settler with God, has two anchors elsewhere in the Bible. Leviticus 25 gives the reason the land cannot be sold permanently, that the people are strangers and settlers with God, who owns it; and in 1 Chronicles 29 David uses the same pair of words in prayer, saying that we are strangers before You and settlers like all our fathers. Read against those, the line is not self-pity. It is a legal argument: a resident alien has no property claim but does have a claim on the protection of the householder, and the request that follows is exactly what a tenant may ask. The tears of the same verse are treated in the same register, as something submitted rather than merely shed.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical readings take the last verse as the hard one, because asking God to look away is the reverse of every other prayer in the book. One line of reading treats the psalm as a description of a soul under a scrutiny it cannot survive, where the withdrawal asked for is the concealment that makes a limited creature able to exist at all, and the request is therefore not for abandonment but for a measured distance. The word at הָשַׁע (ha-sha, v.14), look away, and the word for the stranger at גֵר (ger, v.13) then belong together: the guest lives by the host's restraint. The three occurrences of breath are read the same way, less as a verdict on human worth than as a description of what a thing made of breath needs from what made it.
