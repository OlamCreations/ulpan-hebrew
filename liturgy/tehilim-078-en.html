Tehilim 78: Give ear, my people, to my teaching

תְּהִלִּים ע״ח · מַשְׂכִּיל לְאָסָף
HomePs 78
Seventy-two verses, the second longest psalm in the book, and the only one that tells the whole national story from the exodus to the choice of David. It opens like a teacher, not like a singer: listen, incline your ear, I will speak in a parable. Then it runs the wilderness generation through a cycle that repeats four or five times, rescue, forgetting, craving, anger, mercy, and it does not resolve the cycle so much as relocate it, ending at Zion and at a shepherd from the sheepfolds. For a learner it is the best place in the psalter to watch the vav-consecutive narrative past at work: verse after verse begins with the same prefix and the same jolt forward in time.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm is a lesson with a shape. Verses 1 to 8 announce the method and the stakes: this is teaching handed down, and the failure it guards against is the fathers' own. Verses 9 to 39 tell the first cycle, exodus and sea and rock, then craving, quotation of the complaint in verses 19 and 20, anger, and mercy that stops short. Verses 40 to 64 tell it again from a different angle, this time with the plagues of Egypt, the conquest by measuring line, and an ending the first cycle did not have: Shiloh abandoned, the ark handed to an adversary, priests dead and widows not weeping. Verses 65 to 72 turn on a single waking. The two closing choices are stated flatly, Ephraim not chosen and Judah chosen, and no reason is given except love. Note what the psalm does not do. It never says the people repented and were forgiven. Mercy in רַחוּם (ra-chum, v.38) is grounded in God's character and in the observation of בָשָׂר (va-sar, v.39) that they are flesh, wind that goes and does not return. The relief at the end comes from a change of place and a change of shepherd, not from a change of heart in the people.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The opening claims a genre and then seems not to deliver it. Verse 2 promises בְמָשָׁל (ve-ma-shal, v.2), a parable, and חִידוֹת (chi-dot, v.2), riddles, and what follows is straight narrative. One resolution is that the riddle is not in the events but in why they repeat, which is the question the psalm never answers directly. Several words in the psalm are genuinely hard. The last verb of verse 41, הִתְווּ (hit-vu, v.41), occurs so rarely that translators split between marking a limit, wounding, and grieving, and the older versions do not agree. The noun in verse 47, בַּחֲנָמַל (ba-cha-na-mal, v.47), appears nowhere else in the Bible, so hail, frost and flooding have all been proposed on the strength of the parallel line alone. Verse 65 is the most exposed line in the book: God וַיִּקַץ (va-yi-katz, v.65), wakes, like a sleeper and like a fighter loud with wine. Ancient and medieval readers were uncomfortable enough with it to reach for figurative readings, and the Hebrew itself gives no signal that it is anything but a simile squarely stated. Watch also the vocabulary of turning, which stitches the whole psalm: Ephraim turns back in battle in verse 9, the people turn back in repentance in verse 34, they turn back in betrayal in verse 57, and God turns His anger back in verse 38.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The psalm hands later readers a hard question and no answer, which is exactly the condition midrash works in. Why Ephraim, when nothing in the story of the exodus singles that tribe out? Readings offered over the centuries include a premature attempt to leave Egypt before the appointed time, the location of the shrine at Shiloh in Ephraimite territory, and the northern kingdom's later history read backward into its ancestor, since Ephraim is the standard name for the north in the prophets. The psalm supports the second and third without stating either. A second question the tradition presses: what is the status of the repentance in verses 34 and 35, which the psalm demolishes two lines later as flattery? It has been read as a warning that returning under duress is not returning, and also, more generously, as evidence that even a repentance made of fear is registered, since the God of verse 38 keeps pardoning people whose hearts verse 37 says are not steady. A third reading takes the whole psalm as an argument for Jerusalem addressed to an audience that remembered Shiloh, in which case the recital of sin is not accusation but proof that the transfer of the sanctuary was earned rather than arbitrary.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read mystically the psalm is about two names and the traffic between them. Judgement and mercy alternate through it without resolution, and the alternation is the structure rather than a defect in it. Verse 38 is the axis: pardon, restraint, anger turned back before it is fully roused, which is read as strict measure deliberately held under its own capacity so that a world can keep standing. The image of not rousing all the wrath implies a wrath that has more in it, and the mystical readings take that surplus seriously rather than as a figure of speech. Verse 65 becomes, in this register, an account of concealment and its end: the sleeper is not absent but withdrawn, and waking describes the return of an attention that never actually left. The final movement is then not a reward but a repair. The shepherd of verse 52 is God and the shepherd of verse 71 is a man, and the same verb covers both, which is read as the pattern by which what is above works through what is below. Verse 72 places the whole thing in a human heart and a pair of hands, and ends the psalm on the word for leading rather than on any word for arriving.
