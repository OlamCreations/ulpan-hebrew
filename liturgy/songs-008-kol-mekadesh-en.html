Kol Mekadesh: Everyone who sanctifies the seventh day

Friday night, at the table · כָּל מְקַדֵּשׁ
HomeKol Mekadesh
The longest of the Friday night zemirot and usually the first one sung, before the food arrives. Eight stanzas, each rhyming on a single sound, and the opening letters run through the alphabet — this is an acrostic poem of the kind the psalms use, stretched over a table song. Its subject is not the Sabbath in the abstract but the people who keep it: almost every stanza names a class of person and says what they do.

פְּסִיעָה (psi-a) and קְטַנָּה (kta-na) — a small step — is a detail from the Talmud about how one walks on Shabbat, and שָׁלשׁ (sha-losh) names the three meals. כַּבְּהֵמָה (kab-he-ma) through הָהָר (ha-har) is Isaiah 63 and כִּנְחָלִים (kin-cha-lim) through נָהָר (na-har) is Balaam's blessing in Numbers 24, so the poem ends on two of the most visual passages in the Tanakh. Five lines rather than four: the last stanza runs long, which is normal for an acrostic that has to reach the end of the alphabet.

♫ Singing notes

  • Long. Eight stanzas is more than most tables sing, and stopping after three or four is completely normal.
  • Each stanza has its own rhyme, and the rhyme changes when the stanza does. That is your signal that a new one has started.
  • Around 100 BPM, and it is usually the first zemer of the evening, sung before the food comes out while everyone still has breath.
  • Unlike Tzur Mishelo there is no refrain, so a guest cannot join halfway. Listen through one stanza before trying.

♦ About this song

An alphabetical acrostic

The opening letters run through the alphabet, which is the form of several psalms and of Eshet Chayil. Over eight stanzas it acts as a memory aid for the singer and as a formal constraint on the poet, who has to find a word for each letter that also fits the stanza's rhyme.

About people, not about the day

Almost every stanza opens by naming a class of person — whoever sanctifies, lovers of the LORD, those who seek, the pure, those who rest. The poem is a description of a community rather than of a calendar, which is unusual among the zemirot.

Quotation everywhere

Numbers, Kings, Genesis 2, Psalm 36, Psalm 118, Isaiah 63, Balaam's blessing. None marked. Reading the page slowly, a learner will meet half a dozen verses they already know embedded in lines that are not otherwise familiar.
