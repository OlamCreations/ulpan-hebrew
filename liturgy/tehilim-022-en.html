Tehilim 22: My God, my God, why have You forsaken me

תְּהִלִּים כ״ב · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 22
Thirty-two verses that begin at the bottom and end at the ends of the earth. Twenty-one verses of complaint, then one word in verse 22 that changes tense and everything with it, then ten verses of public praise that widen from one man to his brothers, to the assembly, to every family of the nations, to the dead. For a learner it is a drill in the object suffix: forsaken me, surrounded me, encircled me, closed in on me, answered me, save me. The same two letters at the end of verb after verb, first with enemies as the subject and then with God. A rabbinic tradition reads the superscription's hind of the dawn as Esther, and the psalm is associated with Purim.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two unequal halves joined by one word. Verses 2 to 21 are complaint: abandonment, mockery, bulls, dogs, a body coming apart, clothes already divided. Verses 23 to 32 are praise, and it expands in stages, from brothers to assembly to the seed of Jacob to every family of the nations to those going down to the dust. The join is עֲנִיתָנִי (a-ni-ta-ni, v.22), a perfect tense meaning You have answered me, sitting at the end of a verse that began by begging to be saved. Nothing in the text narrates the rescue. It is simply spoken of as done, and everything after it is in a different key. Read straight through, the psalm is one person's account of what it is like to be answered late.

רמז · Remez: allusion

כָּאֲרִי (ka-a-ri, v.17) is one of the most disputed words in the Hebrew Bible, and the honest report is that it is not settled. The Masoretic Text, the received Jewish text, vocalizes it as a preposition plus the noun for lion, giving "like the lion, my hands and my feet." That clause has no verb, which is unusual but not impossible in poetry; traditional Jewish commentary supplies the sense from the image already running through verses 14 and 22, a lion's mouth on the hands and feet. Some Hebrew manuscripts, and a Psalms scroll found at Nahal Hever, read a form ending in a different final letter, which has been taken as a verb and translated "they dug" or "they bound" or "they pierced"; the ancient Greek version reads a verb of digging, and Christian tradition has read it as piercing. The two readings differ by one letter, and the two letters concerned are close enough in the square script that scribes confuse them elsewhere. This page does not decide between them. It notes that the Masoretic reading is the text Jewish liturgy and commentary work from, that the variant is real and old, and that the verse is hard in every version. Elsewhere in the psalm the wordplay is easier to see: the roaring of שַׁאֲגָתִי (sha-a-ga-ti, v.2) and the roaring lion of verse 14 are near neighbours, and the rare noun for strength in אֱיָלוּתִי (e-ya-lu-ti, v.20) shares a root with the hind of אַיֶּלֶת (a-ye-let, v.1).

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

A rabbinic tradition reads the hind of the dawn in אַיֶּלֶת (a-ye-let, v.1) and הַשַּׁחַר (ha-sha-char, v.1) as Esther, and the psalm has been attached to Purim on that basis: the dawn that arrives only after the night has gone as far as it can, and a deliverance that appears with no visible miracle in it. Read that way the speaker is not only David but the community in exile, and the mockers of verse 9 who say let God rescue him are the taunt of every generation that watched Jews trust and get nothing. The reading also explains why the second half insists so hard on public telling: an act of rescue that leaves no trace has to be declared or it is lost. The verse that answers the opening is הִסְתִּיר (his-tir, v.25) and פָּנָיו (pa-nav, v.25), He did not hide His face. The Torah's own phrase for exile is the hiding of the face, and the psalm ends by denying that the hiding was ever an abandonment, without pretending it was not felt as one.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the mystical reading the superscription is the whole map. First light is measured from the darkest point, and the psalm insists that the two are the same event seen from different ends of the night. The moment of maximum concealment, עֲזַבְתָּנִי (a-zav-ta-ni, v.2), and the declaration that no face was ever hidden, הִסְתִּיר (his-tir, v.25), are one state described twice. The kabbalistic tradition reads the hiding of the face as contraction rather than absence, a withdrawal that makes room for a creature to exist as a separate self and therefore to cry out at all. On that reading the unanswered calling of verse 3 is not a failure of the relation but its cost, and the last three words of the psalm, He has done it, are put in the perfect because from the far side the whole night reads as a single completed act.
