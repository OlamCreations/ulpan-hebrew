Maoz Tzur: Rock of my salvation

Chanukah, after lighting the candles · מָעוֹז צוּר
HomeMaoz Tzur
Sung every night of Chanukah once the candles are lit, and the tune is one of the few in the Ashkenazi repertoire that everyone in the room already knows. What is less known is the shape of the poem: six stanzas, each about a different exile — Egypt, Babylon, Haman, the Greeks — and the acrostic of the first letters spells the poet's name. Most tables sing the first stanza and stop, which is a pity, because the first stanza is the only one that is not about history.

Four lines that rhyme on the same syllable — לְשַׁבֵּחַ (le-sha-be-ach), נְזַבֵּחַ (ne-za-be-ach), הַמְנַבֵּחַ (ham-na-be-ach), הַמִּזְבֵּחַ (ha-miz-be-ach) all end in -be-ach — and that rhyme is doing an argument. חֲנֻכַּת (cha-nu-kat) is the word chanukah itself: dedication. The poem announces its own subject in its last word, and the festival is named after it.

The sixth stanza is often omitted from printed siddurim and from most tables, and it is the one place the poem stops narrating and starts demanding. Whether to sing it is a decision each household makes; it is printed here because a poem with its last stanza removed is a different poem.

♫ Singing notes

  • Everyone sings the first stanza. Whether the table goes on to the other five is a household habit — ask before you launch into the second.
  • The melody is strophic: the same eight bars carry all six stanzas, so once you have the first you have all of them.
  • Lines one and two are the question, lines three and four are the answer. Let the voice lift at the end of line two and settle at the end of line four.
  • It is sung standing, in front of the candles, immediately after the blessings and while the flames are still small.
  • Around 96 BPM in a room; slower if the group is mostly reading it off a page for the first time.

♦ About this song

The acrostic

The first letters of the five original stanzas spell the name Mordechai. The sixth stanza does not fit that acrostic and is generally taken to be a later addition, which is one reason it is printed in some editions and not others.

Five exiles, one festival

Egypt, Babylon, Haman, the Greeks, and then the present. Chanukah occupies one stanza of five. The poem treats the festival as one entry in a series rather than as its subject, and the last stanza makes the series open-ended.

The tune

The melody now sung almost everywhere in the Ashkenazi world is a much later borrowing than the words, which are medieval. It is not reproduced here, and it does not need to be: you will hear it the first night you are in anyone's home during the festival.

Text and licence

The Hebrew on this page is the Daat Siddur Ashkenaz text, in the public domain, taken from Sefaria and pinned in this repository. Two variant readings marked in that edition were removed rather than printed, since they are alternatives to a word and not extra words to sing.
