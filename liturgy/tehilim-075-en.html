Tehilim 75: God is the judge: one He lowers, one He lifts

תְּהִלִּים ע״ה · לַמְנַצֵּחַ אַל
HomePs 75
Eleven verses that change speaker three times without announcing it: a congregation gives thanks, God answers in the first person, and a single voice turns to warn the arrogant. Asaph's name is on the heading, as it is on Psalm 50 and on the run from 73 to 83. The word to follow is horn, which appears four times and means the raised head of an animal, so power that can be seen from a distance. Almost everything in the psalm is either being lifted or being brought down. In the thirty-day reading cycle it falls on the fourteenth day.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A courtroom psalm with no trial scene in it, only the announcement that a date has been set. The congregation speaks in verse 2, God speaks in verses 3 and 4, a human voice warns the arrogant from verse 5, and by verse 11 it is no longer marked who is speaking at all. The physical spine is the horn, the raised head of a bull or a ram and the standard image for visible power: it is forbidden at קָרֶן (ka-ren, v.5) and קַרְנְכֶם (kar-ne-chem, v.6), cut off at קַרְנֵי (kar-nei, v.11), and lifted at קַרְנוֹת (kar-not, v.11). Around it the verb for raising keeps returning, in the warning at תָּרִימוּ (ta-ri-mu, v.6), in the verdict at יָרִים (ya-rim, v.8), and in the last line at תְּרוֹמַמְנָה (te-ro-mam-na, v.11). The wicked raise their own horn; the righteous have theirs raised for them.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The heading is a tune name, Do Not Destroy, which also heads Psalms 57, 58 and 59, and its two words אַל (al, v.1) and תַּשְׁחֵת (tash-chet, v.1) are exactly the form the psalm uses later for its own prohibitions at אַל (al, v.5) and אַל (al, v.6). The real crux is הָרִים (ha-rim, v.7). The same consonants can be read as the plural noun mountains, giving the wilderness of mountains as a fourth place that promotion does not come from, or as a form of the verb to lift, which is how the JPS translation takes it when it renders the line as nor yet from the wilderness cometh lifting up. Both readings are current and the Hebrew does not settle it. Note that on the second reading the verse ends on the psalm's key verb, which would make the crux the hinge. סֶּלָה (se-la, v.4) is another unsettled word: Selah is a performance direction whose meaning is not known.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The cup at כוֹס (chos, v.9) is not this psalm's invention. It is the cup of wrath the prophets pass around, poured out in Isaiah 51 and handed to the nations in Jeremiah 25, and its logic is that judgement is a drink rather than a blow: everyone gets a share, and the last share is the sediment. Read together with מוֹעֵד (mo-ed, v.3), the appointed time, the psalm becomes an answer to the oldest complaint in the book, that the wicked prosper. The answer offered is not that they do not prosper but that the matter is scheduled, and that a docket held by a judge who has set the pillars of the earth is not the same thing as a case that has been dropped.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Verse 4 sets two states of the world against each other in one line: at נְמֹגִים (ne-mo-gim, v.4) the earth and everything on it are melting, and at תִכַּנְתִּי (ti-kan-ti, v.4) the pillars are already fixed. The world is dissolving and standing at the same time, and it holds because of an act described in the first person singular. Read the last word of the psalm back into that image and the pillar acquires a name: צַדִּיק (tza-dik, v.11) is a singular, the righteous one, and Proverbs 10 calls the righteous the foundation of the world. What is lifted at the end is then not a reward handed to a person but the load-bearing member of the structure being put back in place.
