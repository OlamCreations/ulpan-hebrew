Tehilim 95: Come, let us sing to the LORD

תְּהִלִּים צ״ה · לְכוּ נְרַנְּנָה
HomePs 95
Eleven verses, said on Friday evening as the first of the six psalms that open Kabbalat Shabbat, which in the common rites makes it the first line of Shabbat. It begins as an invitation and ends as an indictment, and the turn happens inside a single verse. For a learner it is the best page in the book for the cohortative, the let-us form of the verb: let us sing, let us shout, let us come before, let us bow, let us kneel, six of them in the first six verses, all first person plural, all built by adding an unstressed -a to the end. Learn the pattern here and you can read half the calls to worship in the Psalter.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two unequal halves. Verses 1 to 7a are a congregation talking itself into worship: sing, shout, come before, bow, kneel, with two reasons attached, that God outranks every other power and that God made the sea and the land. Then at הַיּוֹם (ha-yom, v.7), today, the speaker changes without warning and God answers. The answer is a warning built entirely out of one episode in the wilderness, and it ends on an oath. Read the whole psalm aloud and the effect is deliberate: the invitation to worship is granted, and what worship turns out to consist of is being spoken to. The last word, My rest, is left hanging over people who have just been called the flock of His hand.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two of the place names in verse 8 are verbs elsewhere. Exodus 17:7 says the place was called Massah and Meribah because the people quarrelled and because they tested the LORD, and this psalm puts the matching roots back to work: כִּמְרִיבָה (kim-ri-va, v.8) is the quarrel word, and נִסּוּנִי (ni-su-ni, v.9) in the next verse is the testing word. Naming the place is therefore also restating the charge. Two other words are genuinely uncertain. מֶחְקְרֵי (mech-krei, v.4), the deep places, comes from the root for searching out, so it means something like the parts that have to be explored, and וְתוֹעֲפֹת (ve-to-a-fot, v.4), rendered peaks here, is rare enough that translations range across heights, summits and treasures. אָקוּט (a-kut, v.10), rendered I was disgusted, is disputed between loathing and weariness. In verse 11 the word אִם (im, v.11) literally means if, and standing after a verb of swearing it is the ordinary Hebrew way of making an oath negative, so if they enter means they shall not enter.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The single word הַיּוֹם (ha-yom, v.7), today, is the most cited word in the psalm. The Talmud in tractate Sanhedrin gives the exchange in which the Messiah is asked when he is coming and answers today, and the answer is then explained by this verse: today, if you will listen to His voice. The condition, on that reading, is not a threat about the past but an open offer standing at every moment. The same verse also does liturgical work by dividing the congregation from the wilderness generation. The psalm says your fathers, not you, and then says do not harden your heart, so each generation hearing it is placed at the same fork the desert generation stood at and told the outcome is not settled.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

This is the first of six psalms that open Kabbalat Shabbat, and the six are commonly counted against the six days of the week, so the week is handed over one psalm at a time before Shabbat is received. That count gives the last word of this psalm a second life. מְנוּחָתִי (me-nu-cha-ti, v.11), My rest, is the same noun family as the rest of the seventh day, so the sequence that ends with a generation shut out of rest is the sequence that opens the evening on which rest arrives. The two halves of the psalm then read as the two things the week does to a person: it invites, and it hardens. Verse 6 puts three verbs of lowering in a row with no room between them, which is the practical answer the psalm offers to the second of those.
