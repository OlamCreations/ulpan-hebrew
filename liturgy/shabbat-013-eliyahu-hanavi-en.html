Eliyahu HaNavi: Elijah the Prophet

אֵלִיָּהוּ הַנָּבִיא · Saturday night after Havdalah · also Brit Milah + Pesach Seder
HomeEliyahu HaNavi
Sung at the end of Shabbat after Havdalah, at the close of every Passover Seder, at every brit milah (circumcision), and on Saturday night before going to sleep. The lyric is short: just Elijah's three epithets repeated, then a single line of hope. Jewish tradition holds that the prophet Elijah, taken up to heaven alive in a fiery chariot (2 Kings 2), will return as the herald of the Messiah. Every Shabbat that ends without his arrival, the people sing him in. Every Seder leaves a cup of wine for him (Kos Eliyahu) and a door open. Every Brit Milah has an empty chair for him.

Read Eliyahu HaNavi (Sefaria) for the surrounding biblical/liturgical context.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Saturday night immediately after Havdalah. Also at every Brit Milah (where it's chanted while the baby is held by the sandek), and at every Pesach Seder.
  • The melody, varies wildly. The Yemenite tune is slow and contemplative; the Ashkenazi tune is a march-tempo nigun (often sung walking around the table); the Sephardic Andalusian tune lyrical. Pick one you like and stick with it.
  • Three epithets, HaNavi (the Prophet, his function), HaTishbi (from Tishbe, his town), HaGiladi (from Gilead, his region). All three identify him from 1 Kings 17:1. Note the pattern: each definite article ha- attaches directly to the next word, no space. This is the standard way Hebrew makes "the X".
  • The verb yavo, qal jussive of ב.ו.א (to come). The jussive expresses a wish ("may he come!"), close in feel to subjunctive. Same root as bo (come!) and tavo (you will come).
  • Mashiach ben David, the Davidic Messiah, distinct from Mashiach ben Yosef (the warrior Messiah in some traditions, who precedes him). Eliyahu announces the arrival of ben David, the king-from-the-line-of-David who restores Israel.

♦ About this prayer

Six lines. Sung weekly. The prophet who didn't die, kept on hand for the moment things turn.

Who Elijah was

Elijah (אֵלִיָּהוּ, "my God is YHVH") is the 9th-century BCE prophet who confronts King Ahab and his Phoenician wife Jezebel over the worship of Baal in the northern kingdom of Israel. The dramatic showdown on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18), the fleeing to the cave at Horeb where God speaks in a "thin small voice" (1 Kings 19), and the ascent to heaven in a chariot of fire (2 Kings 2) are among the most cinematic stories in the Tanakh.

The crucial detail: Elijah doesn't die. The Tanakh is explicit (2 Kings 2:11), he is taken up in a whirlwind, alive. This means he is, in some sense, still here. The folk tradition develops accordingly: Elijah appears at moments of need (in disguises, as a beggar, as a stranger), he is present at every circumcision, he visits Jewish homes on Friday nights, he is the herald who will announce the Messiah's arrival.

Why at end of Shabbat

The end of Shabbat is the moment Jewish hope re-engages with secular time. For 25 hours the world has been close to its perfected state; now we return to the work-week. Eliyahu HaNavi is the song that says: but you don't have to stay in the imperfect state. Things can change. The redeemer is on his way.

The Talmud (Eruvin 43b) discusses why Elijah cannot come on Shabbat itself, because his arrival would impose obligations (prepare the Temple, gather the people) that violate Shabbat rest. So the people wait for Saturday night, and the first thing they do is sing him in.
