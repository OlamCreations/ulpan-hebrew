Aleinu: It Is Upon Us

עָלֵינוּ · closes every service · 3× daily · the universalist closing
HomeAleinu
The closing prayer of every Jewish service, said at the end of Shacharit, Mincha, Maariv, every day. Two paragraphs: (1) Aleinu proper, "It is upon us to praise the Master of all", affirming Israel's distinctive relationship to God; (2) Al ken n'kaveh, "Therefore we hope", a messianic paragraph anticipating universal recognition of God when all the world will say "the LORD is one". The prayer is attributed to Yehoshua bin Nun (Joshua) in some traditions, but its actual composition is post-biblical (~3rd-5th c. CE), from the Rosh Hashanah Musaf service, where it was the centerpiece. Around the 13th century it was moved into the closing position of every daily service. When said, the worshipper bows at the words "va-anachnu kor'im" ("and we kneel"). This page teaches the first paragraph.

♦ Lesson

  • When, closes Shacharit, Mincha, Maariv. Three times a day. Also a centerpiece of Rosh Hashanah Musaf, where it was originally placed.
  • The bow, when reciting va-anachnu kor'im ("and we kneel"), bow forward. Some communities bow with knees touching the ground (full prostration on Rosh Hashanah). This is one of the few moments in daily prayer where the body posture is explicit.
  • Two paragraphs:
    • 1st: Aleinu proper, Israel's distinctiveness. Most-recited paragraph.
    • 2nd: Al ken n'kaveh, universal messianic hope. "On that day the LORD will be one and His name will be one" (Zechariah 14:9).
  • The original context, Aleinu was composed for the Rosh Hashanah Musaf service, as the introduction to the Malchuyot (Kingship) verses, the section affirming God as universal King. Around the 12-13th c., the rabbis recognized it was too good to use only once a year, and moved it to the closing position of every service.
  • Vocabulary:
    • aleinu (עָלֵינוּ), "upon us / it is on us". Al (upon) + einu (us, 1cp suffix). Carries the sense of obligation: "it falls to us".
    • l'shabe'ach (לְשַׁבֵּחַ), "to praise". Piel infinitive of ש.ב.ח. Same root as shevach (praise), different from tehillah (praise from Tehillim).
    • yotzer b'reshit (יוֹצֵר בְּרֵאשִׁית), "Maker of beginning". A poetic name for God as Creator, drawing on Genesis 1:1 (B'reshit bara Elohim).
    • kor'im / mishtachavim / modim, three verbs of acknowledgment. Kor'im (kneeling, qal ptcp), mishtachavim (prostrating, hitpael), modim (acknowledging, hiphil). Three escalating physical/verbal postures.
  • The phrase "Melech malchei ha-melachim", "King of kings of kings", is a Talmudic intensification. Earthly emperors were "king of kings" (e.g. the Persian king); God is one level above, King of those kings of kings. A poetic Hebrew superlative.

♦ About this prayer

The most-said paragraph in Jewish liturgy after the Shema. Originally a Rosh Hashanah declaration; absorbed into daily prayer in the medieval period; now the closing line of every service worldwide.

The censorship history

Aleinu has a fraught textual history. The original text contains a phrase praising God "who did not make us like the nations... who bow to vanity and emptiness and pray to a god who does not save". In medieval Christian Europe this phrase was read as an attack on Christianity. From the 13th century onward, Christian censors repeatedly demanded its removal; many Ashkenazi siddurim from the 15th-19th centuries had the controversial line struck through or omitted entirely.

The phrase is biblical, it's a direct quote from Isaiah 30:7 and 45:20, referring to idol-worshipers in general (not Christianity specifically). Modern Israeli Ashkenazi siddurim have restored the original text; some diaspora siddurim still omit it. Sephardic, Mizrahi, and Yemenite siddurim never omitted it.

The second paragraph: Al ken n'kaveh

The second paragraph (not on this page) is the more universalist one. After establishing Israel's particular relationship to God in paragraph 1, paragraph 2 looks forward to a future in which all humanity will recognize God as one. Key line: "l'takken olam b'malchut Shaddai", "to perfect the world under the kingship of the Almighty". The phrase tikkun olam ("perfecting / repairing the world") that has become a Jewish slogan in the modern era comes from this paragraph.

The paragraph ends with a quote from Zechariah 14:9: "And the LORD will be King over all the earth, on that day the LORD will be one and His name will be one." The same word echad (one) that the Shema affirms about God's nature today is echoed here as a future-tense political-cosmic claim about the recognition of that oneness.

How services close

The closing sequence of every daily service: Aleinu → Mourner's Kaddish (if mourners are present) → Adon Olam or Yigdal (closing hymn). Aleinu is the universal core; the others depend on context. If you walk into a synagogue and people are standing for Aleinu, you know the service is wrapping up, about 5 more minutes.
