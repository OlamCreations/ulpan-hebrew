Tehilim 106: We have sinned with our fathers

תְּהִלִּים ק״ו · הַלְלוּ יָהּ
HomePs 106
Forty-eight verses of national history told as a confession. Psalm 105 recites the same events as a list of what God did; this one recites them as a list of what Israel did, and the pronoun in verse 6 is we, not they. It closes Book Four of the Psalms, which is why verse 48 blesses God and asks the congregation to answer Amen: that line belongs to the book, not only to the psalm. Watch the verbs of memory. Forgetting is the failure the recital keeps returning to, and remembering is what God finally does in verse 45 to end it.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A confession built on a historical recital. Verses 1 to 5 are praise and one personal request, verse 6 is the confession that governs everything after it, verses 7 to 46 are the record, and verses 47 to 48 are the petition and the doxology. The record runs in order: Egypt, the sea, the wilderness episodes, the entry into the land, and out again into exile, and each unit has the same shape, where the people fail, judgment follows, something or someone intervenes, and God rescues for the sake of the name rather than for merit. Readers commonly count seven rebellions, though the psalm itself never numbers them. What separates this psalm from 105, which recites the same events, is the verb in חָטָאנוּ (cha-ta-nu, v.6): the speaker is not reporting on the ancestors, he is joining them. And the request in וְקַבְּצֵנוּ (ve-kab-tze-nu, v.47) to be gathered from among the nations tells you where he is standing when he says it, which is outside the land, inside the punishment described in verse 27.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two words carry most of the philological weight. כְּבוֹדָם (ke-vo-dam, v.20) reads their glory, and traditional lists of the tikkunei soferim, the scribal emendations, count this verse among them on the claim that the text once read the divine glory instead; whether any such emendation ever took place is disputed, and the received text is the one that is read. וַיְפַלֵּל (vai-fa-lel, v.30) is a form of a root that ordinarily means to pray, but the episode it points to in Numbers 25 demands something closer to intervening or executing judgment, and translators split between the two senses without settling it. וַתֵּחָשֶׁב (va-te-cha-shev, v.31) is the clearest deliberate echo in the psalm: counted to him as righteousness is the phrasing Genesis 15 uses of Abraham, transplanted here onto a priest in the middle of a plague. Note also וַיָּמֹכּוּ (va-ya-mo-ku, v.43), a rare verb of sinking or being brought low, which ends the historical section on a slow downward motion rather than a violent one, and מִהֲרוּ (mi-ha-ru, v.13), where the verb for hurrying is doing the work of an adverb: they were quick to forget.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The intercession verses are where the reading tradition concentrates. בַּפֶּרֶץ (ba-pe-retz, v.23) pictures Moses standing in a breach, the gap in a city wall where an attack comes through, and the image has been read as a definition of what prayer is: not persuasion but occupying a position that would otherwise be empty. פִּינְחָס (pin-chas, v.30) puts Phinehas in the same slot, and the pairing raises a question the tradition returns to, whether the zeal that stops the plague is being praised or only recorded, since the reward in the next verse is granted rather than claimed. The omission of Korah from verse 17, where only Dathan and Abiram are named, was noticed early and read variously; one reading connects it to Numbers 26, which states plainly that the sons of Korah did not die. Across the whole recital the operative teaching is carried by two verbs rather than by any episode: forgetting is treated as the root sin, and remembering, when God does it in verse 45, is what reverses everything the forgetting caused.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes the two ends of the psalm as a single movement and treats the history between them as a test of one word. לְעוֹלָם (le-o-lam, v.1) declares the kindness to be forever, before any of the failures have been named, so every rebellion in the recital becomes an obscuring and every rescue an uncovering, and nothing in the middle is allowed to alter what was said at the start. חֲסָדָו (cha-sa-dav, v.45) closes the circuit by making the abundance of kindness the operative cause of the turn, which means the covenant works downward from above rather than being triggered from below by anything the people do. In that frame וְקַבְּצֵנוּ (ve-kab-tze-nu, v.47), gather us, is heard as more than a national petition: exile is read as a condition of scattering that reaches further than the people who are scattered, and the gathering asked for is the return of everything dispersed to the place it belongs. That the last word of the psalm repeats the first is read as a circle rather than a repetition.
