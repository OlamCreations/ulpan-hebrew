Tehilim 94: God of retribution, shine forth

תְּהִלִּים צ״ד · אֵל נְקָמוֹת
HomePs 94
Twenty three verses, said in the synagogue on Wednesday morning as the psalm of the day, and the hardest of the daily psalms to say lightly: it opens by asking God to take vengeance and spends its first half on a society where killing the powerless has become normal practice. The turn comes at verse 12, from what God should do to what the sufferer can learn. The grammar to watch is one prefixed letter, the interrogative he-, which is all that separates a statement from the question that carries verses 9 and 10.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements. Verses 1 to 7 are the complaint, and they end by quoting the wicked. Verses 8 to 11 answer that quotation with an argument anyone can follow, built on the interrogative he- of הֲנֹטַע (ha-no-ta, v.9) and הֲיֹסֵר (ha-yo-ser, v.10): whoever made the ear is not deaf. Verses 12 to 15 turn from what the wicked deserve to what the sufferer receives, and 16 to 23 drop into the first person and testify. The pivot on the word for discipline is deliberate: it disciplines nations in verse 10 and one man in verse 12, and the psalm treats those as the same act at two scales.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three places repay a second look. הָבֶל (ha-vel, v.11) is the word Ecclesiastes uses for everything, breath or vapour, and here it is the verdict on human calculation rather than on human life. דוּמָה (du-ma, v.17) means silence, and is widely read as a name for the realm of the dead rather than a state of quiet, which makes the verse a report of a near miss. חֹק (chok, v.20) is the ordinary word for a statute or a decree, and the line is not about lawlessness but about harm produced lawfully, which is why it has been so heavily used since. Verse 15 is genuinely unsettled: the syntax allows judgement returning to righteousness or righteousness being the point judgement returns to, and translations divide.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 12 is the seat of the rabbinic category of chastisements of love, suffering read as instruction rather than punishment, and whether such a category exists at all is an old and unresolved argument in the tradition. The tradition that assigns this psalm to the fourth day of the week connects it to the sun and moon made on that day and to a future reckoning with those who worship them, which is why a psalm about human courts is said on a day of luminaries. Verse 9 became a standard argument in its own right: the maker of a faculty cannot be supposed to lack it, and the same reasoning gets applied well beyond the ear and the eye.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm sets two thrones against each other. כִּסֵּא (ki-se, v.20) is a seat of ruin claiming alliance with God, and verse 22 answers it with a stronghold and a rock, which in the mystical reading are the same image seen from below: the throne one cannot join and the rock one can stand on. The closing verb of יַצְמִיתֵם (yatz-mi-tem, v.23) and יַצְמִיתֵם (yatz-mi-tem, v.23) is written twice in the Masoretic text, and the repetition is usually read as emphasis rather than error, the pattern of measure for measure stated once for the act and once for the actor. Nothing is added to the wicked in that ending; what they built is simply left with them, which is the strictest form the tradition gives to justice.
