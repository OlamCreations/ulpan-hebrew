Tehilim 11: In the LORD I take refuge

תְּהִלִּים י״א · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד
HomePs 11
Seven verses that work as an argument. Someone tells David to run for the hills, and he refuses the advice in the opening line before spending the rest of the psalm explaining why. It falls on the second day of the monthly cycle, where the reading runs from Psalm 10 to Psalm 17. The grammar to watch is a pair of roots that cross the two halves: the verb for beholding appears at יֶחֱזוּ (ye-che-zu, v.4) and again at יֶחֱזוּ (ye-che-zu, v.7), the verb for testing at יִבְחֲנוּ (yiv-cha-nu, v.4) and again at יִבְחָן (yiv-chan, v.5). What God does to people in the middle of the psalm is what the upright get to do at the end.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm is a reply, and the thing being replied to is quoted first. Verses 1 to 3 belong to the advisers: flee, the ambush is set, the foundations are going. Verses 4 to 7 are the answer, and the answer denies none of it. The bow stays bent. What changes is the vantage point, from ground level where arrows come out of the dark to a throne from which the dark is not dark. No rescue is promised anywhere in the text. What is promised is that the looking runs in both directions.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two words carry most of the difficulty. At פַּחִים (pa-chim, v.6) the noun means traps or snares everywhere else it appears, yet it sits at the head of a list of fire words, so translations split between snares and burning coals; the JPS line printed above chose coals. At פָנֵימוֹ (fa-nei-mo, v.7) the ending is an archaic poetic suffix, and it has been read both as His face, the thing the upright get to see, and as their faces, which would make the upright the ones being seen. The same verse also puts a singular subject at יָשָׁר (ya-shar, v.7) in front of a plural verb, a mismatch Hebrew tolerates when the singular names a type rather than a person.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 3 is the line that gets quoted on its own, usually as a question about what a decent person can still do once the structures are down. Inside the psalm it is not an open question at all: it is the last thing the advisers say before being answered, and the answer is verse 4. The fire and brimstone of verse 6 point back to Sodom in Genesis 19, which is the Bible's standing case of a place whose foundations were destroyed, and the place where the question of what the righteous accomplish had already been put to God out loud by Abraham.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The two beholding verbs frame the psalm as one act of vision with two directions. God's eyes and eyelids test in verse 4, the upright behold in verse 7, and a single verb serves both, so being examined and being able to look are described as one event rather than two. The eyelid is the odd term. An eye that narrows is an eye adjusting to what it is looking at, and the image gives the divine gaze a measure, a squint, instead of a flat and effortless omniscience.
