Tehilim 130: Out of the depths I have called You

תְּהִלִּים ק״ל · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 130
A Song of Ascents of eight verses, known everywhere by its first two words, out of the depths. It moves in four pairs: a cry, a question about sin, a wait, and then a promise made to the whole people rather than to the one speaking. Three different names for God appear in it, and the shortest of them, Yah, sits in the middle of the hardest verse. In many communities it is added to the morning service through the days between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, and it is said for the sick and in trouble. The grammar to watch is verse 6, which contains no verb at all and borrows one from verse 5.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four pairs. Verses 1 and 2 are the cry and the request to be heard. Verses 3 and 4 are the problem and its answer, put as a question and a reason. Verses 5 and 6 are the wait, said once and then said again more slowly. Verses 7 and 8 turn outward and say the same thing about Israel that the speaker has been saying about himself. The movement is downward to upward and inward to outward at the same time, which is why it can be read as a private prayer and used as a public one. Note that nothing in the psalm is described as having happened. The rescue is still ahead in verse 8, and the only completed actions in the whole text are calling in verse 1 and waiting in verse 5.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The word in מִמַּעֲמַקִּים (mi-ma-a-ma-kim, v.1) is used elsewhere of deep water, so the depths are not a metaphor invented here but the ordinary word for where a drowning person is. קִוִּיתִי (ki-vi-ti, v.5) and קִוְּתָה (kiv-ta, v.5) are from the root kaf vav he, which gives the noun for hope and also words for a cord or line, so waiting in Hebrew is a taut thing rather than a passive one; הוֹחָלְתִּי (ho-chal-ti, v.5) is a second root entirely, and the verse uses both rather than repeating one. הַסְּלִיחָה (has-li-cha, v.4) carries the definite article, the forgiveness, so it is a specific possession and not a general disposition. תִּוָּרֵא (ti-va-re, v.4) is a passive, that You may be feared, and the logic of the verse is what makes it hard: three names for God appear across the psalm, and the shortest of them, יָהּ (yah, v.3), is placed inside the sentence about a record of sins.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 4 is the verse people argue with. If sins are forgiven, why would anyone fear? The reading the verse itself supports is that fear here is not fear of punishment: a system that only punishes produces calculation, and what produces awe is the discovery that the account could have been kept and was not. On that reading forgiveness is the more demanding of the two and the psalm has not softened anything. The other move worth watching is verse 7, where the speaker stops asking for himself and turns to Israel without having received an answer. The wait is not resolved before it is recommended to other people. In Christian use the psalm is one of the seven penitential psalms and is known by its Latin opening, de profundis; in the days between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur many communities add it to the morning service, and the same eight verses do both jobs without adjustment.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

This is the eleventh of the fifteen Songs of Ascents, and the mystical reading takes the sequence as a climb, which makes the depths of verse 1 the bottom of it. The point that reading insists on is that the call comes from the depths rather than from a step already gained: nobody waits until they are higher to speak. Verse 6 supplies the image the tradition keeps returning to, watchmen at the end of a night shift, who do not make the sun rise and cannot hurry it, and whose whole work is to be awake when it does. The last verse gives the plural object, all his iniquities, so what began as one voice at the bottom ends as a whole people brought up, and the psalm never says when.
