Kaddish: The Aramaic Sanctification

קַדִּישׁ · service transitions + mourners daily for 11-12 months · Aramaic, not Hebrew
HomeKaddish
Mourner's Kaddish, said by mourners (during the year after a parent's death and on each yahrzeit thereafter), and by all worshipers as part of the structural transitions of every service. The text is in Aramaic (not Hebrew), the everyday language of Jewish life from ~500 BCE to ~600 CE, the language of the Talmud. The prayer never mentions death. It is entirely a praise of God's greatness, with the hope that His kingdom will be established. The Jewish tradition of saying Kaddish for the dead is a kind of theological defiance: at the moment of greatest sorrow, the response is to multiply praise of God in public. The mourner says Kaddish three times a day for 11 months (Ashkenazi) or 12 months (Sephardi), then once a year on the death anniversary thereafter. ♦ Lesson

  • When, multiple times in every service. The Mourner's Kaddish appears at: end of Pesukei d'Zimra, after the Aleinu, after the daily Psalm, after Torah readings (in some forms). Mourners say it; others respond.
  • Who can say Mourner's Kaddish, anyone in formal mourning for a parent (during the year of mourning + on yahrzeit). In some communities also for spouse, sibling, or child. The mourner must be in a place where there is a minyan (10 Jewish adults), Kaddish cannot be said alone.
  • The response, when the leader says "yehei sh'mei rabba mevarach l'alam ul'almei almaya" ("may His great name be blessed forever and to the ages of ages"), the congregation says it back in unison. This response is considered the heart of the prayer. The Talmud (Berachot 3a) says: "Anyone who answers Amen to a blessing is greater than the one who said the blessing; anyone who answers 'Yehei sh'mei rabba' with all their strength has any harsh decree against them torn up."
  • Aramaic, not Hebrew, Kaddish is in Aramaic because Aramaic was the everyday language of the Babylonian Jewish community (the Talmudic period). The rabbis wanted ordinary people to understand the prayer they were saying, so the structural transitions of the service were composed in the vernacular. Today Hebrew is the vernacular of Jewish life again (in Israel), but Aramaic Kaddish remains the universal form.
  • The strange grammar, clue: it's Aramaic:
    • sh'meh (שְׁמֵהּ), "His name". Aramaic possessive: sh'ma (name) + -eh (his). Hebrew would be sh'mo.
    • rabba (רַבָּא), "great". Aramaic definite form. Hebrew would be gadol or ha-gadol.
    • b'almah (בְּעָלְמָא), "in the world". Aramaic definite. Hebrew would be ba'olam.
    • di v'ra (דִּי בְרָא), "which He created". Aramaic relative pronoun di + verb. Hebrew would be she-bara.
    • v'imru: amen (וְאִמְרוּ: אָמֵן), "and say: Amen". Imru is the imperative plural form in Aramaic.
  • The 7-or-8 names of magnification: after the response, the leader recites 7 (Ashkenaz) or 8 (Sephard) terms for praise: yitbarach, v'yishtabach, v'yitpa'ar, v'yitromam, v'yitnasei, v'yithadar, v'yit'aleh, v'yithallal. Each is a verb in the hitpael (reflexive/passive intensive) stem, expressing the wish that God's name be blessed, praised, glorified, exalted, lifted, beautified, raised, sanctified.

♦ About this prayer

The Aramaic prayer that never mentions death and is yet the universal Jewish prayer for the dead. The pivot point of every service, the obligation that takes a mourner to synagogue for a year, the praise that defies grief.

The text: Geonic era, ~7th-9th c.

The basic Kaddish text appears in fragmentary form in the Talmud (Sotah 49a, where Aramaic doxology following Torah study is described). The full text as we say it today was standardized by the Geonim of Babylonia (~7th-9th c. CE). Different versions developed for different liturgical uses: Mourner's Kaddish, Chatzi Kaddish ("half-Kaddish", shorter, for internal service transitions), Kaddish Shalem ("full Kaddish", at end of Amida sections), Kaddish d'Rabbanan ("Rabbis' Kaddish", after Torah study, including a special paragraph for scholars). All share the opening text on this page.

Why Mourner's Kaddish is for mourners

The custom of mourners specifically saying Kaddish is post-Talmudic, from ~10th-13th c. The story attached to it (from Tractate Kallah): Rabbi Akiva once met a man in tattered clothes carrying a heavy load. The man revealed he was a soul in torment, condemned to suffer for the wickedness he had done in life. Akiva asked: is there any way out? The man answered: only if my son were to say Kaddish in synagogue and the congregation responded "Yehei sh'mei rabba!" Akiva found the son, taught him to read, and brought him to recite Kaddish. The father appeared to Akiva in a dream and thanked him, his sentence had been lifted.

The theological logic: the merit of a mourner causing the public to bless God redounds upon the soul of the departed. By saying Kaddish for 11 months (Ashkenazi) or 12 months (Sephardi), the mourner is actively elevating the soul of the parent in the afterlife. The duration is not coincidental: the rabbis taught that the maximum stay in the post-death purification realm is 12 months, so saying Kaddish for 11 implies that the parent did not deserve the full 12, a mark of respect.

What it's like to be a mourner saying Kaddish

For 11-12 months, the mourner must attend synagogue 3 times a day to recite the prayer. This obligation often pulls people back into Jewish community at the moment they would otherwise drift away. Many secular Israelis and Diaspora Jews who hadn't been to synagogue in years find themselves in synagogue every day after a parent's death. Many describe the experience as profoundly stabilizing, the structure of obligation gives shape to grief.

The prayer itself is paradoxical. The mourner is sad, but the prayer is exuberantly affirmative, "Magnified and sanctified be His great name!" The act of praising God in the worst moment becomes the work of grief. By the end of the year, something has shifted.

The yahrzeit, annual anniversary of the death, continues for the rest of the mourner's life. On that one day each year, the mourner returns to synagogue and again says Kaddish. A small Jewish year-marker that runs through the calendar of every observant family.
