Tehilim 136: For His steadfast love is for ever

תְּהִלִּים קל״ו · הוֹדוּ לַיהֹוָה
HomePs 136
Twenty six verses, and the same second half in every one of them. That is not repetition for its own sake: the psalm is a call and response, one voice reading what God did and the whole room answering. Chronicles and Ezra both show crowds answering with these exact words at dedications, so the shape is documented rather than guessed. Read down the left side only and you get the argument: creation, then the exodus, then the kings east of the Jordan, then us, then bread for everything alive. The tradition calls it the Great Hallel.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Every verse is cut in two by the same second half, which makes this a call and response rather than a poem read straight through: one voice takes the left side, the room answers the right. Chronicles and Ezra both record crowds answering with these exact words at dedications, so the form is attested and not merely inferred from the layout. The left side moves in three blocks. Verses 4 to 9 are creation, following the order of the first chapter of Genesis. Verses 10 to 22 are the exodus and the march east of the Jordan, ending in land held as inheritance. Verses 23 to 25 drop the past tense of history and speak as us, and the last of the three widens past Israel to everything that eats. Verses 1 to 3 and verse 26 are the frame, four calls to give thanks.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The familiar English, His mercy endures for ever, is weaker than what stands there. The noun at חַסְדּוֹ (chas-do, v.1) is covenant loyalty, what one party owes another because of a bond already in place, and for ever is a stretch of time rather than a condition outside it. The grammar makes the psalm's main claim by itself: the deeds are introduced by participles, to the One who makes, to the One who spreads out at לְרֹקַע (le-ro-ka, v.6), to the One who strikes, so that flattening the earth and striking Egypt are the same kind of act stated in the same shape. Lines that continue a previous one drop the participle and take a plain past verb, which is how you can tell the list's joints. Two verbs repay attention: וְנִעֵר (ve-ni-er, v.15), used of shaking crumbs from a garment, and וַיִּפְרְקֵנוּ (va-yif-re-ke-nu, v.24), used of tearing a yoke off a neck. And the sun receives a singular dominion at לְמֶמְשֶׁלֶת (le-mem-she-let, v.8) where the moon and stars share a plural at לְמֶמְשְׁלוֹת (le-mem-shlot, v.9).

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The tradition calls this psalm the Great Hallel, and the reason usually given for the name is its last deed, נֹתֵן (no-ten, v.25), that God gives bread to all flesh. On that reading the greatness is not the sea or the kings but the feeding of every living thing every day, and the psalm is arranged as a descent from the spectacular to the ordinary with the ordinary as the climax. The count of verses, twenty six, matches the numerical value of the four letter divine name, and the match is widely taken as deliberate rather than accidental. The turn at שֶׁבְּשִׁפְלֵנוּ (sheb-shif-le-nu, v.23) is where the reciters stop being an audience: up to that word the psalm lists what was done for other people in other centuries, and from there the deliverance is the singer's own, with no event named, so that any generation can put its own low state into the gap.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In kabbalistic accounts loyalty is the first of the outflowing attributes, the one that gives before anything has been weighed or judged, so a psalm that names it twenty six times is read as a channel held open rather than a receipt for past favours. Notice that the refrain never varies: all the variation sits on the human side of the line, while the answer stays identical, which is itself the claim being made about the giver. The closing title at לְאֵל (le-el, v.26), the God of heaven, is the name that dominates the latest books of the Bible, and the reading takes the whole psalm as one long movement from the heavens made with understanding at בִּתְבוּנָה (bit-vu-na, v.5) down as far as bread, and then back up.
