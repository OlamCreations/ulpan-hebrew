Tehilim 20: May the LORD answer you in a day of distress

תְּהִלִּים כ׳ · לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 20
A blessing said over someone about to go into danger, and then the answer coming back. Verses 2 to 6 are a congregation speaking to one man, and eleven of their words end in the second person singular suffix -kha: your help, your offerings, your heart, your plan, your victory, your requests. Verse 7 changes everything with two words, now I know, and from there the psalm speaks about him rather than to him. It is said on weekday mornings in the standard order of prayer and left out on days of joy when Tachanun is not said, which is the practical clue to what it is for.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A liturgy in two parts around a silence. Verses 2 to 6 are a crowd blessing one man before he goes out, all jussive verbs and all addressed to you; verse 7 answers with a perfect verb, עַתָּה (a-ta, v.7) now, I know. Nothing tells us what happened in between, and the gap is the form: the psalm is built to be said before and after, with the event itself outside the text. Verse 10 closes the circle by repeating the opening verb with a different suffix, so the prayer for the one becomes a prayer for the many.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The last verse is a real crux of division. The Masoretic accents join the words as LORD, save, and then may the King answer us, which makes the King a title for God; the Greek and Latin versions read the King as the object of save, giving LORD, save the king, and answer us. Both readings are current and the choice changes who is being asked for what at הַמֶּלֶךְ (ha-me-lech, v.10). In verse 8 there is no verb at all in the first half, only these with chariots and these with horses, and the single verb belongs to וַאֲנַחְנוּ (va-a-nach-nu, v.8), but we. The verb at יְדַשְּׁנֶה (ye-dash-ne, v.4) is from the root for fat: an offering accepted is one found rich.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The God invoked in verse 2 is not named as the God of Abraham but as the God of Jacob, and readers have long connected that to Jacob as the one who prayed in a day of distress on the night before meeting Esau, so the blessing cites a precedent rather than a general attribute. Verse 4 has been read as the reason the request can be made at all: the offerings brought when nothing was wrong are what stand up now, which turns routine worship into a deposit. Where the psalm is said daily in the morning service, the anointed of verse 7 is understood as any Israel that finds itself in the position the psalm describes.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Verse 8 is read as a statement about where a person locates power. Chariots and horses are the visible instruments, and the mystical reading takes the missing verb in that half line as deliberate: those who trust the instruments have nothing to do but stand next to them. Against that stands the name, which is not a thing but a channel, and the verb attached to it means to bring to mind, that is, to make present by remembering. On this reading verse 9 is not a report of a battle but a description of two structures under pressure, one that is only its own weight and one that is held from elsewhere.
