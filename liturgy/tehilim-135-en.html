Tehilim 135: Praise the name of the LORD

תְּהִלִּים קל״ה · הַלְלוּ יָהּ
HomePs 135
Twenty one verses of praise, framed by the same word at both ends: הַלְלוּ (hal-lu, v.1) at the opening and יָהּ (yah, v.21) at the close. Almost every line of this psalm is found somewhere else in the Bible, which is the first thing to know about it. Verse 7 stands nearly word for word in Jeremiah, verse 13 reuses the pairing of name and renown from Exodus 3, verse 14 is a line of Deuteronomy 32, and verses 15 to 18 are a compressed version of Psalm 115. For a learner it is the easiest praise psalm to read aloud, because it repeats structures deliberately: three imperatives in verse 1, the same relative clause opening verses 8 and 10, and a fixed four word pattern for each idol in verses 16 and 17 that breaks on purpose in the last line.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements inside one frame. Verses 1 to 4 call for praise and give the reason, which is election, stated as something already done. Verses 5 to 14 are the evidence, and they run in one continuous sweep from weather to history without marking the join: clouds and lightning, then Egypt, then Sihon and Og, then the land handed over, then the name that outlasts all of it. Verses 15 to 18 turn to the idols and argue by inventory, granting each part of the body and removing its function. Verses 19 to 21 pick the imperatives back up and hand them out by group. The frame is the word at הַלְלוּ (hal-lu, v.1) and again at יָהּ (yah, v.21), so the psalm ends by saying its own first word. Notice what the argument in verses 15 to 18 actually is: not that the idols are weak but that they are inert, and that the people who made them are drifting toward the same condition.

רמז · Remez: allusion

This psalm is a mosaic, and reading it next to its sources is the fastest way into it. Verse 7 appears almost word for word in Jeremiah 10:13 and again at Jeremiah 51:16. Verse 13 reuses the pairing of name and memorial from Exodus 3:15, so זִכְרְךָ (zich-re-cha, v.13) is not a synonym for name but the second half of a fixed phrase. Verse 14 is Deuteronomy 32:36 verbatim. Verses 15 to 18 shorten Psalm 115:4 to 8, with one substitution: Psalm 115 says their idols, this one says the idols of the nations. Verses 19 and 20 take the roll call of Psalm 115:9 to 11, which is also used in Psalm 118, and insert הַלֵּוִי (ha-le-vi, v.20), the house of Levi, which the other lists do not have. Which text borrowed from which is disputed. Two smaller points for a reader with the Hebrew in front of them: נְשִׂאִים (ne-si-im, v.7) comes from the root meaning to lift, so the clouds are literally the lifted things, and the lamed on לְסִיחוֹן (le-si-chon, v.11) marks the object of the killing rather than direction, which is why the English has no preposition there. And אֵין (ein, v.17) with יֶשׁ (yesh, v.17) is a doubled construction, there is not there is, an emphatic that no smooth translation preserves.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Two readings the text sets up on its own. First, the list at the end. Israel, Aaron, Levi are all defined by birth, and then the fourth group is defined by fear of heaven and by nothing else, so the sequence moves from lineage to disposition and closes on the category that anyone can enter. That the widening is deliberate is visible in the structure, since Levi had to be inserted into an existing three part list to make room for it. Second, עֹשֵׂיהֶם (o-sei-hem, v.18), their makers. Read strictly the verse says that the craftsmen come to resemble their product, and the second clause extends the same fate to anyone who merely trusts it, which makes the psalm's target the worshipper rather than the statue. Taken together with verse 17, where the failure of the idol is that no breath comes out of its mouth, the passage measures gods by whether anything speaks.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Breath is the hinge of the mystical reading. The idol has a mouth and no wind in it, while the God of this psalm keeps wind in storehouses and lets it out, and the human being who says these words is doing with breath the one thing the idol cannot do. The praise is therefore not decoration on top of the theology but the demonstration of it: a mouth that produces speech is the evidence being offered. Alongside that, לִסְגֻלָּתוֹ (lis-gu-la-to, v.4) names Israel as a set apart possession, a term used for treasure kept back from the general stock, and the kabbalistic reading takes it as a description of a portion held in reserve rather than a reward for merit. The psalm then ends by sending the blessing back up from Zion, which is the standard image of a circuit: what descends as rain and history returns as speech.
