Tehilim 147: He heals the broken in heart

תְּהִלִּים קמ״ז · הַלְלוּ יָהּ
HomePs 147
One of the six psalms said every morning in the opening section of the service, where the last five of the book are recited in order. It is built as three stanzas, each opening with a fresh call to praise at verses 1, 7 and 12, and each running the same circuit: something enormous, then something small and particular. Stars get counted and broken hearts get bandaged in consecutive verses. For a learner the psalm is a gallery of participles, the form that names God by what He is doing rather than by what He did, and almost every line of the first stanza begins with one.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three stanzas, each starting with an imperative to praise: הַלְלוּ (hal-lu, v.1) at the head, עֱנוּ (e-nu, v.7) at verse 7, שַׁבְּחִי (shab-chi, v.12) at verse 12. Each stanza then alternates scales without warning. Stanza one puts rebuilding a city, bandaging wounds and naming stars in three consecutive verses. Stanza two goes from cloud formation to ravens to the uselessness of cavalry. Stanza three narrows to one city and ends by saying that what she has been given was given to nobody else. Read straight through, the psalm never argues that the large and the small are connected. It simply refuses to separate them, line after line, until the refusal is the point.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The word for number in מִסְפָּר (mis-par, v.4) comes back in מִסְפָּר (mis-par, v.5) and turns over: the stars have one, God's understanding does not. That pair is the tightest piece of wordplay in the psalm and it survives translation badly. The verb in עֱנוּ (e-nu, v.7) is the ordinary root for answering, used here for song, which is why some renderings give sing responsively rather than simply sing. There is also a division worth knowing: in the Greek and Latin traditions this is two psalms, the break falling exactly at שַׁבְּחִי (shab-chi, v.12), where the change to feminine singular imperatives and the shift from participles to completed verbs both happen. The Hebrew tradition keeps it as one, and the two numbering systems part company here.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The classic reading of this psalm hangs on the seam between verses 3 and 4. The one who counts the stars and gives each a name is the one who bandages the broken hearted, and the order matters: the wounds come first. Read that way, the astronomy is not there to impress but to establish that attention at that scale is possible, so that attention to one person is not a smaller claim. The end of the psalm has been read as a matching pair. Rain and bread in verse 8 and verse 9 go to everyone, animals included; the statutes of verse 19 go to one people. What is universal is subsistence, what is particular is instruction, and the psalm is careful not to confuse which is which.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the kabbalistic vocabulary cold and contraction belong to the side of judgment and warmth and flow to the side of kindness, which makes verses 16 to 18 a compressed account of how a severe state resolves. The frost of כַּצָּמֶר (ka-tza-mer, v.16) is scattered, the cold is unanswerable, and then a word is sent and everything melts and moves in וְיַמְסֵם (ve-yam-sem, v.18). Nothing is removed by force. A word arrives and the same substance that was locked becomes water. The last verse then reads as a statement about the vessel rather than the gift: גּוֹי (goi, v.20) says the nations were not given this, and the reading takes that to mean not that they were denied something but that the instruction requires a form prepared to receive it.
