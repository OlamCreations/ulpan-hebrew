Tehilim 62: Only for God is my soul still

תְּהִלִּים ס״ב · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 62
Thirteen verses built on one small word. Six of them open with ach, which can restrict or dismiss: only God, but also nothing but a breath. The psalm uses both senses, and a learner who follows that single particle through the poem has followed the argument. Two other things to watch. The refrain of verses 3 and 7 is repeated almost exactly, with one word dropped the second time, and the psalm ends stronger than it began without anything in the situation having changed. And the word for stillness appears twice, in verse 2 as a noun and in verse 6 as a command, which is the difference between having quiet and having to choose it. The heading names Jeduthun, one of the temple musicians listed in Chronicles.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm alternates between audiences without announcing it. Verses 2, 3, 6 and 7 face inward, the speaker talking about God or talking to his own soul. Verse 4 faces the attackers, once, and never again. Verses 9 to 11 face a crowd and issue instructions. Verses 12 and 13 face God. The structural fact worth noticing is that the refrain is not identical the second time: רַבָּה (ra-ba, v.3), the word for greatly, is there in verse 3 and gone in verse 7, so the claim ends stronger than it started. Nothing in the situation has changed between the two. The change is in him.

רמז · Remez: allusion

אַךְ (ach, v.2) is the hinge of the whole psalm and it is one syllable. Ach opens verses 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 and 10, and it means only in the sense of nothing but. Aimed at God it concentrates; aimed at people it deflates; the poem gets its shape from letting one word do both. דּוּמִיָּה (du-mi-ya, v.2) and דּוֹמִּי (do-mi, v.6) are the same root, first a noun for stillness and then an imperative, be still. תְּהוֹתְתוּ (te-hot-tu, v.4) is a hapax whose root is not agreed on, which is why English versions offer set upon, rush at, and shout at for the same word: the assault is clear from context, the verb is not. בְּפִיו (be-fiv, v.5) keeps a singular suffix, with his mouth, between plural verbs on both sides, and most translations quietly regularize it to their mouths. הֶבֶל (he-vel, v.10) is hevel, the vapour of Ecclesiastes, and תֶּהְבָּלוּ (teh-ba-lu, v.11) is a verb from that same root, so the warning is literally not to turn into vapour.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The counting line of verse 12, one spoken and two heard, invites the question of what the second thing was, and the text answers it on the page: strength belongs to God in verse 12, kindness belongs to the Lord in verse 13, two attributes carried out of one utterance. A traditional reading hears Sinai behind the formula, one voice speaking and the hearers receiving more than one commandment directly from it. That reading is old and it is not the only one. Either way the argument comes out the same: what sounds like two competing claims about God, that he is strong and that he is kind, came from a single mouth and are not in tension.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Stillness here is not the absence of speech. Verse 9 tells the same people to pour their heart out in front of him, and שִׁפְכוּ (shif-chu, v.9) is a violent verb, the one used for spilling liquid. The psalm asks for both, an interior that is quiet and an interior that is emptied, and in the mystical idiom that pair is the standard description of a vessel: it holds by being still and it receives by being empty. The scales of verse 10 are the same image seen from the other side. What has no weight of its own cannot hold anything down, and the only thing in the psalm given real weight is חָסֶד (cha-sed, v.13), the kindness that belongs to the Lord and is paid out by measure.
