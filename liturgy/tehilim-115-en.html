Tehilim 115: Not to us, but to Your name

תְּהִלִּים קט״ו · לֹא לָנוּ
HomePs 115
Eighteen verses, and the sharpest mockery of idols in the book. It belongs to Hallel, said on festivals and at the seder after the meal, and it carries a seam a learner can see: in the shortened Hallel of Rosh Chodesh and the last days of Pesach the first eleven verses are left out and the recitation begins at verse 12. Watch the pronouns. The nations say their God, the psalm says our God, and four verses of body parts that do nothing end by turning the whole list back onto the people who made them.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm answers a taunt. Verse 2 quotes the nations asking where this God is, and the rest is the reply in three moves. First, placement: He is in the heavens, which is why He is not on a shelf, and being unbound He does whatever He wants. Second, the counter-example: an idol has every organ a person has and not one of them works, and כְּמוֹהֶם (kmo-hem, v.8) turns the list back on its owners so that the makers inherit the deadness they built. Third, instruction: three groups are told to trust, three blessings come back down, and the psalm ends by dividing the two domains in וְהָאָרֶץ (ve-ha-a-retz, v.16), heaven kept and earth handed over.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The idol section is built like a grammar exercise. Each line names an organ, assigns it to them, then cancels it with the same negative, and two of the cancelling verbs, יְרִיחוּן (ye-ri-chun, v.6) and יְמִישׁוּן (ye-mi-shun, v.7), end in an extra nun that is an archaic poetic ending rather than a change of meaning. The noun for idols, עֲצַבֵּיהֶם (a-tza-bei-hem, v.4), is built on a root that elsewhere yields the words for pain and for toil, so the thing made by hands is named for the labour and the grief in it. Note also what breaks the pattern: verse 7 drops the phrase they have, and its final clause abandons the limbs to return to the throat, which puts the catalogue back where it started, at speech.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The liturgy reads this psalm in two pieces. In the shortened Hallel of Rosh Chodesh and the last days of Pesach the first eleven verses are omitted and the recitation picks up at verse 12, זְכָרָנוּ (ze-cha-ra-nu, v.12), exactly where the people stop speaking and the blessing starts coming back down. What gets cut is the mockery and the calls to trust; what remains is the blessing. וְהָאָרֶץ (ve-ha-a-retz, v.16) carries a second reading with a long life: since the earth was handed to human beings, what happens on it is charged to them, and the verse is used for both the duty to cultivate and the duty not to ruin. דוּמָה (du-ma, v.17) names the grave by its silence, and the common reading takes praise as work available only to the living, which turns the final verse into an argument for staying alive and useful rather than a statement about what follows death.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The doubled refusal at לֹא (lo, v.1), not to us and not to us, reads as an emptying: a vessel that claims nothing can hold what is poured into it, and glory that stops at the name never lodges in the one who says it. The idol passage describes the opposite condition, a form fully finished and completely closed, with openings that do not open. Between the two stands the promise of כְּמוֹהֶם (kmo-hem, v.8), that resemblance follows trust, which in this reading is a law about attention rather than a curse: whatever a person keeps facing becomes the shape that person takes. The close at וַאֲנַחְנוּ (va-a-nach-nu, v.18) sets the alternative in time, from now and with no end, the only unbounded quantity the psalm allows itself.
