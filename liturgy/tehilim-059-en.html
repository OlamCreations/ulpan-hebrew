Tehilim 59: Deliver me from my enemies

תְּהִלִּים נ״ט · לַמְנַצֵּחַ אַל
HomePs 59
An ambush prayer in eighteen verses. The heading sets it on the night Saul posted men around David's house, and the psalm keeps that setting all the way through: enemies who work after dark, a speaker waiting for morning. It runs in two panels, each closed by Selah at סֶלָה (se-la, v.6) and סֶלָה (se-la, v.14), and each panel carries the same two refrains, the enemies who come back at evening and growl like a dog, and God who is a high fortress. One thing to watch for a learner: the root of תְּשַׂגְּבֵנִי (te-sag-ve-ni, v.2), to set someone up out of reach, is the same root as the noun in מִשְׂגַּבִּי (mis-ga-bi, v.10), so the verb of the opening plea and the noun of the closing refrain are the same idea in two shapes. Note also that Hebrew counts the heading as verse 1, so every verse number here is one ahead of most English Bibles.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two panels of roughly equal size, each ending in Selah. Panel one runs to verse 6 and is mostly petition; panel two runs to verse 14 and is mostly sentence. Each panel is followed by the same two-part refrain, the enemies at evening in verses 7 and 15, and God as a high fortress in verses 10 and 18. Read the refrains next to each other and the psalm's shape is obvious: the situation outside the house does not change between them, and the speaker does. What is asked for is never the destruction of the siege but relocation above it, which is why the vocabulary of height carries the argument from תְּשַׂגְּבֵנִי (te-sag-ve-ni, v.2) at the start to מִשְׂגַּבִּי (mis-ga-bi, v.18) at the end.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The crux is the first word of verse 10, עֻזּוֹ (u-zo, v.10), which the Masoretic text vocalises as his strength while the matching line at עֻזִּי (u-zi, v.18) has my strength. The two verses are otherwise a pair, so many translations read the first as my strength too and treat the difference as a copying slip; others keep it and take it as a reference to the enemy's strength, which the speaker answers by watching for God. The reading is disputed and both are defensible from the consonants. Second thing to notice: the noun in מִשְׂגַּבִּי (mis-ga-bi, v.10) and the verb in תְּשַׂגְּבֵנִי (te-sag-ve-ni, v.2) share a root meaning to be too high to reach, so a fortress in this psalm is not a wall you hide behind but an elevation. Third, the fierce ones of עַזִּים (a-zim, v.4) is a word for insolence and force together, and it is the same adjective used of a strong animal, which sets up the dog of כַכָּלֶב (cha-ka-lev, v.7) before the dog arrives.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 12 has drawn the most attention: do not kill them, so that my people will not forget. The line has long been read as asking for a living monument rather than a clean victory, on the reasoning that a defeated enemy still walking around teaches the lesson that a buried one cannot. Read this way the psalm asks for something harder than rescue, namely a permanent visible reminder, and the tension with verse 14, which asks that they be finished off, is left standing rather than smoothed away. The heading points to the night in the book of Samuel when Saul's men surrounded the house, and the dogs of verses 7 and 15 have often been taken as those watchers rather than as animals, which turns the refrain into a description of a specific stakeout instead of a general image of menace.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm is organised on an axis of evening against morning. The enemies belong entirely to the night: they return at evening in verses 7 and 15, they roam to eat and lodge out until dawn in verse 16, and their weapon is speech spoken in the dark on the assumption that no one hears. The speaker owns exactly one time of day, the לַבֹּקֶר (la-bo-ker, v.17) of verse 17, and what he sings there is loyalty. In the mystical reading the night is the state of concealment in which judgment operates unopposed and the morning is the return of loyal love, so the psalm is not asking for the night to be shortened but for the singer to be held above it until it turns. The high fortress is then a description of consciousness, not of masonry: the same house, the same watchers outside it, and a person who has been raised out of their reach without being moved.
