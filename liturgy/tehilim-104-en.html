Tehilim 104: Bless the LORD, my soul

תְּהִלִּים ק״ד · בָּרְכִי נַפְשִׁי
HomePs 104
Thirty five verses, the longest continuous description of the created world in the book, and the psalm said on Rosh Chodesh in most rites. Two features make it a good page to learn on. The first is the participle used as a divine title: whoever wraps, whoever stretches, whoever lays the beams, whoever makes, whoever sends, whoever looks. Six verses in a row can go by without a finite verb. The second is the archaic ending -un, hung on more than a dozen verbs here, which changes no meaning and marks only that this is old poetry. The psalm also refuses to settle on a person, moving from He to You and back, sometimes inside one verse. Its first four words return as the last four before the closing halleluyah, which is the first halleluyah in the book, so the whole tour of the world sits inside one command the speaker gives to himself.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A tour of the world in the order of Genesis 1, with one command wrapped around it. Verses 1 to 4 dress God and pitch the sky; 5 to 9 set the earth and push the water back behind a boundary; 10 to 18 let the same water out again and follow it to everything that drinks it; 19 to 23 run a night and a day; 24 stops to exclaim; 25 and 26 handle the sea; 27 to 30 turn the catalogue into dependence, where food, breath and death all come from the same hand; 31 to 35 respond. The frame is exact: בָּרְכִי (bar-chi, v.1) and נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.1) return as בָּרְכִי (bar-chi, v.35) and נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.35), so everything between is what the soul is being told to bless. Two habits are worth watching while reading. The psalm names participles rather than actions, so God is identified by what He is always doing, and it slides between He and You without warning, as at verse 13, where the upper chambers are His and the works in the same verse are Yours.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 4 is ambiguous in the Hebrew and the ambiguity is not resolvable from word order alone: עֹשֶׂה (o-se, v.4) with מַלְאָכָיו (mal-a-chav, v.4) and רוּחוֹת (ru-chot, v.4) can mean who makes winds His messengers, or who makes His messengers into winds. The first keeps the verse in the weather of verses 2 and 3, the second makes it a line about angels, and both readings are old. Verse 8 has the same problem with subjects: יַעֲלוּ (ya-a-lu, v.8) and יֵרְדוּ (yer-du, v.8) can describe mountains rising and valleys sinking as the land emerges, or the water of verse 7 running up and down the terrain as it flees. In מִשָּׁמֶן (mi-sha-men, v.15) the prefix is the ambiguous mem, so the face shines with oil or shines more than oil. וּרְחַב (ur-chav, v.25) with יָדָיִם (ya-da-yim, v.25) is literally broad of hands, an idiom for wide reaching, and יִשְׁבְּרוּ (yish-bru, v.11) is literally to break, used here of breaking a thirst. Then there is the ending -un on more than a dozen verbs, from יְנוּסוּן (ye-nu-sun, v.7) to יִבָּרֵאוּן (yi-ba-re-un, v.30): it adds nothing to the meaning and is one of the clearest markers in the book that a text is deliberately archaic.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The Talmud at Berakhot 10a reports the best known reading of the last verse. Rabbi Meir prayed for the death of the troublemakers in his neighbourhood, and Beruriah, his wife, pointed at חַטָּאִים (cha-ta-im, v.35): the consonants say chata'im, which can be read as sins rather than as sinners, and the next clause then follows on its own, because once sins are finished the wicked are no more. Rabbi Meir prayed for their repentance instead. On לְשַׂחֶק (le-sa-chek, v.26) the Talmud at Avodah Zarah 3b reads the playing as God's own, and says He spends part of each day playing with Leviathan, which turns the verse from a note on sea life into a statement that creation includes leisure. The custom of saying this psalm on Rosh Chodesh is usually explained from two lines inside it, יָרֵחַ (ya-re-ach, v.19), the moon made for appointed times, and וּתְחַדֵּשׁ (ut-cha-desh, v.30), the renewal of the face of the ground, so the monthly renewal of the moon and the renewal of the world are read as one subject.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The garment of עֹטֶה (o-te, v.2) and אוֹר (or, v.2) is the standard mystical image for a light that hides what it reveals: what can be seen of God is exactly the covering, and the world is the outside of the garment. The midrashic tradition on the first day of creation answers the question of where that light came from by pointing at this verse. The deeper move is in verses 29 and 30, which use one word, ruach, for wind, breath and spirit, and describe creatures as created when it is sent and expiring when it is gathered back. Read that way the psalm is not about an event but about a continuous supply: nothing here holds itself in existence for a moment, and the difference between a living animal and dust is which direction the breath is moving. לְשַׂחֶק (le-sa-chek, v.26) belongs to the same reading. A world made for play is a world not made from need, which is the usual mystical answer to why anything was created at all.
