Tehilim 90: Teach us to number our days

תְּהִלִּים צ׳ · תְּפִלָּה לְמֹשֶׁה
HomePs 90
The only psalm whose heading names Moses, and the one that opens the fourth book of the Psalter. It is the shortest serious statement in the Bible about how long a human life is and what that fact should do to a person: seventy years, eighty with strength, against a God for whom a thousand years is last night. Verse 17 begins the Saturday night service in the Ashkenazi rite, which is the moment the working week starts again. For a learner this is the counterpart of Psalm 51: almost everything here is first person plural, so the suffix to watch is the one meaning us and our, and it lands on days, years, iniquities, hands.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements. Verses 1 and 2 set the scale: God as dwelling place, and a time before mountains. Verses 3 to 6 put a human life inside that scale using a thousand years, a night watch, and grass that starts and finishes in one day. Verses 7 to 11 name the cause, which is anger, and connect it to iniquities set in plain light, ending with a question nobody can answer. Verses 12 to 17 are the response, and they are all imperatives: teach, return, satisfy, gladden, let be seen, establish. The pivot is לִמְנוֹת (lim-not, v.12), to number, which asks for arithmetic rather than for more time. Notice that nothing in the last movement asks for a longer life. The request is that a short one be filled and that its work stand.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm turns on a small number of hard words. דַּכָּא (da-ka, v.3) is read by most as crushed matter, dust or the point of being crushed, and the older English versions read it as contrition instead, which changes the verse from a description of death into a call to repent; the reading is disputed. תָּשֵׁב (ta-shev, v.3) and שׁוּבוּ (shu-vu, v.3) are the same root, so God turns man back and then tells man to turn back, and a translation has to choose whether that echo is deliberate. יַחֲלֹף (ya-cha-lof, v.5) means both to pass on and to sprout again, and both senses are used in the neighbouring verses, which is why one English version has grass that grows up and another has grass that vanishes. הֶגֶה (he-ge, v.9) is a murmur, a sigh, or something spoken under the breath; older translations give a tale that is told, modern ones a sigh, and the noun is rare enough that neither can be proved.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The heading assigns the psalm to Moses, and the traditional reading takes that seriously: a man who watched an entire generation die in the desert over forty years, addressing the God who set the term. Read that way שִׁבְעִים (shiv-im, v.10), seventy, is not a statistic but a verdict already carried out, and the plural throughout is the community rather than a poetic habit. The classical commentators connect verse 17 to the completion of the Tabernacle, where Moses blesses the people that the divine presence rest on the work of their hands. In the Ashkenazi rite that verse opens the Saturday night service, so the last words of the prayer of Moses are the first words said as the working week begins. Whether the psalm is by Moses or written in his voice is an old question and the text does not settle it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

מָעוֹן (ma-on, v.1), dwelling place, is also the name a talmudic tradition gives to one of the seven heavens, and the mystical reading holds the two senses together: the shelter human generations live in is not a place inside the world but one of its upper stories, which is how a house can be older than the mountains. Verse 4 is the seed of the reading that measures the world in divine days, a thousand years to each, and builds a cosmic week out of them; that scheme is a later construction and the verse itself only says that a thousand years look brief from where God stands. נֹעַם (no-am, v.17), pleasantness, is read as the light that rests on finished work and makes it permanent, which is why the request in the final line is not for success but for establishment. Counting days, in this reading, is how a creature bounded by time gets a share in what is not.
