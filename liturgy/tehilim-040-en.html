Tehilim 40: I waited for the LORD

תְּהִלִּים מ׳ · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד
HomePs 40
Two psalms sharing one page, and the seam is visible. Verses 2 to 11 give thanks for a rescue that has already happened; verses 13 to 18 beg for a rescue that has not, and that second half turns up again almost word for word as Psalm 70. For a learner the opening line is the clearest example in the book of the Hebrew intensifier, an infinitive laid on the finite verb of the same root. The line everything turns on is verse 7: what God wants is not the offering.

The last verse holds both halves of the psalm at once: poor and needy, and already rescued. The final word asks God not to be late, which leaves the psalm deliberately unfinished.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two psalms sharing a page. Verses 2 to 11 are thanksgiving, past tense throughout: a pit, a rescue, a new song, a public telling. Verses 13 to 18 are lament in the present, the speaker surrounded again and asking for the help he has just finished describing as received. Verse 12 is the joint, and it faces both ways, taking the kindness and truth of the thanksgiving and asking them to work as protection from here on. The most direct reading is chronological: the man telling the rescue story is in trouble again while telling it, and the psalm never resolves the tension that creates.

רמז · Remez: allusion

קַוֺּה (ka-o, v.2) and קִוִּיתִי (ki-vi-ti, v.2) are the same root twice, an infinitive laid on a finite verb, which is how biblical Hebrew intensifies: not waited, but waited and waited. מִבּוֹר (mi-bor, v.3) and סֶלַע (se-la, v.3), the pit and the rock, are set as opposites inside one line. The crux is אׇזְנַיִם (oz-na-yim, v.7) with כָּרִיתָ (ka-ri-ta, v.7), ears and a verb meaning to dig or bore. Read one way the ears were opened so they could hear, which fits verses 8 and 9 exactly. Read another way it is a mark cut into the ear, and the picture behind it is the servant of Exodus 21 who chooses to stay, though the verb used there is a different one. The Greek translation of the psalm renders the clause with a different noun altogether, which is why translations of this line diverge so widely.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 7 is the line that had to be handled, because on its face it says God does not want the offerings the Torah commands. The reading that answers it does not treat the verse as abolishing sacrifice but as ranking it: what is wanted first is the ear that hears and the will that follows, and an offering brought without those is the thing being refused. The same move is made with Samuel's line to Saul that obeying is better than sacrifice, and with the complaints in Isaiah 1 and Hosea 6. Verse 8 then reads as the person himself arriving where the offering used to go, which is why the scroll is mentioned in the same breath: he comes carrying a text rather than an animal.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The geography of the psalm is a vertical line: a pit of noise and mud at the bottom, rock underfoot, a mouth given a song, an assembly told. In the mystical idiom that is the standard shape of a descent that turns out to have been for the sake of the ascent, and the noise of the pit is not incidental, since what is drawn up carries something of where it was. The pairing of חַסְדְּךָ (chas-de-cha, v.11) and וַאֲמִתְּךָ (va-a-mit-cha, v.11), kindness and truth, is read as two attributes that must travel together, one giving without measure and one holding the limit, and the plea of verse 12 is that they arrive as a pair rather than one without the other.
