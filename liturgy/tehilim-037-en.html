Tehilim 37: Do not burn with anger at evildoers

תְּהִלִּים ל״ז · לְדָוִד אַל
HomePs 37
Forty verses of advice for one problem: the wicked are doing well and you are not. This is a wisdom poem, closer to Proverbs than to a prayer, and it argues rather than pleads. It is built as an alphabetical acrostic, but not a tidy one: most letters open a two-verse strophe, so the letter changes roughly every other verse, while dalet at verse 7, kaf at verse 20 and qof at verse 34 get one verse each, samekh at verse 27 stretches over three, and no verse in the Masoretic text begins with ayin. A learner should track one phrase: inheriting the land, which comes back at verses 9, 11, 22, 29 and 34 and is the psalm's answer each time. Verse 25 is the one most people know, because it is said at the table in the Grace after Meals.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Someone is watching a dishonest man do well and it is eating him. The psalm answers with two moves, repeated in cycles. The first is a prohibition against the feeling itself, stated three times with the same verb, at verses 1, 7 and 8. The second is a time argument: what looks permanent is short, so what looks like an unjust distribution is really an unfinished one. Around this the poem builds a refrain, inheriting the land, at verses 9, 11, 22, 29 and 34, and each occurrence gives the promise to a different subject, those who wait, the humble, those God blesses, the righteous, and finally you. Nothing here is a prayer. There is no request, no complaint addressed upward, and God is spoken about rather than to for all forty verses. It is an older person giving advice, which is exactly how verse 25 presents it.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The acrostic is the frame and it is worth counting. Each Hebrew letter opens a strophe of two verses, so the letter changes every second verse, but four places break the pattern: dalet gets verse 7 alone, kaf gets verse 20 alone, qof gets verse 34 alone, samekh runs across verses 27 to 29, and no verse in the Masoretic text opens with ayin. The Greek version has an extra clause in verse 28 about wrongdoers being destroyed, and many modern editors retrovert that to a lost Hebrew line beginning with ayin, which would restore the sequence; others take the letter as simply missing. Both are conjectures and the printed Hebrew supports neither. A second crux sits in כָּרִים (ka-rim, v.20), a word that can be read as lambs or as meadows: the older translations give the fat of lambs going up on an altar, the newer ones the splendour of pasture that dries out. The verb of גּוֹל (gol, v.5) is worth its own note, because it means to roll a weight, not to entrust a matter.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 25 is recited in the Grace after Meals, and it is the verse of this psalm people have always pushed back on, because plenty of righteous people have in fact gone hungry. The traditional readings do not deny that. One takes the line as testimony, David reporting the limits of one lifetime rather than legislating for all of them. Another reads it forward into verse 26 and makes it conditional: the children who do not beg are the children of a man who spent his own days lending. Both readings agree that a verse about not having seen something is not a verse about it being impossible. The refrain about inheriting the land was heard by later readers as more than a property claim, since וַעֲנָוִים (va-a-na-vim, v.11), the humble, are promised the same land that armies were fighting over, and the psalm's own logic is that possession follows patience rather than force.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading works from the verbs of transfer. In גּוֹל (gol, v.5) you roll your way off yourself, and the sentence that follows has no object at all: He will act, and what He will do is not specified, because the point of the handover is that you stop holding the outcome. In וּרְעֵה (ur-e, v.3) you are told to graze on faithfulness, which makes trust a food rather than an attitude, something consumed daily and never stored. Against this the wicked in verse 20 are the reverse image, substance turning into smoke, mass leaving without being handed to anyone. The land in the refrain is then read as the settled state itself, the condition of being placed rather than the acreage, and inheriting it is what happens to a person who has stopped burning.
