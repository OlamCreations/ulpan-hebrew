Tehilim 143: LORD, hear my prayer

תְּהִלִּים קמ״ג · מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד
HomePs 143
Twelve verses, one of the eight psalms of David in the closing group that runs from 138 to 145, and the plainest lament in that group. The shape is easy to hold: two verses of request, four of description, six that are almost nothing but imperatives. Two words carry it. The speaker's own spirit appears twice and fails both times, in verses 4 and 7, and then in verse 10 he asks for a different spirit, God's. And the word servant opens the argument in verse 2 and closes it in verse 12, so everything asked for in between rests on that and on nothing else.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements. Verses 1 and 2 ask to be heard and then rule out one way of being answered. Verses 3 to 6 describe the situation, moving from what an enemy did, to what happened inside the speaker, to one image of dry ground. Verses 7 to 12 are a run of ten or so imperatives and jussives with no narrative between them. The word soul appears five times, at נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.3), נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.6), נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.8), נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.11) and נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.12), and it is the object of nearly everything that happens: pursued, thirsty, lifted, brought out, harassed. The two attributes named at בֶּאֱמֻנָתְךָ (be-e-mu-nat-cha, v.1) and בְּצִדְקָתֶךָ (be-tzid-ka-te-cha, v.1) return at בְּצִדְקָתְךָ (be-tzid-kat-cha, v.11) and, as kindness, at חַסְדֶּךָ (chas-de-cha, v.8) and וּבְחַסְדְּךָ (uv-chas-de-cha, v.12). Nothing in the psalm reports that any of it worked.

רמז · Remez: allusion

כִסִּתִי (chi-si-ti, v.9) is the crux. The verb is from the root for covering, and the clause reads to You I have covered, with no object at all. It has been read as I have hidden myself with You, taking the speaker as the object; as I have covered my soul, supplying the noun from the neighbouring verses; and there is a long standing proposal that the original consonants held a different verb meaning to flee, which some ancient versions appear to reflect. The Masoretic text supports the first two, and it is the Masoretic text this page prints. A second point, at אֶת (et, v.2): the plain object marker is doing the work of the preposition with, so the verse is about entering judgment alongside someone rather than about judging them. Third, מִישׁוֹר (mi-shor, v.10) is a level plain, from the same root as the words for upright and straight, so being led on level ground is at once a request for easy walking and for a straight life. Fourth, the two thinking verbs of verse 5 are not silent ones: הָגִיתִי (ha-gi-ti, v.5) is the verb behind the noun higayon and denotes a low murmur, and אֲשׂוֹחֵחַ (a-so-che-ach, v.5) is a rare verb closer to talking something over aloud than to reflection.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 2 is the verse this psalm is remembered for. It does not claim the speaker is innocent and it does not concede that he is guilty. It says the category itself does not survive contact with God, and it says so about every living thing rather than about him, which is why the verse is quoted far outside its own psalm. One traditional line joins it to Ecclesiastes 7:20, that there is no righteous man on earth who does good and never sins, and treats the pair as the reason prayer asks for kindness instead of for a verdict. A second line works on what the psalm does with the word righteousness. At verse 2 righteousness is the thing nobody has; at verses 1 and 11 God's righteousness is the thing that rescues. The same root that would convict in a court is what the speaker appeals to for release, and that reversal is the real argument of the psalm. In Christian tradition this is the last of the seven penitential psalms, a grouping formed in late antiquity that has no Jewish counterpart.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read the three occurrences of the word spirit in order and the psalm has a plot its surface does not show. At רוּחִי (ru-chi, v.4) the speaker's own spirit wraps itself over him, a verb used elsewhere of a garment and of fainting, so the breath that animates him becomes a covering that smothers him. At רוּחִי (ru-chi, v.7) the same word is finished, worn out, using the verb that Psalm 84 uses for a soul spent with longing. Then at רוּחֲךָ (ru-cha-cha, v.10) he stops asking anything of his own spirit and asks for God's, and the adjective attached to it, good, is the only positive word in the last six verses. On the mystical reading the whole psalm is that exchange: what a person carries runs out, and what is asked for at the end is not more of the same but a different source. The dry land of כְּאֶרֶץ (ke-e-retz, v.6) works the same way. Land cannot water itself, and the image is chosen precisely because it removes the speaker's own agency from the picture.
