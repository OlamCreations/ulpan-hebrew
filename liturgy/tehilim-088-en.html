Tehilim 88: LORD, God of my salvation

תְּהִלִּים פ״ח · שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 88
Nineteen verses of lament that never turns. The psalm opens in the dark and its last word is darkness, and unlike every other lament in the book it offers no thanks, no vow and no rescue at the end. It is the best page here for the second person perfect, because almost every complaint is a verb ending in -ta, You have: You put me, You have removed, You have afflicted. The superscription carries two attributions and two musical terms nobody can now translate with confidence. Read it slowly and let it stay unresolved, because that is what it is.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three cycles, each starting with an address to God and sliding back down. Verses 2 to 8 move from God of my salvation to the deeps; verses 9 to 13 move from isolation to the land of forgetting; verses 14 to 19 move from a prayer that arrives in the morning to a last word of darkness. Count the names for the underworld as you go, because the psalm collects them: Sheol, the pit, the grave, the depths, Abaddon, the dark, the land of forgetting. Notice too that the requests stop at verse 3. After that the psalm asks nothing and only reports, which is why it feels different from every other lament in the book.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three cruxes worth knowing. אָפוּנָה (a-fu-na, v.16), the last word of verse 16, occurs nowhere else in the Bible; translations render it distracted, numb, or helpless, and the honest position is that it is unknown. מָחֲלַת (ma-cha-lat, v.1) and לְעַנּוֹת (le-a-not, v.1) in the superscription are performance directions of contested meaning, variously read as an instrument, a melody name, or a manner of singing, and no reading is secure. חׇפְשִׁי (chof-shi, v.6), free among the dead, is a phrase that reads as a benefit and functions as the opposite: the freedom in question is being released from God's memory, which the verse then restates as being cut off from His hand. רְפָאִים (re-fa-im, v.11), the shades, is the standard biblical word for the dead as a class, and here they are asked whether they could rise and give thanks.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

What the tradition did with this psalm is mostly that it kept it. It sits in the Korahite collection unsoftened, and the doxology that follows the next psalm belongs to the closing of the third book of the Psalter rather than to any resolution of these nineteen verses. One well established rabbinic reading turns חׇפְשִׁי (chof-shi, v.6) inside out: free among the dead becomes the source of the idea that the dead are released from the commandments, so that a phrase written as abandonment is heard as exemption. Read that way, the psalm's worst line becomes the tradition's technical term for what death changes, which is a fair example of how a lament is absorbed without being answered.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm can be read as a description of concealment rather than of punishment. Verse 15 names it exactly: the face is hidden, not withdrawn, and everything else in the psalm follows from a presence that is entirely there and entirely unavailable. In the kabbalistic vocabulary this is the state in which the light is present but the vessel receives nothing, and the questions of verses 11 to 13 are then not rhetorical but structural: they ask whether the flow reaches the place where nothing is remembered. The psalm gives no answer, and מַחְשָׁךְ (mach-shach, v.19), its last word, leaves the reader inside the condition rather than outside describing it.
