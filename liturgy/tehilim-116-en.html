Tehilim 116: I love that the LORD hears my voice

תְּהִלִּים קט״ז · אָהַבְתִּי כִּי
HomePs 116
Nineteen verses of one person's thanksgiving after a near death, and one of the six psalms of Hallel (113 to 118), said on festivals, on Rosh Chodesh, and at the seder, where the second part of Hallel comes after the meal. Two things to watch. The psalm keeps returning to one refrain, calling on the name of the LORD, at verses 4, 13 and 17, and it uses that refrain to turn a rescue into an obligation. And the grammar is unusual in places: second person feminine endings in -chi where standard Hebrew has -ch, and a suffix on תַּגְמוּלוֹהִי (tag-mu-lo-hi, v.12) that looks Aramaic, which is part of why the date of the psalm is argued over.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two halves with a seam at verse 10. Verses 1 to 9 tell what happened: cords, straits, a call, a rescue, and a modest future in which the speaker simply keeps walking. Verses 10 to 19 ask what is owed and pay it. Verse 12 is the pivot question and verses 13, 14 and 17 are its three answers, a lifted cup, a vow settled in public, and a thanksgiving offering. The refrain about calling on the name of the LORD ties the halves together: it is a cry for help at verse 4 and a liturgical act at verses 13 and 17, the same words doing opposite work. Note also that the psalm never says what the danger was. It gives the shape of the trouble (אֲפָפוּנִי (a-fa-fu-ni, v.3) surrounded, חֶבְלֵי (chev-lei, v.3) cords) and never the cause, which is part of why anyone can say it.

רמז · Remez: allusion

This is a psalm of strange forms. לִמְנוּחָיְכִי (lim-nu-chai-chi, v.7) and עָלָיְכִי (a-lai-chi, v.7) carry a second person feminine ending in -chi where standard biblical Hebrew has -ch, and the same ending shows up on בְּתוֹכֵכִי (be-to-che-chi, v.19); תַּגְמוּלוֹהִי (tag-mu-lo-hi, v.12) takes a suffix of the type ordinarily found in Aramaic. These are usually read as marks of a late composition, though the dating is disputed and an archaic-dialect explanation has also been proposed. Verse 10 is the famous crux: the particle כִּי (ki, v.10) can be read as because, or that, or concessively as even when, and the Greek translation took it in the sense of therefore, giving I believed, therefore I spoke, which is a different sentence altogether. One more seam worth knowing: the Greek and Latin traditions split this psalm in two at verse 10, which is why the numbering of the psalms differs between Jewish and some Christian bibles through this stretch of the book.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Two readings that the text invites without needing a source attached. First, יָקָר (ya-kar, v.15), precious: if the word means valued, the verse consoles, and if it means costly, it says that God does not spend the lives of the faithful cheaply, which is why the same verse can be read as comfort at a funeral and as an argument for staying alive. The two readings are both current and the choice between them is disputed. Second, לְמוֹסֵרָי (le-mo-se-rai, v.16), my bonds, together with the double declaration of servanthood: a slave born in the household could not simply walk away, so the psalm has the speaker claim the status and then thank God for undoing the restraint that came with it. Being God's servant is presented as the freedom, not as the chain. And because verse 13 is said with a cup in hand at the seder, the lifted cup of the psalm and the cup on the table are heard as one act, whatever the derivation.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the mystical reading the psalm is addressed inward. שׁוּבִי (shu-vi, v.7) tells the soul to return, using the verb of repentance, and the rest it is sent back to is not a place the soul reaches but a state it originally had. The soul is spoken to in the feminine throughout, as the receiving side of the relation, and the cup of כּוֹס (kos, v.13) is the standard image for that side: a vessel whose whole worth is its capacity to be filled and then lifted. Read this way the answer to the question of verse 12 is deliberately paradoxical. What one gives back to the giver of everything is the act of receiving properly and in public, which is why יְהוֹשִׁיעַ (ye-ho-shi-a, v.6) saves and אֶתְהַלֵּךְ (et-ha-lech, v.9) walk are not two different endings but the same one, an ordinary life continued on borrowed time.
