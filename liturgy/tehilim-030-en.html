Tehilim 30: You turned my mourning into dancing

תְּהִלִּים ל׳ · מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר
HomePs 30
Thirteen verses of a man reporting his own rescue, said every morning at the opening of the psalms of praise and again through Chanukah. The heading calls it a song for the dedication of a house, and then the body never mentions a house: it is illness, a pit, a hidden face and a recovery. Grammatically it is the best place in the psalter to learn two suffixes at once, the -ta ending that means You did something and the -ni ending that means to me, because almost every verb here carries one or both.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The shape is a thanksgiving report. Verses 2 to 6 announce the rescue and call others to join; verses 7 to 11 go back and tell the story, complacency first, then the hidden face, then the prayer quoted word for word; verses 12 and 13 return to the present with the reversal accomplished. The illness is never named and the enemies never act, which keeps the psalm usable by anyone who has been pulled back from something. Watch the two forevers: לְעוֹלָם (le-o-lam, v.7) is the boast that failed, לְעוֹלָם (le-o-lam, v.13) is the promise that stands.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two hard words. In מִיׇּרְדִי (mi-yor-di, v.4) the consonants can be read as from my going down to the pit or as from among those going down to the pit, and translations still split on it: one has the speaker turned back at the edge, the other has him already down among the dead and hauled out. In לְהַרְרִי (le-har-ri, v.8) the noun for my mountain carries a prefix that will not sit comfortably in the sentence, so some read You established strength for my mountain and others You established my mountain as a stronghold. Neither reading changes the psalm, but the learner should know the line is rough in Hebrew and smooth only in translation.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The gap between the heading and the poem is where the interpretation happens. The house of חֲנֻכַּת (cha-nu-kat, v.1) has been read as the Temple that David prepared but did not build, as David's own palace, and as the site bought after the plague at the end of Second Samuel, where a personal deliverance and a future altar occupy the same ground. All three readings are old and none is decisive. Liturgy took the widest option: the psalm is said on Chanukah, treating dedication as rededication, and its daily recitation before the morning psalms lets an individual waking up borrow the vocabulary of a rebuilt sanctuary.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The hiding of the face in הִסְתַּרְתָּ (his-tar-ta, v.8) and פָנֶיךָ (fa-nei-cha, v.8) became a technical term in later Jewish thought for a concealment that is itself a mode of relation, not an absence. Read that way, verse 6 is not consolation about bad moods but a measurement: anger occupies a moment, favour occupies the duration, and the two are not symmetrical quantities. The turning verb of הָפַכְתָּ (ha-fach-ta, v.12) is then the key to the whole psalm. Mourning is not deleted and replaced with joy; it is rotated, the same substance seen from the other side, which is why the sackcloth is untied rather than torn off.
