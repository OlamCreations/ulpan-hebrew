Tehilim 63: My soul thirsts for You

תְּהִלִּים ס״ג · מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד
HomePs 63
Twelve verses written from a desert and remembering a sanctuary. The superscription puts David in the wilderness of Judah, and the psalm takes its images from exactly there: thirst, dry ground, night, and animals that feed on the dead. For a learner it drills one thing, the second person suffix -kha attached to a verb: I seek You, I have beheld You, I will bless You, I remember You, my lips praise You. Five different verbs, one ending. Watch also the word nefesh, soul or throat, which appears four times and changes state each time: it thirsts, it is satisfied, it clings, and at the end it is the thing the enemies are hunting.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm is one continuous address, and it travels through the body. It begins in the throat with thirst, צָמְאָה (tzam-a, v.2), moves out to the flesh, then to the lips and the mouth in verses 4 to 6, then to a bed at night in verse 7, then to a hand holding the speaker upright in verse 9. Only in verse 10 does anyone else enter, and the enemies are shown going down into the earth in the same movement in which the speaker is held. The final verse steps out of the first person for the king, and it is the psalm's only sentence about the public world.

רמז · Remez: allusion

אֲשַׁחֲרֶךָּ (a-sha-cha-re-ka, v.2) is the crux. Its root can be read as the ordinary verb for seeking, and it is also the root of the noun for dawn, so translators split between I seek You earnestly and I seek You at daybreak. Both are defensible from the consonants, and both suit a psalm that will name the night watches later. חֵלֶב (che-lev, v.6) is the fat of an animal, the portion burned on the altar, so the satisfaction of verse 6 is described with the vocabulary of an offering by a man with neither altar nor water. Verse 11 gathers the difficulties: יַגִּירֻהוּ (ya-gi-ru-hu, v.11) is rare enough that translations disagree over whether the sense is poured out, handed over or hurled, and שֻׁעָלִים (shu-a-lim, v.11) is the ordinary word for foxes, though most modern readings take it here as jackals, which are the scavengers a battlefield actually attracts.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The plural in בְּאַשְׁמֻרוֹת (be-ash-mu-rot, v.7), the night watches, drew a discussion of its own: the Talmud at Berakhot 3a preserves a dispute over whether the night divides into three watches or four. The superscription sends the reader back to the books of Samuel, but it does not say which flight is meant, and commentators split between David hiding from Saul and David leaving Jerusalem during Absalom's revolt. Either way verse 12 is read as the sentence of a man who holds a crown and has no throne, and the sanctuary of verse 3 as a memory rather than a building he can walk into, which makes the whole psalm a model for prayer said in the wrong place.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

חַסְדְּךָ (chas-de-cha, v.4), better than life, is the line the mystical reading turns on. If that love is worth more than existence, then existence is not the highest term, and the thirst of verse 2 stops being a lack to be fixed and becomes the accurate perception of a soul that has ranked its wants correctly. The dry land of צִיָּה (tzi-ya, v.2) is then a condition rather than a geography, the state in which nothing at all is available except the wanting. דָּבְקָה (dav-ka, v.9), clinging, is the verb Genesis uses for a man joined to his wife and Deuteronomy for holding fast to God, and the two halves of verse 9 describe that single bond from both directions: the soul reaching up from below, the right hand holding from above.
