Tehilim 93: The LORD reigns, robed in majesty

תְּהִלִּים צ״ג · יְהֹוָה מָלָךְ
HomePs 93
Five verses, among the shortest in the book, and one idea held steady: the throne stands while the water rises. This is the daily psalm for Friday, and it closes Kabbalat Shabbat, said after Lecha Dodi alongside Psalm 92. For a learner it is a short course in Hebrew word order. Verse 1 puts the thing worn before the verb twice, majesty He is robed in, strength He girded on, so you hear the clothing before you hear the wearing. Verse 3 then says one noun three times in nine words and lets the repetition do the work of a flood coming up.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm is built as a rise and a floor. Verses 1 and 2 set two things firm, the world and the throne, using the same verb for both. Verse 3 lets the water rise in three steps, each longer than the last. Verse 4 does not push the water back; it simply places something higher than the noise. Verse 5 then leaves the cosmos entirely for a house and a set of testimonies. Note that God never speaks and never acts in this psalm. Every verb about Him is either a state or an item of clothing, and the only motion in the five verses belongs to the rivers.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The opening two words are a perfect tense, and how to read it is disputed: as a standing fact, the LORD reigns, or as an accession, the LORD has become king, which would make the line a shout at a coronation. The same two words open Psalms 97 and 99 and are quoted inside Psalm 96, so whatever they are, they are a formula. Three words in the middle are rare. דׇּכְיָם (doch-yam, v.3) occurs nowhere else in the book and is usually read as pounding or roaring from a root meaning to crush; מִשְׁבְּרֵי (mish-brei, v.4), breakers, is the same noun that Psalm 42 verse 8 uses of waves passing over a drowning man, which is worth holding next to this verse, where they stay underneath; and how אַדִּירִים (a-di-rim, v.4) attaches is disputed, either as a second thing being compared, mightier than the breakers, or as its own clause about the waves. נַאֲוָה (na-a-va, v.5) sits between two roots that look alike, one meaning to be fitting and one meaning to be lovely, and translations split between holiness befits Your house and Your house is adorned in holiness.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The classical list of the daily psalms, in the Mishnah's tractate Tamid, assigns this psalm to the sixth day, and the reason usually given is that the world was finished on that day, so its king took the throne over something completed, which is what the last four words of verse 1 say. Readers have long set verses 3 and 4 against the older stories in which a god fights the sea, and the difference is the reading: here the sea only makes noise. It lifts up its voice and its pounding, never a hand, and it is answered by a comparison rather than by a blow. On the covenantal side, verse 5 is what stops the psalm being about weather. Testimonies are terms, and a throne that outlasts the flood is being described as reliable rather than merely strong.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the kabbalistic idiom the clothing verbs of verse 1 are read strictly: a garment is put on and can be taken off, so kingship is described as something worn toward the world and not as what God is in Himself. Verse 2 keeps the distinction visible, since the throne is established from of old while the You of אָתָּה (a-ta, v.2) carries no verb and no date. The rising rivers are then read as the noise of the lower worlds, which is loud, real, and unable to reach the height of בַּמָּרוֹם (ba-ma-rom, v.4). The psalm ends by narrowing from the whole earth to one house, which is the standard direction of this literature: what could not be contained anywhere is given an address, and קֹדֶשׁ (ko-desh, v.5), holiness, is the word for what makes a small place able to hold it.
