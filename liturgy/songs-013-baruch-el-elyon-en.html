Baruch El Elyon: Blessed is God Most High

Shabbat day, at the second meal · בָּרוּךְ אֵל
HomeBaruch El Elyon
A lunch zemer whose refrain is one long sentence spread over four short lines: the one who keeps the Sabbath, the son and the daughter, will be acceptable to God like an offering brought on a griddle. That last image is from Leviticus, and it is a deliberately modest one — not a bull, a pan of flour. The poem's argument is that keeping the day is a small offering that counts as a large one, and it repeats that argument seven times.

One sentence over four lines, returning seven times. מַחֲבַת (ma-cha-vat) is the griddle of Leviticus 2, where the flour offering is the one a poor person brings. The refrain is quietly saying that a day of rest counts as a sacrifice, and choosing the cheapest sacrifice on the list to say it with.

♫ Singing notes

  • The refrain is four short lines and returns seven times. It is the only part a guest needs.
  • The lines are very short — two or three words — so the melody turns fast and a stanza is over quickly.
  • Around 100 BPM at lunch. It sits well after Yom Zeh Mechubad, which shares its tempo.
  • Seven refrains is a lot. Most tables sing three or four stanzas and stop, and nobody minds.

♦ About this song

The griddle

Leviticus 2 lists the flour offerings, and the one brought on a griddle is among the smallest. The refrain claims that keeping the Sabbath is accepted like that offering — deliberately modest, and the whole argument of the poem rests on the choice of which sacrifice to name.

The son and the daughter

The refrain names both, and the last stanza names sons, daughters, servant and maidservant. That is the commandment's own list from Exodus 20, and the poem keeps it complete rather than shortening it.

Baruch bar Shmuel of Mainz

The Daat edition closes with an attribution to him. That line is editorial matter, unpointed as such matter always is in these editions, and the scaffold removed it rather than printing it as a line of the song.

A column break inside a word

This edition twice breaks a word across its own line break, leaving a one-letter prefix at the end of one line and the rest of the word at the start of the next. Split naively, the page would show a word cell containing only the definite article. The scaffold rejoins them; measured across all twenty-two songs the rule fires exactly twice, both here.
