Yom Zeh LeYisrael: This day is light and joy for Israel

Friday night, at the table · יוֹם זֶה
HomeYom Zeh LeYisrael
Two words close every stanza and open the song: Sabbath of rest. Eleven stanzas, three lines each, and the last line of every one lands on that phrase — which is why a table can sing this for ten minutes with only three words of Hebrew between them. The tune is one of the most loved in the Ashkenazi repertoire and is often hummed long after the words have run out.

בְּמַעֲמַד (be-ma-a-mad) is the standing at Sinai, the fixed Hebrew name for that scene. The stanza moves from a mountain to a laid table in three lines, which is the poem's habit.

light of my sun, appear, shine always: a Sabbath of rest.
The poem ends on the sun, which is where a Friday night song about light should end — and on the same two words that have closed every stanza since the first.

♫ Singing notes

  • The two closing words are the whole song for a guest. They arrive at the end of every stanza, and a table will land on them together whether or not anyone knows the verses.
  • Slower than most: around 88 BPM. The melody is worth taking time over and is often hummed after the words stop.
  • Three lines per stanza, and the third is always longer — it carries the refrain words. Give it room.
  • Twelve stanzas is more than most tables sing. Four or five is normal; the tune usually outlasts the text.

♦ About this song

The acrostic

The opening letters of the stanzas spell the name Yitzchak. Which Yitzchak is not established, and the poem is usually dated by its style rather than by any external record.

The additional soul

The third stanza names the neshamah yeterah, the extra soul the Talmud says a person receives for the Sabbath and loses when it ends. That idea is why havdalah uses spices — to revive the ordinary soul left behind — and this poem is one of the places a learner is most likely to meet it.

The tune

The melody now sung across the Ashkenazi world is later than the words and is one of the most recognisable in the repertoire. It is not reproduced here; the chord grid is a suggestion for accompanying it, not a transcription of it.
