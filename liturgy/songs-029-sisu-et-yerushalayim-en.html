Sisu et Yerushalayim: Rejoice with Jerusalem

Isaiah 66:10 · שִׂמְחוּ אֶת
HomeSisu et Yerushalayim
Four commands in thirteen words: rejoice, be glad, be joyful, and the surprise — the last group addressed is the one that mourns for her. Isaiah does not tell the mourners to stop mourning. He tells them to rejoice, in the same breath and the same grammar. That reversal is the whole verse, and it is why the line is sung at weddings and on Jerusalem Day and also at the end of a fast.

be joyful with her, all you who mourn for her.
Three of the four imperatives are different verbs for the same thing, which is not clumsiness: שִׂמְחוּ (sim-chu), וְגִילוּ (ve-gi-lu) and שִׂישׂוּ (si-su) pile up because the verse is trying to make joy sound plural. Then מָשׂוֹשׂ (ma-sos) repeats the root of שִׂישׂוּ (si-su) as a noun — literally "be joyful with her a joy", a construction Hebrew loves and English has no way to keep. And every one of these forms is masculine plural, addressed to a crowd. You cannot sing this verse to one person; the grammar will not let you.

♫ Singing notes

  • Clapping on beats two and four is the default and it is what a room will do without being asked.
  • The two halves are the same length. If your tune makes the second half longer, you have stretched מָשׂוֹשׂ (ma-sos) — let it pass quickly, it is a grammatical echo rather than a word to lean on.
  • The last two words are the point of the verse. Whoever is leading should not swallow them.
  • It sits comfortably in A minor for most voices. If the group is mostly low, drop it to G minor rather than trying to reach.

♦ About this song

The text

Isaiah 66:10, from the last chapter of the book. The surrounding verses use the image of a mother and a newborn, which is why the joy in this verse is described as something you share with the city rather than something you feel about it.

Addressed to the mourners

The second half is not a separate group being consoled. It is the same command given to the people least able to obey it, and the verse offers no transition between the two. Reading it, or singing it, in one breath is what makes the point land.

Where you will hear it

At weddings, at Jerusalem Day gatherings, and at the meal that ends the fast of the Ninth of Av, where the reversal in the verse is doing exactly the work it was written for.
