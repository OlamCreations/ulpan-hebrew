Tehilim 38: Do not forsake me

תְּהִלִּים ל״ח · מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד
HomePs 38
Twenty two lines of complaint after the superscription, and the whole psalm is one sick man's inventory: flesh, bones, loins, wounds, heart, eyes. For a learner it is the best place in the book to collect body nouns carrying the first person suffix, because nearly every line supplies one. Watch verse 16, where the psalm stops describing and starts addressing, and notice that nothing is cured by the end. The last thing asked for is speed.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A body, then a social circle, then a courtroom. Verses 2 to 11 catalogue the illness from the inside; verses 12 and 13 pull back far enough to show friends withdrawing and enemies moving into the gap; verses 14 to 21 are the speaker's conduct under that pressure, which turns out to be silence, confession, and no self defence. The hinge is הוֹחָלְתִּי (ho-chal-ti, v.16), the verb of waiting, and it explains everything before it, because a man who has handed his case over has no reason to argue his own corner. Nothing is healed by the end. The final two verses ask only that God stay close and come fast.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The opening of verse 2 is the opening of Psalm 6 with one word of anger substituted for another, close enough that the two are regularly noted together. מְתֹם (me-tom, v.4), glossed here as soundness, is a rare noun from the root meaning whole or complete, so the complaint is not that something hurts but that nothing in him is intact. קֹדֵר (ko-der, v.7) means darkened, and the same word is used elsewhere of people in mourning dress and of a sky going black, which is why the line can be read as clothing or as mood. And לְהַזְכִּיר (le-haz-kir, v.1), the only real word in the superscription, shares a root with the memorial portion burned from the grain offering in Leviticus 2, which lets the psalm present itself as an offering rather than as a song.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The reading tradition takes the superscription at its word: a psalm said to bring something to remembrance is one a person recites when he wants his own case raised, which turns the whole catalogue of symptoms into evidence submitted rather than complaint vented. The silence of verses 14 and 15 is the part most often expanded. Plainly read, a man too weak to answer keeps quiet; read the other way, the silence is deliberate, a decision not to defend himself in front of people because the defence has already been filed in verse 16. On that reading the psalm is not really about illness. It is about what to do while under accusation. In Christian liturgy it is counted among the seven penitential psalms, which is a fact about its reception rather than about its text.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read mystically the catalogue stops being medical. The psalm counts downward through flesh, bones, loins, wounds, heart and eyes, and in the kabbalistic idiom the body is the lower map of a whole structure, so organs that have stopped working are describing channels that have stopped conducting. סְחַרְחַר (schar-char, v.11), a heart going round in circles, and the light leaving the eyes in the same verse are the two directions of that flow slackening at once. This is also why the request of verse 22 makes sense exactly as it stands: not repair, but that the source not withdraw, since a channel with nothing above it has nothing left to carry.
