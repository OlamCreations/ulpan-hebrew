Birkat HaMazon: Grace After Meals

בִּרְכַּת הַמָּזוֹן · after any bread-meal · 4 blessings · biblical command (Deut 8:10)
HomeBirkat HaMazon
Grace After Meals, said after eating bread at any meal. Four blessings, ~5-10 minutes when said fully, ~2-3 minutes when abbreviated. The four blessings: (1) HaZan, the universal blessing for food; (2) HaAretz, thanks for the land of Israel; (3) Boneh Yerushalayim, the prayer for Jerusalem; (4) HaTov v'HaMetiv, the goodness blessing, added by the Sages after a specific historical event (the burial of the slain of Beitar in 135 CE). The four blessings are remarkable for their grounding: from generic gratitude → particular gratitude for the land → eschatological hope for Jerusalem → historical memory. Eating becomes an exercise in Jewish historical consciousness. On Shabbat and holidays the text expands; weekdays use a shorter form. This page teaches the first blessing only, the universal opening.

♦ Lesson

  • When, after any meal that included bread. If no bread was eaten, a shorter blessing called Beracha Acharona is said instead. Birkat HaMazon is for bread-meals specifically.
  • The four blessings:
    1. HaZan (this page), the universal blessing: God feeds the entire world.
    2. HaAretz, thanks specifically for the Land of Israel, where the wheat for the bread grew. Includes thanks for the covenant (Brit) and the Torah.
    3. Boneh Yerushalayim, "Builder of Jerusalem", petition for the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the Temple. On Shabbat, this section includes the Retzeh (a longer paragraph about Shabbat's specialness).
    4. HaTov v'HaMetiv, "the Good and the Doer of Good", added by the Sages after the Bar Kokhba revolt (135 CE), when the dead of Beitar were finally allowed by the Romans to be buried. The added blessing thanks God for that mercy.
  • The Zimun, when 3 or more adult Jewish males eat together, they begin Birkat HaMazon with a public invitation: the leader says "Rabotai, n'varech!" ("Gentlemen, let us bless!") and the group responds. With 10 or more, the leader inserts "Eloheinu" in the invitation, raising the level. The Zimun turns a private meal into a communal acknowledgment.
  • Why specifically the land, the rabbis emphasize that Birkat HaMazon is the only blessing explicitly required by the Torah (Deut 8:10: "You shall eat, be satisfied, and bless the LORD your God for the good land He has given you"). The blessing was originally just for the land; the universal HaZan and the messianic HaYerushalayim blessings were added by the rabbis to bracket the original commandment.
  • Vocabulary:
    • ha-zan (הַזָּן), "the one who feeds". Qal participle of ז.ו.ן (to nourish, sustain). Same root as mazon (food).
    • tuv (טוּב), "goodness". Same root as tov. B'tuvo = "with His goodness".
    • chen / chesed / rachamim (חֵן · חֶסֶד · רַחֲמִים), three modes of divine kindness: chen (grace, unmerited favor), chesed (covenantal loyalty), rachamim (mercy, from rechem = womb).
    • basar (בָּשָׂר), "flesh". Used here in the inclusive sense "every living creature". The blessing is universal, God doesn't only feed humans, He feeds animals too.
  • Length:
    • Weekday full: ~5-10 min recited at conversational pace.
    • Weekday abbreviated (Birkat HaMazon Kitzur): ~2 min, used when time is short or for children.
    • Shabbat / Yom Tov: longer, with the Retzeh insertion + Yaaleh v'Yavo (for holidays).

♦ About this prayer

The longest blessing of Jewish daily life. Four blessings stacked over the residue of every bread-meal. The biblical commandment that turns eating into history.

The biblical command: Deuteronomy 8:10

The Torah explicitly commands: "You shall eat, be satisfied, and bless the LORD your God for the good land He has given you." This is the only post-meal blessing the Torah itself requires; all other blessings (HaMotzi before, the various pre-fruit blessings, etc.) are rabbinic additions. The Deut 8:10 verse names three conditions: eating, being satisfied, and the land. "For the good land", the original direction was specifically toward gratitude for the Land of Israel, where the wheat grew. The Sages later expanded this into the four-blessing structure.

The four-layered theology

The structure of Birkat HaMazon is one of the great architectural achievements of Jewish liturgy:

Blessing 1 (HaZan): universal scope. God feeds all flesh, all creatures, in all places. Cosmic gratitude.

Blessing 2 (HaAretz): particular scope. Israel thanks for the land that produced this specific bread. Covenantal gratitude.

Blessing 3 (Boneh Yerushalayim): future scope. The full meal is incomplete until Jerusalem is rebuilt. Eschatological hope.

Blessing 4 (HaTov v'HaMetiv): historical scope. A specific 135 CE event becomes part of the daily meal-grace. Historical consciousness.

Eating, in Jewish tradition, is supposed to engage all four dimensions: gratitude that the universe nourishes its creatures, that the land of Israel specifically did so today, that the world is incomplete until restoration, and that history is full of small mercies worth remembering. Most people who say Birkat HaMazon every day don't articulate this theology consciously, but it's all there, every meal.

The Bar Kokhba addition: Blessing 4

The fourth blessing (HaTov v'HaMetiv) was added by the Sanhedrin of Yavneh after a specific historical moment. After the failure of the Bar Kokhba revolt against Rome (132-135 CE), the Roman emperor Hadrian initially refused to allow the Jewish dead of the city of Beitar to be buried, leaving the bodies exposed as a warning. Years later, after Hadrian's death, his successor Antoninus Pius granted permission for burial. Additionally, the bodies had miraculously not decomposed in the intervening years. The Sanhedrin marked the moment with a new blessing thanking God for two miracles: HaTov (the Good, that they did not decompose) and HaMetiv (the Doer of Good, that permission was finally given to bury them). The blessing has been said at every Jewish meal ever since.

This is one of the very few places where Jewish liturgy preserves a specific dated historical event in its standard text.
