Havdalah: The Separation

הַבְדָּלָה · Saturday night · 3 stars · 4 blessings · wine, spices, fire, separation
HomeHavdalah
The ceremony that ends Shabbat, said Saturday night when three stars are visible in the sky (~40-60 minutes after sunset). Four blessings in sequence: wine (over a cup, held in the right hand), spices (smelled, usually cloves or other fragrant herbs in a small ornate container), fire (over a multi-wick braided candle, the only blessing of the week that requires a multi-wick flame), and the separation blessing (the climactic Havdalah proper, distinguishing holy from ordinary). The four blessings are sealed by extinguishing the candle in the spilled wine. After Havdalah, Shabbat is fully ended and the new week has begun. Many families add Eliyahu HaNavi after, the next prayer/song page in this section.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Saturday night, after 3 stars are visible. In Jerusalem, ~50 minutes after sunset; elsewhere, ~40-60 min. Local Shabbat-end times are published online.
  • Setup, you need: (1) a cup of wine (poured to overflow, as during Kiddush), (2) a spice container (besamim), often a small silver tower (the "besamim box") filled with cloves, cinnamon, or other fragrant spices, (3) a multi-wick braided candle (the ner havdalah), (4) a small plate to catch the overflow wine.
  • Sequence: 1) Say the wine blessing, holding the cup, but don't drink yet. 2) Say the spices blessing, holding the spice container, pass it around for everyone to smell. 3) Say the fire blessing, holding hands up to the candle to see the reflected light on the fingernails. 4) Say the separation blessing. 5) Drink the wine. 6) Pour the remaining wine on the plate and extinguish the candle in it.
  • Why these four senses: wine (taste), spices (smell), fire (sight), word of separation (hearing/speech). Havdalah engages four of the five senses at once. Some commentators say touch is engaged when the candle is extinguished in the wine.
  • Why the spices, the kabbalists teach that on Shabbat each person receives an extra soul (neshamah yeteirah), which departs at the end of Shabbat. The spices comfort the soul as the extra soul leaves. Without them, the transition back to the ordinary week is too abrupt.
  • Why the multi-wick candle, the rabbis derive this from Genesis: Adam discovered fire on the first Saturday night. The multi-wick candle represents the multiplicity of light that came into being, and the blessing form is plural (me'orei ha-esh, "lights of the fire"). A single candle won't fulfill the obligation.
  • The hamavdil blessing, repeated in the closing phrase "Blessed are You, LORD, who separates between holy and ordinary". The verb hamavdil (hiphil of ב.ד.ל, to separate) is the Hebrew of l'havdil ("by way of distinction"), a common spoken-Hebrew phrase to mark contrast between two things.
  • Pronunciation watch:
    • besamim (בְשָׂמִים), "spices". Plural of bosem. Modern Hebrew word for "perfume" is bosem, same root.
    • me'orot (מְאוֹרוֹת), "lights / luminaries". Plural of ma'or. Same word used in Genesis 1:14 for the celestial lights (sun, moon, stars).
    • kodesh / chol (קֹדֶשׁ / חוֹל), "holy / ordinary (or: secular)". The opposite pair that structures the Jewish week.

♦ About this prayer

A 90-second ceremony that closes Shabbat. Four blessings, four senses, four separations. The dramatic counterweight to Friday night's Kiddush.

Origin: Talmudic foundation, Geonic finalization

The basic obligation to mark the end of Shabbat verbally goes back to the Talmud (Berachot 33a, Pesachim 103a-105a). The four-blessing structure with wine, spices, fire, and separation was established by the early Geonim (~7th-10th centuries CE) and has been universal since. Earlier rabbinic discussion debated whether all four blessings are obligatory or only the separation blessing, the halacha settled on all four.

The four separations in the closing blessing

The final blessing names four distinctions:

  • קֹדֶשׁ / חוֹל (holy / ordinary), the structural distinction of Jewish time.
  • אוֹר / חֹשֶׁךְ (light / darkness), the first distinction made at creation (Gen 1:4).
  • יִשְׂרָאֵל / עַמִּים (Israel / the nations), the distinction of peoplehood and covenant.
  • יוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי / שֵׁשֶׁת יְמֵי הַמַּעֲשֶׂה (the seventh day / the six work-days), the weekly rhythm.

The order is intentional: holy/ordinary is the most abstract; light/dark is sensory; Israel/nations is national; the seventh day vs the six work days is the immediate practical one. The blessing zooms from cosmic to immediate.

The extra soul that leaves

The Talmud (Beitzah 16a) teaches that every Jew receives an extra soul (neshamah yeteirah) for Shabbat, an additional spiritual capacity that enables the heightened state of the day. As Shabbat ends, this extra soul departs. The body, used to its enlarged spirit, feels a kind of loss. The besamim (spices) are smelled to comfort the soul through this transition, the spices' aroma replaces for a moment what the departed soul provided.

Practically: if you've ever felt sad or restless on Saturday night after a deep Shabbat, this is what the tradition describes. Havdalah is designed to ease the descent.

After Havdalah

Once Havdalah is over, work resumes, you can drive, write, light a fire, use the phone. The first weekday meal traditionally begins shortly after (called Melaveh Malka, "escorting the queen", escorting Shabbat out). Many families sing Eliyahu HaNavi (see the next page in this section) and other Saturday-night songs. Some Hasidic and Sephardic communities continue with a small meal that runs late into the night.

For Jonas: the moment after Havdalah is also the moment to plan the upcoming week. The Shabbat rest has restored capacity; the work-week begins with a freshness that is the entire point of the institution.
