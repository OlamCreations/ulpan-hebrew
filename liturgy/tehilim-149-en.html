Tehilim 149: Sing to the LORD a new song

תְּהִלִּים קמ״ט · הַלְלוּ יָהּ
HomePs 149
Nine verses, and one of the five psalms that both open and close with Halleluyah at the end of the book. Those five are said every morning in Pesukei deZimra, so this is a psalm a beginner hears constantly without noticing it. The thing to watch is the collision in verse 6: the high praises of God in the throat and a double-edged sword in the hand, one clause each, with nothing between them to mark a contrast. Everything after that verse is an infinitive, which matters more than it sounds, because an infinitive states a purpose without saying who carries it out or when.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two movements of unequal weight. Verses 1 to 5 are praise: a new song, an assembly, a Maker and a King, dancing, a drum, a lyre, and finally singing in bed. Verses 6 to 9 are a sword, vengeance, rebukes, chains, iron and a written judgment. Verse 6 belongs to both and joins them with nothing stronger than the letter that means and. What holds the second half back from being a call to arms is grammar: לַעֲשׂוֹת (la-a-sot, v.7), לֶאְסֹר (le-sor, v.8) and לַעֲשׂוֹת (la-a-sot, v.9) are all infinitives, purpose clauses attached to a sword that is described as held, not used. The psalm ends by calling the whole thing splendour and returning to the word it opened with, so the frame around the violence is praise on both sides.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The sword of verse 6 is literally a sword of two mouths, פִּיפִיּוֹת (pi-fi-yot, v.6), since a blade edge in Hebrew is a mouth. That puts two mouths in the hand of someone whose throat, בִּגְרוֹנָם (big-ro-nam, v.6), is already full of praise, and the pun is almost certainly deliberate: the verse is built out of speech organs. The second crux is כָּתוּב (ka-tuv, v.9), the judgment written. Written where is not stated. Read as the Torah's own sentences it makes the second half a matter of enforcement rather than initiative; read as a decree recorded elsewhere it makes it prophecy. Third, הָדָר (ha-dar, v.9) can be construed as an abstract noun, splendour, so the line means this is splendour for all His faithful, or as a predicate for God, so it means He is the splendour of all His faithful. Translations split on it and the Hebrew does not decide.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

A long tradition of reading takes the sword of verse 6 as words rather than metal, on the grounds that the verse gives the faithful a throat before it gives them a hand and that the weapon is named after mouths. On that reading the vengeance of verse 7 is carried out in study and testimony, and the psalm is describing what praise does when it is precise. A second reading works on כָּתוּב (ka-tuv, v.9) and treats the word written as a limit rather than a licence: the faithful execute only a judgment already recorded, which forbids them from inventing one. Both readings are attempts to solve the same discomfort, that a liturgical psalm said every morning puts a blade in the hand of the congregation and does not apologise for it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm keeps moving between the public and the hidden. It begins in an assembly, חֲסִידִים (cha-si-dim, v.1), and reaches its quietest point in מִשְׁכְּבוֹתָם (mish-ke-vo-tam, v.5), praise sung on the bed, in the dark, where nobody hears it. That verse is often read as the real location of the work: what a person sings alone at night is what the daylight praise is made of. The two words for radiance, בְּכָבוֹד (be-cha-vod, v.5) and הָדָר (ha-dar, v.9), bracket the sword section, so the entire middle passage sits inside a description of light. In that frame the double-edged sword is not an instrument added to praise but what praise becomes when it is directed, and the reason the psalm can end on Halleluyah without any transition is that it never left.
