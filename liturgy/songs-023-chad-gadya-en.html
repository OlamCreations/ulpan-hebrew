Chad Gadya: One little goat

Pesach seder, the last song of the night · חַד גַּדְיָא
HomeChad Gadya
The last song of the seder, in Aramaic rather than Hebrew, and the one every child in the room is waiting for. A father buys a goat for two coins; a cat eats the goat; a dog bites the cat; and each verse repeats the whole chain from the top before adding a new link. Ten verses, and the last one ends the chain. Whether it is a nursery rhyme that wandered into the Haggadah or a compressed history of empires depends entirely on who you ask, and nobody at the table needs to decide before singing it.

One little goat, one little goat, that father bought for two zuzim.
This is Aramaic, not Hebrew, which is why the definite article comes at the END of a word — גַּדְיָא (gad-ya) is "the kid", with the article as a final vowel. Every noun in the song does it, and once you have heard it four times you have the rule.

Two words are doing the linking: לְשׁוּנְרָא (le-shun-ra) introduces the new victim and דְּאַָכְלָה (de-ach-la) turns the previous verse into a relative clause. Aramaic builds the chain with one syllable, de-.

And the Holy One, blessed be He, came and slew the angel of death, who slew the slaughterer, who slaughtered the ox that drank the water that quenched the fire that burned the stick that beat the dog that bit the cat that ate the goat, that father bought for two zuzim.
The last verse is the only one that ends the sequence rather than extending it. Nothing comes after הַקָּדושׁ (ha-ka-dosh), and the song simply stops — which, at the end of a night about a redemption, is an argument made entirely by structure.

♫ Singing notes

  • It gets faster every verse and it is meant to. By verse ten the table should barely be getting the words out.
  • Everyone knows the first line and the last few words. The middle of the chain is carried by whoever remembers the order, and the rest join back in at דְּזַבִּין (de-za-bin).
  • The chords do not change across ten verses. One shape per verse, and let the voices do the accelerating.
  • Some families do animal noises. This is not in any manuscript and is not going away.

♦ About this song

The language

Aramaic, which is the language of the Talmud and of the Kaddish, not the Hebrew of the rest of the Haggadah. The definite article is a final vowel rather than a prefix, and the relative particle is a single syllable at the front of a word — two features that make the whole song audible as a foreign register even to someone who does not know why.

What it is about

One long tradition reads the chain as the empires that swallowed one another over Israel's history, ending with the one that ends them. Another reads it as a children's counting song, of the same family as the house that Jack built, adopted into the Haggadah because the children were falling asleep. Both readings are old. The song does not choose between them and neither does this page.

Two zuzim

זוּזֵי (zu-zei) is a real coin of the Talmudic period, and two of them is a small sum — the price of a goat, not of an inheritance. The song opens on the smallest possible transaction and ends on the largest possible one.
