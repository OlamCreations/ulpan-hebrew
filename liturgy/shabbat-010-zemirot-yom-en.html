Zemirot Yom Shabbat: Saturday Lunch Table Songs

זְמִירוֹת יוֹם שַׁבָּת · Saturday lunch · between courses · featuring Baruch El Elyon
HomeZemirot Yom
The Saturday-lunch table songs, sung between courses or before Birkat HaMazon on Saturday day. Distinct corpus from Friday-night zemirot, with its own classics. The most-sung is Baruch El Elyon, by Rabbi Baruch ben Shmuel (Spain, ~12th c., signature spelled by the acrostic). Other widely-sung Saturday zemirot: Yom Zeh Mechubad ("This day is honored"), Ki Eshmera Shabbat ("If I observe Shabbat", by Avraham ibn Ezra, ~1150), Yom Zeh L'Yisrael ("This day for Israel"). Each is shorter than Friday-night zemirot (most have 3-5 stanzas vs 4-6 on Friday). Saturday-day singing is often more relaxed and gentle, the mystical intensity of Friday night gives way to a calmer afternoon mood. This page features Baruch El Elyon as the representative song.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Saturday lunch (Shabbat day meal), between courses or before Birkat HaMazon. Total singing time: 5-20 minutes depending on how many are sung.
  • Author signature, Baruch El Elyon has an acrostic of the author's name in the first letters of each stanza: בָּרוּךְ חָזָק (Baruch [be] strong), a common scribal signature. The author is Rabbi Baruch ben Shmuel of Mainz (~12th c. Spain/Provence), who wrote several other piyyutim.
  • The other Shabbat-day zemirot:
    • Yom Zeh Mechubad, "This day is honored above all days, for on it the Eternal One rested". Most-sung after the meal, before Birkat HaMazon.
    • Ki Eshmera Shabbat, by Avraham ibn Ezra. Each stanza follows a strict syllabic meter. The refrain: "If I observe Shabbat, God will keep me".
    • Yom Zeh L'Yisrael, by an anonymous medieval poet. Each stanza ends with the refrain "Yom zeh l'Yisrael, orah v'simcha" ("This day for Israel is light and joy").
    • Shabbat haYom L'Adonai, "The Sabbath today is for the LORD", sung in some Ashkenazi communities.
  • The "son with the daughter" inclusion, the refrain explicitly says "ha-ben im ha-bat" (the son with the daughter), an unusually egalitarian phrase for a 12th-century Hebrew poem. The author makes clear that the obligation and reward of Shabbat-observance are equal for both genders. This is a small but notable theological move.
  • The "griddle offering" image, "k'minchah al machavat" ("as a meal-offering on a griddle") references Leviticus 2:5, the prescribed grain offering. The poet is saying: keeping Shabbat is now the equivalent of a Temple offering, a sacrifice without an altar, given in the form of weekly rest.
  • Vocabulary:
    • el elyon (אֵל עֶלְיוֹן), "God Most High". An ancient name for God, first used in Genesis 14:19 by Melchizedek priest of Salem. Means literally "the highest God".
    • menuchah (מְנוּחָה), "rest". From נ.ו.ח (to rest, settle). Same root as Noach (Noah) and nachat ru'ach ("satisfaction", lit. "resting of the spirit").
    • pidyon (פִּדְיוֹן), "redemption". From פ.ד.ה (to redeem, ransom). Same root as pidyon haben (the ceremony of redeeming a firstborn son) and podeh (redeemer).
    • se'et va-anachah (שֵׂאת וַאֲנָחָה), "burden and sighing". Two words for the weight of weekday life. Shabbat redeems the soul from this weight.
    • yera'tzu (יֵרָצוּ), "they will be acceptable". Niphal imperfect of ר.צ.ה (to be pleased). Same root as ratzon (will/desire) and retzeh ("be pleased", a key word in Shabbat liturgy).

♦ About this prayer

The Saturday-day singing tradition, distinct from Friday night. Slower, gentler, with its own corpus of medieval Spanish-Provençal piyyutim. Baruch El Elyon is the universal favorite.

Why Saturday lunch needs its own songs

The day meal of Shabbat has a different texture than the evening meal. Friday night is the dramatic entrance, candles, Kiddush, the descent of the Shechinah, the family gathered around the table after synagogue. Saturday day is the abiding mood, Shabbat already underway, the morning service over, the sun high. The lunch is unhurried; the singing reflects that.

Friday-night zemirot frequently use language of mystical union (bride/groom). Saturday-day zemirot focus more on the practice of Shabbat, what observing Shabbat means, what the rest accomplishes, how the soul is restored. Less ecstatic, more meditative.

The acrostic signature tradition

Medieval Hebrew poets routinely hid their names in the first letters of stanzas, a tradition that allowed authorship to be preserved even when names were dropped from titles or worn off old manuscripts. Baruch El Elyon, Ki Eshmera Shabbat, and several others have such acrostics. Yedid Nefesh hides the divine name (yud-heh-vav-heh) in the same way; Lecha Dodi hides the name of the author Shlomo HaLevi.

If you read a zemer slowly and check the first letter of each stanza, you'll often find the author's signature hidden in the page. It's like a secret thumbprint, ~800-1000 years old.

The corpus today

Most modern benchers (the booklets used at Jewish tables) print 4-6 Saturday-day zemirot, often the same ones across communities. Baruch El Elyon is universal. The order and selection vary slightly by rite: Ashkenazi siddurim emphasize Baruch El Elyon and Yom Zeh Mechubad; Sephardi siddurim often start with Yom Zeh L'Yisrael; Yemenite communities preserve some unique Saturday-day songs not found in other rites.

The melodies are folk-traditional; many have Hasidic or Mizrahi modern reinterpretations. Spend a few Shabbat lunches with a family that sings, and you'll absorb the corpus within a month.
