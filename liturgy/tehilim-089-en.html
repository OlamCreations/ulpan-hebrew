Tehilim 89: I will sing of the LORD's kindnesses forever

תְּהִלִּים פ״ט · מַשְׂכִּיל לְאֵיתָן
HomePs 89
The longest psalm in Book III of the Psalter and the one that ends it. Ethan the Ezrahite, named in the heading, is listed among the wise men in the book of Kings, and the family name is usually connected with Zerah of Judah. The poem does three things in sequence: it praises the LORD for kindness and faithfulness, it quotes at length an oath granting David a permanent throne, and then it reports that the throne has been thrown to the ground, using the vocabulary of the oath to say so. It does not resolve. For a learner it is a drill in two forms: the suffixed noun (Your faithfulness, My kindness, his throne, our horn), and the second person perfect verb, which carries the entire accusation from verse 39 on. The last verse is familiar from the weekday evening service.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements and a seam. Verses 2 to 19 are praise, built on two nouns, kindness and faithfulness, and on the claim that nobody in the divine council is a rival. Verses 20 to 38 are a quoted oracle in which God swears an unconditional throne to David, explicitly allowing the sons to sin and be punished without the covenant lapsing. Verses 39 to 52 report that the throne is on the ground, and they do it in the oracle's own vocabulary: covenant, crown, seed, right hand, sword, all reversed one by one. The hinge is a single word, וְאַתָּה (ve-a-ta, v.39), and after it the You who founded the world in verse 12 is the You who broke the walls in verse 41. Nothing is resolved: the last thing the poem says is that enemies are mocking the anointed. Verse 53 is the doxology that closes Book III of the Psalter, so it seals the book rather than answering the poem.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The two nouns to track are kindness and faithfulness. Each occurs seven times, opening in the vow at אֱמוּנָתְךָ (e-mu-nat-cha, v.2) and closing in the demand of verse 50, where the speaker asks back for exactly what he sang about, which is why the psalm reads as a legal claim rather than a lament. Several cruxes stay open. In verse 3 the word rendered forever also means world, and the line has long been read both as kindness is built forever and as the world is built by kindness. At אִם (im, v.36) the particle is literally if: a Hebrew oath states the first half of a self curse and leaves the rest unsaid, so if I lie to David functions as I never will. רָהַב (ra-hav, v.11) is Rahab, a name for the sea monster of the creation combat and elsewhere a byword for Egypt. צוּר (tzur, v.44) is spelled like the ordinary word for rock, the same word the king uses of God in verse 27, and most readers take it here as the flint edge of a sword. חָלֶד (cha-led, v.48) is a rare word for a lifespan, and the first half of verse 48 is broken enough that translators disagree about who its subject is.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

An old rabbinic identification reads Ethan the Ezrahite of the heading as Abraham, which turns the psalm into a complaint made by the first man to receive a promise of descendants, and lets the oracle be heard as older than David. The reading of verse 3 as the world is built by kindness has had a long life outside this psalm, as a claim that creation is held up by acts of kindness rather than by law. Firstborn at בְּכוֹר (be-chor, v.28) was taken as a rank granted rather than a fact of birth, and became one of the anchors of messianic reading here, since the throne promised in the oracle outlived every king who actually sat on it. The placement of the doxology at בָּרוּךְ (ba-ruch, v.53) was itself read as commentary: the book will not end on the taunt of verse 52, so it answers a complaint it cannot refute with a blessing instead of an argument.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the kabbalistic reading the pair that governs the psalm, kindness and faithfulness, is a pair of measures rather than two moods, and the covenant with David is the tie between an upper throne and a lower one, so that damage below is spoken about as damage above. The hiding asked about in verse 47 is the standard term for the concealment of the face, a condition in which the relation is not cancelled but withdrawn from sight, which is what lets the psalm accuse and go on addressing at the same time. The throne thrown to the ground in verse 45 is read in this register as exile described from the divine side rather than the human one. The doxology at the end is then not a change of subject but the one move still available: to bless the source while the channel is shut.
