Tehilim 46: God is our refuge and strength

תְּהִלִּים מ״ו · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִבְנֵי
HomePs 46
Twelve verses in three stanzas, each closed by Selah, and the singers named in the first line are the sons of Korah rather than David. Two of the stanzas end with the same refrain, verses 8 and 12 word for word, which is how you can hear that this was written for a group with an answering part. The word to watch is the root m-w-t, to totter. Mountains totter into the sea in verse 3, kingdoms totter in verse 7, and in verse 6 the city does not. One verb, used three times, carries the whole argument.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three stanzas, each shut with Selah at verses 4, 8 and 12. The first is the world coming apart: earth, mountains, sea. The second is a city that does not come apart, watered by a river with channels rather than by a sea. The third is history, nations and kingdoms, ending in the destruction of weapons. The refrain of verses 8 and 12 is the congregation's line, and the fact that it does not appear after verse 4 has led many readers to suppose it dropped out there; that is a proposal about the text, not something the manuscripts show. The last word before each Selah is a claim already made, and the psalm never argues for it. It only widens the frame until the claim has been said in three sizes.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm is held together by two roots repeated across its stanzas, and both are visible in the transliteration. The root m-w-t, to totter or give way, runs through וּבְמוֹט (uv-mot, v.3), תִּמּוֹט (ti-mot, v.6) and מָטוּ (ma-tu, v.7): mountains give way, the city does not, kingdoms do. The root h-m-h, to make a loud noise, runs from יֶהֱמוּ (ye-he-mu, v.4), the waters, to הָמוּ (ha-mu, v.7), the nations, so the two halves of the psalm are describing the same disturbance twice. Two words in it are genuinely unsettled. עֲלָמוֹת (a-la-mot, v.1), the direction over the title, is read as a reference to young women and so to high voices, or as the name of a tune or an instrument, and the case for either is thin. בְּהָמִיר (be-ha-mir, v.3) is a causative form of the root m-w-r, to exchange or alter, so the line says the earth swaps its nature rather than merely moves; some translations render it as the earth being removed, which needs a different root.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The singers in the superscription are the descendants of the man the ground opened to swallow (Numbers 16:32), and Numbers 26:11 goes out of its way to record that his sons did not die. A song about ground that gives way, credited to that family, is a strange coincidence to leave unnoticed, and it makes the refrain sound less like a slogan and more like something learned. Verse 10 is read across the tradition as a description of the world to come rather than of any past battle, alongside Isaiah 2:4, where the same weapons are not burned but reforged into farm tools. The two images differ on what happens to the metal, and that difference is the whole question of whether the end of war is a repair or a clearing away. Verse 11 is spoken by God to everyone at once, which is why it is usually quoted alone, out of the psalm.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the kabbalistic reading the sea and the river are two ways the same water arrives: undivided and destructive, or divided into channels and life-giving, and נָהָר (na-har, v.5) is the second of these, the flow that reaches a place small enough to receive it. The city with God in her midst is the standard image of indwelling presence, feminine in the Hebrew throughout verse 6, and the help that comes at בֹּקֶר (bo-ker, v.6), the turning of the morning, is read as the point where night has finished rather than as sunrise. Verse 11 is then not addressed to enemies but to the reader: הַרְפּוּ (har-pu, v.11) is a verb of slackening the grip, and knowing follows from letting go, in that order and not the other.
