Tehilim 24: The earth is the LORD's

תְּהִלִּים כ״ד · לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 24
Ten verses in three unequal blocks: a two verse claim about who owns the world, a four verse entrance liturgy asking who may go up, and a four verse dialogue at a gate that is mostly repetition. This is the Sunday psalm, the shir shel yom for the first day of the week, and in Ashkenazi practice it is sung on weekdays while the Torah scroll is carried back to the ark, which is where most people first meet it. Watch how much of it is questions: three verses out of ten ask something, and in each case the answer comes from another voice.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three blocks. Verses 1 and 2 claim ownership of everything and ground the claim in an act of founding. Verses 3 to 6 are an entrance liturgy: a question at the gate, a list of conditions, a blessing for whoever meets them. Verses 7 to 10 are a second entrance, this time for the King rather than for the worshipper, built as call and response, which is why the same lines return with small changes instead of new material. The conditions of verse 4 repay slow reading, because not one of them is ritual: כַפַּיִם (cha-pa-yim, v.4) is about hands, and the two clauses after it are about what a person does with his word.

רמז · Remez: allusion

נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.4) is written as "my soul", meaning God's own self, and read aloud in the synagogue as "his soul"; printed Bibles carry both forms. The written text makes the clause a warning about carrying the divine self in vain, close to the third commandment, while the read text makes it about a man who stakes his own life on something empty. Neither reading has pushed the other out. Note also that the verb of נָשָׂא (na-sa, v.4) and the verb of יִשָּׂא (yi-sa, v.5) are the same root: what the man refused to lift is what ends up lifting him. עוֹלָם (o-lam, v.7) is usually rendered "everlasting", though the noun names duration rather than antiquity, which is why both ancient doors and eternal doors are defensible.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The Mishnah in Tamid lists this psalm as the one the Levites sang in the Temple on the first day of the week, and the Talmud explains that assignment by the first day of creation, when God acquired the world and ruled it alone. That is why the psalm opens on possession rather than on praise. The gate dialogue of verses 7 to 10 is widely read as accompanying a procession, and the ark entering the city in Second Samuel 6 is the scene usually attached to it, though the psalm names no occasion of its own. In Ashkenazi practice these verses are sung on weekdays as the Torah scroll goes back into the ark, which turns the gates into the doors of that cabinet and the entering King into a scroll being put away.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The gates that have to lift their heads are read as an image of a vessel enlarging itself for what is about to enter, since nothing can be received by a container measured for something smaller. On that reading verses 7 to 10 are two stages rather than one repeated stage: the first opening is requested, the second is performed. The title in צְבָאוֹת (tze-va-ot, v.10), hosts, names God through multitude, and placing it at the end of a psalm that began with a single owner of everything closes the circle. The one and the many turn out to be the same claim seen from its two ends.
