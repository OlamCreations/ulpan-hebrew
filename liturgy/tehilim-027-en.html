Tehilim 27: The LORD is my light and my salvation

תְּהִלִּים כ״ז · לְדָוִד יְהֹוָה
HomePs 27
Fourteen verses in two tempers. The first six speak about God with complete confidence; from verse 7 the same voice turns and asks, and the imperatives arrive one after another: hear, be gracious, answer, do not hide, do not abandon me. Many communities say it morning and evening from the start of Elul through Hoshana Rabbah, so it is the psalm a learner meets most often in the weeks around the New Year. Watch the seam between verse 6 and verse 7. The Hebrew does nothing to soften it.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Two psalms' worth of mood in one text. Verses 1 to 6 are declarative and speak of God in the third person: light, stronghold, shelter, rock, and an enemy who has already fallen. From verse 7 nearly every main verb is an imperative addressed directly to God, and most of the requests are negative: do not hide, do not turn away, do not abandon, do not hand me over. Because of this, many scholars treat the halves as two originally separate poems joined later, while others read the change as the whole point, a person saying what he believes and then discovering he still has to ask for it. The Hebrew offers no seam to point at, so the question stays open. Notice וְעַתָּה (ve-a-ta, v.6), and now, which closes the confident half by pulling it into the present tense.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm is built on eyes and faces. אוֹרִי (o-ri, v.1), my light, makes seeing the governing image, and it comes back at בְּנֹעַם (bno-am, v.4), to gaze on the delight of the LORD, and again at לִרְאוֹת (lir-ot, v.13), to see. Against that, the whole petition half is afraid of a face being hidden. וּלְבַקֵּר (ul-va-ker, v.4) is a genuine crux: the root can mean to visit, to inspect, or to seek out, and the 1917 Jewish Publication Society rendering, to visit early in His temple, hears in it the word for morning instead. The word at לׅׄוּׅׄלֵׅׄאׅׄ (lu-le, v.13) carries the Masoretic extraordinary points, dots written above the letters, and the sentence it opens has no ending at all: the condition is given, the consequence is never stated, and translators have to supply one or leave the line hanging.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The custom of saying this psalm twice a day from the start of Elul through the festival season is usually explained by a reading that hears the calendar inside it: my light for the New Year, and my salvation for the Day of Atonement, and He will hide me in His shelter for the festival of booths, since בְּסֻכֹּה (be-su-ko, v.5) is written with the same consonants as the word for that booth. Which came first, the custom or the reading, is not clear from the sources, and the practice is attested far later than the psalm. Verse 10 has its own afterlife in Jewish reading, where the father and mother who leave are taken less as a biography of David than as the limit case that makes the argument work: the psalm names the one abandonment nobody expects, in order to say that the claim survives it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read mystically the psalm is one movement, not two. The first half is what a soul knows while the light is on it; the second is the same soul when the light withdraws, and the withdrawal is not punishment but the condition that makes asking possible. בְּסֻכֹּה (be-su-ko, v.5) is a shelter that hides, and hiding is treated here as a form of nearness rather than distance, which is why the worst thing the speaker can name in verse 9 is a face turned away and the best thing he can name in verse 4 is a face he is allowed to look at. קַוֵּה (ka-ve, v.14), wait, is the verb of the space between the two states, and it is said twice in a single line, as though the waiting had to be doubled to be survivable.
