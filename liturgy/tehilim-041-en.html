Tehilim 41: Happy is the one who considers the weak

תְּהִלִּים מ״א · לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 41
Fourteen verses, and one of the psalms people say at a sickbed. It opens with a rule stated about somebody else, that whoever attends to the weak is attended to in his own weak hour, and then the speaker turns out to be the weak one: from verse 5 he is in bed, his visitors are counting the days, and the friend who ate at his table has turned on him. For a learner the drill is the third person object suffix, four verbs carrying it in verses 2 to 4 alone. Verse 14 is not part of the poem. It is the doxology that closes Book One of the Psalms, and the same formula ends each of the other four books.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements and a seal. Verses 2 to 4 state a principle in the third person: the one who attends to the weak is attended to in his turn. Verses 5 to 11 are the speaker's own case, in the first person, from a bed, with a visitor who comes to look, whispering behind his back, and a friend who ate his bread. Verses 12 and 13 close with the evidence he is willing to accept, which is only that the enemy has not yet won. Verse 14 stands outside all of it as the doxology ending Book One of the Psalms. Read whole, the psalm is a man testing his own opening line on himself and finding it holds, barely.

רמז · Remez: allusion

One noun carries the psalm twice. בְּנֶפֶשׁ (bne-fesh, v.3) asks that he not be handed over to the nefesh of his enemies, where the word means appetite or the throat that swallows, and נַפְשִׁי (naf-shi, v.5) asks for healing of nafshi, the same noun used for the self that is sick. Biblical Hebrew gives that word both jobs, and this psalm puts them two verses apart. הָפַכְתָּ (ha-fach-ta, v.4) is the crux: a second person verb meaning You have turned, arriving without warning in a line otherwise about God in the third person, with an object that is either his bed or his lying down. Whether the sense is that the bed was turned in nursing him, or that the sickness itself was turned into health, is disputed, and translations split on it. עָקֵב (a-kev, v.10) is an idiom found nowhere else in the Bible, literally he made the heel great against me. Every English version guesses, and lifted his heel is a guess.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 4 became the text behind visiting the sick. If the sustaining happens at the bed itself, then the bed is where a person should be, and a visitor who arrives there is doing a small version of what the verse describes. That reading also explains why the psalm is said at sickbeds rather than only said about them. Verse 10 is usually read against the books of Samuel, where David's own counsellor goes over to Absalom, and the phrase the man of my peace is taken as naming him without naming him. The identification is not forced by the text, and the psalm never gives a name, which is part of why it stays usable by anyone betrayed by someone closer than an enemy.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The shape of the psalm is a measure coming back. What is given at the top, attention paid to someone with no power, returns at the bottom, when the giver has no power either, and the traditional name for that symmetry is measure for measure. וַתַּצִּיבֵנִי (va-ta-tzi-ve-ni, v.13) is the end point: a verb meaning to set upright and station, with the speaker as its object and before Your face as its place, forever. Nothing is said about the illness being cured. What changes is where he stands, and in the mystical idiom that is the whole of what a person can ask for: not the removal of the condition but a fixed position in front of the source.
