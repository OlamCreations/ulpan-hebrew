Tehilim 126: We were like dreamers

תְּהִלִּים קכ״ו · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 126
Six verses that most people know as a tune, since this is the psalm sung before the grace after meals on Shabbat, festivals, and any day the penitential prayers are dropped. It is also the strangest of the Songs of Ascents, because it reports the return in the past tense in verse 1 and then asks for it in verse 4. Two pieces of grammar carry the second half. Verse 6 doubles the infinitive onto the verb twice, going he goes, coming he comes, which is how Hebrew intensifies without adverbs, and verses 5 and 6 build the whole ending out of one exchange, tears for glad song, repeated three times with different words.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three verses of memory, one of petition, two of principle. The memory is told from outside in: first what it felt like, then what it looked like to the nations, then the speakers agreeing with what the nations said. The petition in verse 4 asks for the same thing the memory reports, which means either the return happened and stopped short, or the first restoration is being used as the warrant for asking again. The psalm does not decide it. Verses 5 and 6 answer neither question and instead state the rule the whole thing runs on, that what is carried out weeping comes back as harvest.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The two phrases at the centre of the crux are related but not identical. שִׁיבַת (shi-vat, v.1), the return of Zion, is built plainly on the root for turning back. שְׁבִיתֵנוּ (shvi-te-nu, v.4) is the noun of the standing idiom that the older translations render turn the captivity and modern ones render restore the fortunes, and which root it belongs to, turning or capturing, is disputed and has been for a long time. כְּחֹלְמִים (ke-chol-mim, v.1) is usually those who dream, and the verse depends on it: the return was so unlikely it felt unreal. A minority reading attaches the word instead to a second root meaning to be restored to health, and translates like those made well. מֶשֶׁךְ (me-shech, v.6) is the same spelling as the place name in Psalm 120 verse 5, and here it is disputed too, read as the trail of seed, the seed pouch, or a measure of it. The doubled verbs in הָלוֹךְ (ha-loch, v.6) with יֵלֵךְ (ye-lech, v.6) and בֹּא (bo, v.6) with יָבֹא (ya-vo, v.6) are infinitives laid on finite verbs, the ordinary Hebrew intensifier.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The tension between verse 1 and verse 4 is the reason the psalm gets read as being about an unfinished return, and the streambeds of verse 4 sharpen it: a wadi in the Negev is not a river that has dried up, it is a channel that is dry by nature and fills all at once, so what is asked for is a reversal that arrives suddenly and not a gradual repair. Verse 2 has drawn attention for putting the confession of God's action in the mouth of the nations first and only then in Israel's, which reverses the usual order of who testifies. The sowing of verse 5 has been read as an argument about cost: the seed that goes into the ground is really lost, the weeping is not a mood but the loss itself, and the promise concerns what that loss becomes rather than denying it.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read mystically the psalm is about the gap between an event and the capacity to receive it. Verse 1 gives the state of a vessel too small for what it has just been handed, which is what being like a dreamer means: present at your own good and unable to hold it. Laughter and glad song in verse 2 are then the widening, and they begin in the mouth, the narrowest opening. The seed of verse 6 is the standard image for something that must go down and be hidden before it can return in a form worth carrying, and the psalm makes the weeping part of the mechanism rather than an obstacle to it. The last word, his sheaves, is the only possessive in the two closing verses: what comes back belongs to the one who went out.
