Tehilim 109: God of my praise, do not be silent

תְּהִלִּים ק״ט · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד
HomePs 109
Thirty-one verses of a man attacked by speech, answering with speech. The middle block, verses 6 to 19, is a curse violent enough that translators disagree about whose mouth it belongs in: the JPS version this page is built from puts the whole block inside quotation marks and reads it as the enemies' words quoted back at them. The psalm has no fixed place in the daily service, so most readers meet it only going through the book in order. Watch one detail travel the whole length: the accuser told to stand at the right hand in verse 6 is answered exactly in verse 31, where God stands at the right hand of the poor man.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Five parts. Verses 1 to 5 are the complaint, and every weapon in them is verbal. Verses 6 to 19 are a sustained curse against a single man, his office, his family, his estate and his name. Verse 20 seals that block and hands it to God as a wage owed. Verses 21 to 25 turn to God and describe a body wrecked by fasting and public contempt. Verses 26 to 31 ask for rescue, promise public thanks, and close on the image the curse opened with. The clearest structural fact in the psalm is the pair of right hands: an accuser is told to stand at the right hand of the accused in verse 6, and in verse 31 God stands at the right hand of the needy. Same posture, opposite courtroom.

רמז · Remez: allusion

One root runs the length of the psalm: the root behind וְשָׂטָן (ve-sa-tan, v.6), the word satan, which here is a common noun for a courtroom accuser and not a proper name. It appears as a verb in יִשְׂטְנוּנִי (yis-te-nu-ni, v.4), they accuse me, and as a plural noun in שֹׂטְנַי (sot-nai, v.20) and again in שׂוֹטְנַי (sot-nai, v.29). Reading those four points in order gives the plot without the sentences. The other crux is who speaks verses 6 to 19. The Hebrew has no quotation marks, and the block can be read either as the speaker's own curse or as the enemies' curse quoted back so that verse 20 can call it their wage. The JPS translation printed in this project's source file takes the second option and opens a quotation mark at verse 6, closing it at the end of verse 19. Both readings are old, and neither is settled. Note also תְפִלָּה (tfi-la, v.4): the Hebrew is a noun, prayer, not a verb, so the line says the speaker is prayer rather than that he prays.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

One line of traditional reading refuses to leave the target anonymous and attaches the psalm to named enemies out of the David narratives, which turns an open curse into a verdict on a person the reader already knows and limits how far it can be reused. A second line works on תְפִלָּה (tfi-la, v.4) and reads it as a description of a state rather than an activity: a man so far into his situation that prayer is no longer something he does at intervals but what he now consists of. A third, less comfortable, notices that verses 9 and 12 curse orphans and widows, the two groups the Torah names most often as protected, and treats the psalm as showing what rage produces when it is given full speech instead of being acted on. On that reading the block is preserved precisely because it was said and not done.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm is built out of mouths. It opens with the mouth of the wicked and the mouth of deceit in פִי (fi, v.2) and וּפִי (u-fi, v.2), and it does not reach the speaker's own mouth until בְּפִי (be-fi, v.30), twenty-eight verses later. In between, cursing stops being sound and becomes matter: worn as clothing, drunk like water in כַמַּיִם (cha-ma-yim, v.18), soaked into the bones like oil. That is the mystical reading in condensed form, that speech is a substance and takes on the shape of whoever holds it, so a man who loves cursing ends up wearing it. The turn at verse 21 is therefore not a change of subject but a change of what the speaker's own speech is being used for, and the last word of the psalm is a mouth speaking praise in front of a crowd rather than a mouth speaking harm behind a back.
