Tehilim 84: How lovely are Your dwelling places

תְּהִלִּים פ״ד · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 84
Thirteen verses by the sons of Korah about wanting to be somewhere you are not. It is a pilgrim's psalm: the courts are far off, the road runs through a dry valley, and the speaker never once places himself inside. Three times it says ashrei, happy, and the first of the three, verse 5, is the line that opens Ashrei in the daily service, prefixed to Psalm 145. Track the word for house as you read. A bird finds one, the poet envies it, and by verse 11 he would rather stand in the doorway of God's house than live inside anyone else's.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three beatitudes divide the poem: verse 5 for those already in the house, verse 6 for those on the road, verse 13 for anyone who trusts. Between them the movement runs one way. Verses 2 to 4 are longing at a distance, verses 6 to 8 are the journey, verses 9 and 10 are the prayer at the gate, verses 11 and 12 are the comparison that justifies the trip. Four words hold the psalm together and can be tracked by eye: house at בַיִת (va-yit, v.4), בֵיתֶךָ (vei-te-cha, v.5) and בְּבֵית (be-veit, v.11); courts at לְחַצְרוֹת (le-chatz-rot, v.3) and בַּחֲצֵרֶיךָ (ba-cha-tze-rei-cha, v.11); shield at מָגִנֵּנוּ (ma-gi-ne-nu, v.10) and וּמָגֵן (u-ma-gen, v.12); and the man of אָדָם (a-dam, v.6), who comes back at אָדָם (a-dam, v.13). Note that the speaker never puts himself inside the house. The arrival in verse 8 belongs to they, and the last thing he asks for in verse 10 is a look, not a door.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 7 is the philological crux and almost every word in it is contested. הַבָּכָא (ha-ba-cha, v.7) may be a place name, Baca, or a common noun: the same consonants give the verb for weeping, which yields the valley of weeping, and 2 Samuel 5:23 has a plural form usually taken as a kind of tree, which would make it a valley of balsams and a real dry wadi. בְּרָכוֹת (bra-chot, v.7) is written with consonants that carry two vocalizations, berakhot, blessings, which is how the Masoretes pointed it, and berekhot, pools, which fits the springs in the first half of the verse better. מוֹרֶה (mo-re, v.7) is either the early rain or a teacher, both from the same root. Nothing in the verse settles any of the three, and translations diverge line by line. Two smaller points. הִסְתּוֹפֵף (his-to-fef, v.11) is a verb that occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible, built on the noun for a threshold, so it means standing in the doorway rather than going in. And אֶת (et, v.4) is the plain object marker sitting where the sense wants a preposition of place, which is why translations quietly supply at or beside in front of Your altars.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 5 does not stay inside this psalm. The daily prayer called Ashrei is Psalm 145 with two verses attached in front of it, and the first of those two is this one; Berakhot 4b is the passage usually cited for saying that psalm three times a day. A second reading works on the heading. Korah is swallowed by the earth in Numbers 16, and Numbers 26:11 records flatly that his sons did not die, so the guild that sings the most homesick psalm in the book descends from a man who lost his place at the sanctuary permanently and whose children kept theirs. A third reading works on verse 7: the valley is not removed and not avoided, it is converted by being walked through, and the springs can be read as something the pilgrims leave behind them for whoever walks the road next.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical centre of this psalm is the bird, not the pilgrim. צִפּוֹר (tzi-por, v.4) and וּדְרוֹר (ud-ror, v.4) have no standing, no offering and no invitation, and they are the only creatures in the poem already at the altar, which supports a reading in which nearness is not produced by the journey the rest of the psalm describes. The dwelling places of מִשְׁכְּנוֹתֶיךָ (mish-kno-tei-cha, v.2) are plural while the house of בְּבֵית (be-veit, v.11) is singular, and one line of mystical reading treats the plural as the levels through which a single place is approached rather than as several buildings. Then there is verse 12, which calls God a sun. The book calls God a shield often; this is the only verse in Tehilim that calls Him a sun, and the pairing gives the two things a plant needs and cannot arrange for itself. The last word of the psalm is a two letter word meaning in You. Courts, house, altars and threshold have all been replaced by a preposition.
