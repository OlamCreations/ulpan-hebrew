Yom Shabbaton: A day of rest not to be forgotten

Shabbat day, at the second meal · יוֹם שַׁבָּתוֹן
HomeYom Shabbaton
Judah Halevi wrote this, and the refrain is the reason to learn it: the dove found rest there, and there the exhausted will rest. The dove is Noah's, sent out over the water and coming back because there was nowhere to land — and the poem makes the Sabbath the place she was looking for. Every single line of the poem, refrain included, ends on the same syllable, which is a piece of technical showing-off you can hear without knowing any Hebrew at all.

The last line names the dove's flood, which the refrain has been alluding to for five stanzas without saying so. נֹחַ (no-ach) is Noah, and מֵי (mei) his waters — the oath is Isaiah 54, where God swears never to flood the earth again. The poem has been building to this word since its first refrain.

♫ Singing notes

  • The refrain is eight words and returns five times. It is also the only part with a name in it, so it is the part people remember.
  • Every line of the whole poem ends on the same syllable. If you are singing by ear and a line does not land on it, you have gone wrong.
  • Around 96 BPM, steady. Slower than Yom Zeh Mechubad, which usually precedes it at the same table.
  • Sung at lunch. Some tables take it after the fish and before the meat, which is as good a reason as any.

♦ About this song

Judah Halevi

Eleventh and twelfth century, Spain, and the poet whose name is attached to more Hebrew verse still sung today than almost anyone's. The Daat edition names him in a closing line, which is editorial matter rather than part of the song and was removed by the scaffold.

The dove

Genesis 8: the dove goes out from the ark, finds no resting place for the sole of her foot, and comes back. The poem takes the word for that resting place and makes the Sabbath into it, then waits until the last line of the last stanza to mention the flood at all.

One rhyme, twenty-five lines

A monorhyme of this length is a formal exercise inherited from Arabic poetry, and Halevi was working in a tradition where it was expected. The effect on a singer is practical rather than decorative: it makes the whole poem one continuous shape instead of five separate stanzas.
