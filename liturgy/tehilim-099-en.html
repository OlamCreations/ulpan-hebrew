Tehilim 99: The LORD reigns; let the peoples tremble

תְּהִלִּים צ״ט · יְהֹוָה מָלָךְ
HomePs 99
Nine verses, said on Friday evening as the fifth of the six psalms that open Kabbalat Shabbat, and the last of the group of enthronement psalms that runs from 93 through here. It is built around a refrain that says holy three times, at the end of verse 3, the end of verse 5 and inside verse 9, and those three points cut the psalm into three sections of decreasing distance: the peoples, then the footstool, then three men with names. For a learner it is a compact drill in the plural, singular and possessive of one noun: a people, the peoples, all the peoples, and then the same consonants again as a verb meaning He answered them.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three sections marked by three closing refrains. Verses 1 to 3 are the widest shot, kingship announced to the peoples and the earth. Verses 4 and 5 narrow to what the kingship consists of, justice established in Jacob, and to a place, the footstool. Verses 6 to 9 narrow again to three named men who spoke and were answered. Watch the pronoun in the refrain: קָדוֹשׁ (ka-dosh, v.3) and קָדוֹשׁ (ka-dosh, v.5) both say holy is He, and קָדוֹשׁ (ka-dosh, v.9) says the LORD our God is holy, so the third repetition drops the pronoun and names Him. Watch the location too. Verse 5 sends the worshipper to the footstool and verse 9 to the mountain, which is the same instruction given twice with the second one closer in.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 4 is the standing crux of the psalm. Its first words, וְעֹז (ve-oz, v.4) and מֶלֶךְ (me-lech, v.4), sit in the sentence without an obvious verb, and the readings divide: some take the strength of the king as a continuation of the praise in verse 3, some read it as a fresh subject with the loving of justice as its predicate, and some translations insert a break and start over at You have established. The dispute is real and the Hebrew supports more than one solution. In verse 8, נֹשֵׂא (no-se, v.8) is a participle from the root meaning to lift or carry, used elsewhere of carrying away guilt, while וְנֹקֵם (ve-no-kem, v.8) is the participle for taking vengeance, and it is contested whether their deeds means the wrongs the fathers committed or the wrongs done to them. Note also לָמוֹ (la-mo, v.7), a poetic form of the ordinary word for to them, which appears mostly in older verse and marks the register of the line.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 6 calls Moses one of His priests, which is a problem the Torah does not obviously allow, since the priesthood is given to Aaron and his sons. The traditional answer is that Moses served as priest during the seven days of the Tabernacle's consecration, before the office passed to Aaron's line, so the verse is exact rather than loose. The grouping is itself an argument. Moses, Aaron and Samuel are three men remembered chiefly for standing between God and a people about to be punished, and they are produced immediately after verse 5 has put everyone on the ground at the footstool. The psalm answers the question a reader would ask at that point, which is whether anything said from that position is ever heard. Verse 6 says it was, and verse 8 says the answering did not cancel the accounting.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The word holy appears three times, and Isaiah 6:3 has the same threefold holy in the mouths of the seraphim. The psalm gives no sign of quoting Isaiah, and the resemblance may be no more than a shared liturgical habit, but the pairing has shaped how the psalm is heard. Read with the enthronement psalms that precede it, the sequence moves from a throne above the flood to a throne on the cherubim to a throne with a footstool that human beings are told to touch their heads to, so the mystical reading takes the psalm as a controlled descent rather than a description of height. לַהֲדֹם (la-ha-dom, v.5), the footstool, is elsewhere in Scripture the ark and the Temple, which puts the lowest point of the divine throne inside a room in Jerusalem. That is the whole geography of the psalm: exalted above all the peoples in verse 2, and reachable at floor level by verse 5.
