Tehilim 122: I rejoiced when they said to me

תְּהִלִּים קכ״ב · שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת
HomePs 122
One of the fifteen Songs of Ascents, and the one that describes arriving. The speaker is quoted secondhand in verse 1, standing inside the gates by verse 2, and from verse 6 he stops describing the city and starts talking to it. That turn is the grammar lesson: from verse 6 to the end almost every line carries a second person feminine singular suffix, your gates, those who love you, your walls, your palaces, within you, for you, because the addressee is a city and cities are feminine in Hebrew. In many Ashkenazi communities the Songs of Ascents are said after Minchah on the Shabbatot between Sukkot and Passover.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Nine verses in two halves. Verses 1 to 5 look at Jerusalem and report: an invitation, feet in the gates, a compact city, tribes coming up, thrones for judgment. Verses 6 to 9 turn and speak to it. The two halves are joined by what the city is for, which the psalm gives twice, once as worship in verse 4 and once as justice in verse 5, and then repeats in reverse at the end, friends in verse 8 and the house in verse 9. Notice that עֹמְדוֹת (om-dot, v.2) and הָיוּ (hai-u, v.2) are a participle plus a past tense verb, a construction that holds an action open in time, so the standing is not a snapshot.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 6 is built on one consonant cluster repeated four times: שַׁאֲלוּ (sha-a-lu, v.6) ask, שְׁלוֹם (shlom, v.6) the peace of, יְרוּשָׁלָ͏ִם (ye-ru-sha-la͏m, v.6) Jerusalem, יִשְׁלָיוּ (yish-lai-u, v.6) may they be at ease. The name of the city carries the sound of peace inside it, and the verse makes that audible rather than arguing it. Two words are commonly mistranslated. בְּחֵילֵךְ (be-chei-lech, v.7) is a rampart, the outer defensive work, not wealth, though the two are spelled alike. שֶׁחֻבְּרָה (she-chub-ra, v.3) is a passive of the verb to join, so the city is joined to itself, which older translations render compact. The name in יְרוּשָׁלָ͏ִם (ye-ru-sha-la͏m, v.2) is written short in the consonants and pointed as a dual by the Masoretes, and what the ending means, two cities or an old locative form, is disputed.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The two institutions of verses 4 and 5, the pilgrimage and the court, are what made the city a destination, and later readers took the pairing as a claim that worship without justice has no address. The joining of verse 3 attracted a long line of reading in which the city below is matched by a city above and the two are fastened to each other, so that praying for Jerusalem's peace is not local politics. That reading is repeated widely in later literature and is not stated in the verse itself. The instruction in verse 6 is one of the few in the book that tells you what to say rather than what to feel, and it has been treated as an obligation on anyone who benefits from the place, which is the logic verse 8 spells out.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the mystical reading the city is a receiver, which is why the whole second half addresses it in the feminine and asks that it be filled rather than that it act. Verse 7 names two vessels in order, the rampart and the palaces, and asks for a different quality in each, peace at the boundary and stillness inside, which is the standard shape of a container being prepared. The joining of שֶׁחֻבְּרָה (she-chub-ra, v.3) is read as the seam between what is above and what is below, and the ascents of the heading as the fifteen steps of that climb. The last word of the psalm is for you, so the whole movement ends by handing everything it gathered back to the place it came for.
