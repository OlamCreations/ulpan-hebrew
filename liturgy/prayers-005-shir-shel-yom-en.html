Shir Shel Yom: The Psalm of the Day

שִׁיר שֶׁל יוֹם · end of every Shacharit · 7 psalms, one per weekday · Temple-era heritage
HomeShir Shel Yom
The Psalm of the Day, a different psalm assigned to each day of the week, recited at the end of every morning Shacharit service. The custom dates back to Temple times: the Levites, the singers of the Temple, sang a specific psalm at the end of each day's sacrificial service. Each day's psalm was chosen because of a thematic link to a day of creation. After the Temple's destruction (70 CE), the rabbis preserved the seven psalms as the closing element of daily morning prayer, a verbal memory of the Levites' liturgy. On any given morning, you can identify what day it is by which psalm is opened. This page lists all 7 with their opening lines; full word-by-word for each psalm comes in the Tehilim section.

♦ Lesson

  • When, end of every Shacharit service. After Aleinu, just before the closing hymns. A single psalm per day, ~30-60 seconds.
  • The 7 days and their psalms:
    Sunday (יום א')Psalm 24creation day 1, light over chaos; "the earth is the LORD's".
    Monday (יום ב')Psalm 48creation day 2, the firmament/divider; Jerusalem the city of God.
    Tuesday (יום ג')Psalm 82creation day 3, dry land + plants; God judges the judges.
    Wednesday (יום ד')Psalm 94creation day 4, sun, moon, stars (rulers of time); God of justice.
    Thursday (יום ה')Psalm 81creation day 5, fish and birds; joy of song.
    Friday (יום ו')Psalm 93creation day 6, humanity created; God reigns clothed in majesty.
    ShabbatPsalm 92creation day 7, rest; the only psalm whose superscription names a day.
  • Mishnah Tamid 7:4, the original list of the seven psalms appears in the Mishnah (~2nd c. CE) describing the Temple's daily routine. "On the first day of the week they would say 'The earth is the LORD's and its fullness' (Ps 24)...". The Mishnah explains each: "on the first day, because He created and possessed and ruled over the world".
  • How to identify the day from the Psalm, at the start of each psalm, the prayer leader announces: "Today is the X day of the week, on which the Levites used to sing in the Temple...". The day-of-week name acts as a counter, useful when traveling or jet-lagged.
  • Shabbat is special, Psalm 92 is the only psalm in the entire Tanakh whose title (verse 1) names a day: "Mizmor shir l'yom haShabbat" ("A psalm, a song for the Sabbath day"). The other six psalms are assigned to weekdays by rabbinic tradition; Psalm 92 was born for Shabbat by the psalmist himself.
  • Sephardi addition, Sephardi communities precede the daily psalm with Lamenatze'ach (Psalm 8:5) and other introductory verses. Ashkenazim go directly to the day's psalm. Both end with the same closing.

♦ About this prayer

A 2,500-year-old rotation of seven Psalms across the days of the week, surviving from Temple practice into daily synagogue liturgy. The compact mnemonic that makes every morning slightly different from the one before.

Temple origin: Mishnah Tamid

The Mishnah, tractate Tamid chapter 7, describes the daily service in the Second Temple in detail. After the morning tamid (continual offering), the Levites, the temple choristers, would ascend the platform and sing the day's psalm with full musical accompaniment. The Mishnah names each psalm and explains why each one fits its day, mostly by linking to that day of the creation week (Sunday = day 1 of creation, etc.). After the Temple was destroyed in 70 CE, the rabbis preserved the seven-psalm rotation as part of the post-Temple synagogue liturgy.

Why these specific psalms

The Mishnah's explanations connect each psalm to a day of creation:

  • Sunday Psalm 24, "The earth is the LORD's". On day 1, God acquired the world.
  • Monday Psalm 48, "Great is the LORD in the city of our God". On day 2 He divided waters above from below, implicit in the divided Jerusalem of the psalm.
  • Tuesday Psalm 82, "God stands in the assembly". On day 3 dry land emerged, the stage on which God's law would later be given.
  • Wednesday Psalm 94, "God of vengeance". On day 4 the sun, moon, and stars were placed, and (the Talmud explains) future star-worshipers would be punished.
  • Thursday Psalm 81, "Sing joyfully". On day 5 birds and fish were created, the singers of nature.
  • Friday Psalm 93, "The LORD reigns". On day 6 humans were created; God's kingship over a creature with free will began.
  • Shabbat Psalm 92, "A psalm for the Sabbath". The day of rest itself.

How the Levites sang in the Temple

The Temple service was musical. There were ~12 Levites singing on a daily basis (more on festivals), accompanied by musical instruments, the harp (kinor), lyre (nevel), cymbals (tziltzelim), trumpets (chatzotzrot). The Levites stood on a wooden platform (duchan) overlooking the courtyard where the sacrifices were offered. After each day's morning sacrifice, the Levites would sing the day's psalm. Crowds would respond. The performance lasted ~10-15 minutes.

After the Temple's destruction, this musical tradition mostly fell silent, the Sages forbade instrumental music in synagogues as a sign of mourning for the Temple. The psalms remained, but the orchestra was gone. Today, in synagogues across the world, the seven psalms are spoken or chanted simply, without instrumental accompaniment, every morning, the verbal trace of a vanished orchestral liturgy.
