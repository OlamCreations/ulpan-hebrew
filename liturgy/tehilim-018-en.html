Tehilim 18: I love You, LORD, my strength

תְּהִלִּים י״ח · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְעֶבֶד
HomePs 18
Fifty-one verses, the longest psalm in the first book of the Psalter, and a royal thanksgiving built like a report: I was drowning, God came down in a storm, God pulled me out, and here is what I did afterwards. A second copy of this poem stands in the Bible as 2 Samuel 22, and the two versions differ from each other in wording and spelling throughout. For a learner it is the best single training ground for the object suffix on a verb: the -ni ending, me, lands on more than twenty verbs here, and once you hear it you can read half the Psalter. The middle section, verses 8 to 16, is a storm theophany, so the vocabulary is weather and geology rather than prayer.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A campaign report in five movements. The heading dates it (verse 1); a burst of titles declares who was called on (2 to 4); the speaker sinks under water and rope until he shouts (5 to 7); God comes down in a storm that dismantles the landscape (8 to 16); and one hand pulls one man out of the water (17 to 20). The rest is aftermath: a legal claim that the rescue was deserved (21 to 25), a general rule about how God meets people (26 to 31), then training, battle, pursuit and dominion (32 to 46), closing in praise (47 to 51). Two structural facts do the work. First, the distress of verse 7 is built on the root meaning narrow and the rescue of verse 20 on the root meaning wide, so the whole first half is a movement from a tight place to an open one. Second, verse 39 puts the enemies under my feet, and verse 10 had put thick darkness under His feet; the psalm arranges its own echo and does not comment on it.

רמז · Remez: allusion

This poem exists twice in the Bible, here and as 2 Samuel 22, and the two copies differ from each other in wording and spelling from beginning to end. The most visible consequence is liturgical: Grace after Meals ends with this psalm's last verse on ordinary days, using the participle מַגְדִּל (mag-dil, v.51), and switches to the noun form of the parallel verse in Samuel on Shabbat and festivals, so a diner meets both versions of the same line in one week. Four cruxes are worth marking. The verb אֶרְחׇמְךָ (er-chom-cha, v.2) is the ordinary root for compassion, but in a stem it almost never takes elsewhere in the Bible, which is why translations range from I love You to I am moved toward You. The word בְלִיַּעַל (vli-ya-al, v.5) is usually explained as worthlessness or as something like without escape, and it later hardens into a proper name for a demonic figure; the derivation is disputed. In verse 15 the single word רָב (rav, v.15) can be read as an adjective, lightnings in abundance, or as a verb, He shot, and both readings are in print. Hardest is וְעַנְוַתְךָ (ve-an-vat-cha, v.36) in verse 36: it is derived either from the root for answering or from the root for lowliness, giving either Your response or Your stooping made me great, and no reading has won.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verses 26 and 27 are the passage everything else in the psalm gets read through. Four times the same construction appears: an adjective describing a person, then a verb built from that adjective in the stem that means to act a quality out, with God as its subject. With the faithful You act faithfully, with the pure You act purely, and then, without warning, with the crooked You act twisted. Read plainly it says God takes the moral shape of whoever approaches Him. The traditional handling of the last clause is to make it a description of consequence rather than of God's nature: a crooked person meets a world that keeps turning out crooked, and the crookedness he meets is his own returned. The same logic organises the two halves of the psalm. In verse 7 the speaker cries out and is heard; in verse 42 his enemies cry out with the same verb and are not answered. Read against verse 21, where the speaker claims the rescue was earned, the psalm is arguing that deliverance is not arbitrary favour but a mirror. Note also that the lamp of נֵרִי (ne-ri, v.29) is not a stray image: elsewhere in the historical books God promises David a lamp in Jerusalem, meaning a surviving descendant on the throne, which is where verse 51 ends up.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The theophany hides more than it reveals, and it says so. In verse 12 God makes חֹשֶׁךְ (cho-shech, v.12), darkness, into a hiding place and pitches it as a shelter, so the closer He comes the less can be seen; the light of verse 13 arrives only as a brightness breaking out of cloud from the inside. Mystical reading takes this as the standing rule for divine approach: concealment is not the absence of presence but its working condition, and the storm is a garment. The cherub of כְּרוּב (kruv, v.11) places the psalm in the same image family as the chariot vision of Ezekiel, the text that later becomes the seed of merkavah mysticism, and here that chariot is in motion for the sake of one drowning man. The pivot at verse 16 belongs to the same reading: the moment the world is stripped to its foundations is also the moment the psalm stops saying He and starts saying You, so the undoing of the created order and the arrival of direct address are described as one event. Verse 51 then extends the whole thing past the speaker, from the anointed king to his seed forever, which is how the poem stops being one man's rescue.
