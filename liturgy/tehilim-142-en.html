Tehilim 142: You are my shelter in the land of the living

תְּהִלִּים קמ״ב · מַשְׂכִּיל לְדָוִד
HomePs 142
Eight verses of one man talking, with the heading placing him in a cave. It is the smallest complete lament in the book: two verses of crying out, two of describing the situation, one of turning, one of asking, one of what happens afterwards. For a learner it is dense with the first person singular, the possessive suffix -i on nouns and the prefix a- on verbs, so almost every line says my something or I do something. Watch how the pronouns behave: the psalm speaks about God until verse 4, then says You and never goes back.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

A lament with all the standard parts and nothing extra. Verses 2 and 3 are the cry, described rather than quoted. Verses 4 and 5 are the situation: a spirit failing, a hidden trap, and the discovery that the place where a defender should stand is empty. Verse 6 is the turn, and it is marked grammatically as well as emotionally, the verbs switching to completed action for the first time. Verse 7 is the request, held back until almost the end, with two reasons given, both of them concessions of weakness. Verse 8 is the outcome, and it is the only verse with other people in it. Note what does not happen: the enemies are never named, no wrong is alleged against them, and the speaker never claims to be innocent.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three places where the Hebrew is harder than any translation admits. The image in בְּהִתְעַטֵּף (be-hit-a-tef, v.4) is a verb of wrapping, so the spirit is muffled rather than merely weak, and the same idiom for a spirit wrapping itself over its owner recurs in Psalm 143:4. The imperative of הַבֵּיט (ha-beit, v.5) does not say who is being addressed: read as speech to God it is a challenge, read as speech to a bystander it is a man pointing at the space beside him, and translations split on it. The verb of יַכְתִּרוּ (yach-ti-ru, v.8) is the difficulty everyone agrees on: it is built on the root for crown, and it is variously rendered as the righteous will crown themselves, will crown me, will surround me, or will gather about me. The choice changes who is honoured in the last line of the psalm, and the text does not decide it.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The heading at בַמְּעָרָה (vam-a-ra, v.1) invites the psalm to be read against the narratives in 1 Samuel where David hides in a cave, the escape to Adullam in chapter 22 and the encounter with Saul at En Gedi in chapter 24. The text does not say which, and both have been proposed. Reading it against chapter 24 is the sharper option: there David is the one holding a hiding place while his pursuer walks in unaware, so the man praying that there is no escape for him is, at that moment, the one with the advantage. That gap between the felt situation and the actual one is what the tradition takes from the psalm, along with the fact that מַחְסִי (mach-si, v.6) is spoken from inside a cave, where shelter is exactly what a man already has and still does not trust.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes מִמַּסְגֵּר (mi-mas-ger, v.8), the confinement, as the body and the exile rather than a prison of stone, and the soul brought out of it as the soul returning to a range of movement it lost rather than escaping a place. On that reading the portion in the land of the living in verse 6 is not survival but an allotment in a world that does not end, claimed by a man who at that moment owns nothing at all. The cave then does double duty: the narrow place a person is pressed into, and the enclosure inside which a prayer becomes audible because there is nowhere else for the voice to go.
