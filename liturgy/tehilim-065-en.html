Tehilim 65: To You silence is praise

תְּהִלִּים ס״ה · לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר
HomePs 65
Fourteen verses in three movements: a temple that hears prayer and settles sin (2 to 5), a God who steadies mountains and quiets seas (6 to 9), and a rainy season that ends in a harvest loud enough to be called singing (10 to 14). The grammar to watch is the object suffix. From verse 10 on, nearly every verb carries a feminine ending pointing back at the land: You watered her, You enrich her, You soften her, You bless her growth. The psalm also crosses the whole range of speech, opening on the word for silence in verse 2 and closing on the verb for they sing in verse 14.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements, each with its own setting. Verses 2 to 5 stand inside the temple: prayer is heard, sin is admitted, a person is chosen and brought near. Verses 6 to 9 walk out of the building to mountains, seas and the far ends of the earth, held to the first movement by one verb, You answer us. Verses 10 to 14 are a rainy season, from the first watering to a harvest that shouts. The frame is the clearest signal in the psalm: the second verse gives God silence, דֻמִיָּה (du-mi-ya, v.2), and the last word of the last verse is they sing, יָשִׁירוּ (ya-shi-ru, v.14). Nothing between those two points argues that the silence was wrong. The noise is what the silence turns into once it has passed through soil.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three cruxes worth carrying. The first is דֻמִיָּה (du-mi-ya, v.2). Read from the root for being silent it gives silence is praise to You; read from the root for waiting it gives praise waits for You, and JPS 1917 took the second. The consonants do not settle it and both readings are in use, so the verse is genuinely disputed rather than merely ambiguous. The second is verse 11, where רַוֵּה (ra-ve, v.11) and נַחֵת (na-chet, v.11) are imperative in form. A reader can hear two commands aimed at God dropped into the middle of a description, or a description that keeps going in a clipped form; translations split, and JPS 1917 smoothed them into participles. The third is כָרִים (cha-rim, v.14), spelled alike as the plural of the word for a lamb and the plural of the word for a meadow, so the line is either meadows clothed with the flock or rams clothed as the flock's covering. JPS 1917 chose meadows.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 4 does something easy to miss on a first reading. The speaker says the record of wrongs is too strong for me, singular, and in the next clause says our transgressions, plural. A long-standing way of reading takes the singular as a representative voice, one person carrying a community's account to the one place built to settle it, which is exactly what verses 2 to 5 describe. Verse 3 is read in the same expansive direction: all flesh comes, not all Israel, so the house in Zion is treated as the address of everyone who prays. The last movement is read as the rainy season in the land rather than as weather anywhere, which makes the psalm a thanksgiving said after a year that came out well, not a request for one.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes the psalm as a description of descent. Silence at the top, דֻמִיָּה (du-mi-ya, v.2), because at that level anything said is already inaccurate; then a name, a house, courts, rain, grain, and finally noise in a field. Each stage is coarser than the one above it, and each one is the stage above made available to something that could not have received it directly. The channel of God in פֶּלֶג (pe-leg, v.10) is the working image for that, a measured flow rather than a flood, since what is above arrives only in the width of the pipe. On this reading the shouting of verse 14 is not a lesser thing than the silence of verse 2. It is the same content, arriving in the only form a valley can hold.
