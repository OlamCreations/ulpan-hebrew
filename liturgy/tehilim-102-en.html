Tehilim 102: A prayer of the afflicted

תְּהִלִּים ק״ב · תְּפִלָּה לְעָנִי
HomePs 102
Twenty nine verses, and the superscription names no author, only a condition: a prayer for whoever is faint and pouring out his complaint. That is why the psalm has no fixed occasion. It fits any of them. The vocabulary is the vocabulary of a body giving out, bones, heart, skin, sleep, bread and drink, and almost every noun in the first half carries a first person suffix. Then verse 13 says but You, and the psalm changes subject to Zion and to the age of the world without ever resolving the illness it started with.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three panels. Verses 2 to 12 are a body giving out, measured throughout in days rather than in symptoms. Verses 13 to 23 leave the body completely and speak of Zion rebuilt and the nations assembling, in a future stated as though already done. Verses 24 to 29 drop back to the speaker, who asks not to be taken in the middle of his life and is answered with the age of the world. The hinge is וְאַתָּה (ve-a-ta, v.13), but You, and everything after it runs on the contrast between a lifespan and a throne. Watch the word for my days: it appears in verses 4, 12, 24 and 25, and it marks every seam in the poem.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The birds of verse 7 are traditionally rendered pelican and owl and the identifications are disputed; what the two words carry for certain is habitat rather than species, one fixed to wilderness and one to ruins, which is the whole point of the comparison. נְשָׂאתַנִי (ne-sa-ta-ni, v.11) sharpens the complaint by the order of its verbs: You lifted me up and then threw me down, so the height was part of the fall. נִבְרָא (niv-ra, v.19) uses the verb of Genesis for a people not yet created, which turns the line from a prediction into a promise. And תַּעֲלֵנִי (ta-a-le-ni, v.25) literally says do not bring me up, understood as do not take me away, one of the several ways the language declines to name death directly.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

הָעַרְעָר (ha-ar-ar, v.18) is a rare word for the stripped and destitute, and the same root is used elsewhere in the Bible for a bare plant in the desert; the traditional reading holds both senses at once, a man with nothing and a shrub with no water, which matches the birds of verse 7. On נִבְרָא (niv-ra, v.19), one reading takes the people yet to be created to be a generation remade by repentance, on the argument that a people already in existence does not need creating. Verse 19 is also where the psalm becomes aware of itself: it asks to be written for a generation not yet born, and that request is the reason a single sick man's complaint was kept as a public text rather than as a private one.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Verse 27 supplies the image the mystical readings work with: worlds wear out like a garment and are exchanged, and the one who changes them is not changed by the change. כַּלְּבוּשׁ (kal-vush, v.27) is clothing, and clothing in this register is what a thing shows rather than what it is, so a world that ages is a covering that has served its term. הוּא (hu, v.28) then says only You are He, two words with nothing predicated of them, treated as the point where description has to stop. The ending fits that reading: the speaker is not healed, and what endures is the line of servants after him, continuity taking the place of cure.
