Tehilim 137: By the rivers of Babylon

תְּהִלִּים קל״ז · עַל נַהֲרוֹת
HomePs 137
Nine verses, the only psalm that names the exile as something the speakers are living in rather than praying about, and the only one that ends where this one ends. In many communities it is said before the grace after meals on ordinary weekdays, with Psalm 126 taking its place on Shabbat and festivals, so the two psalms divide the week between memory of loss and memory of return. For a learner the page is a drill in the first person plural: we sat, we wept, we hung, our lyres, our captors, our tormentors, seven forms of the -nu suffix in the first three verses. Then verse 5 switches to the singular and stays there, and that switch is the psalm.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements. Verses 1 to 4 are plural and set in Babylon: a group by the water, instruments hung up, a demand for entertainment, and a question. Verses 5 and 6 are a single voice under oath, and they are the only part of the psalm addressed to Jerusalem rather than about her. Verses 7 to 9 turn outward and ask for reckoning against two enemies, Edom for what it said and Babylon for what it did. Watch the verb remember. In בְּזׇכְרֵנוּ (bzoch-re-nu, v.1) it is what the exiles do and it produces weeping; in אֶזְכְּרֵכִי (ez-kre-chi, v.6) it is what the speaker swears never to stop doing; in זְכֹר (ze-chor, v.7) it is what God is asked to do, and there it produces judgement. One root carries the psalm from grief to oath to demand.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 5 says only let my right hand forget, with no object, and the omission is deliberate enough that translations supply one, most often her skill. Some read the verb differently and take it as wither or become useless rather than forget, and both readings are current; what is not in dispute is that the hand named is the one that stops a lyre string. הַשְּׁדוּדָה (hash-du-da, v.8) is a passive form, the one laid waste, which sits oddly on a city still standing at the time, and readings divide between a prophetic past, an address to a city already doomed, and an emendation to the one who lays waste. וְתוֹלָלֵינוּ (ve-to-la-lei-nu, v.3), our tormentors, is rare enough that its sense is argued over. The two closing verses both open with אַשְׁרֵי (ash-rei, v.8) and אַשְׁרֵי (ash-rei, v.9), the word that opens the whole book of Psalms in verse 1 of Psalm 1, so the vocabulary of blessing is used for the thing furthest from blessing.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 6 is the source of the line sung at Jewish weddings, and the custom of breaking a glass under the canopy is regularly explained by it: Jerusalem is set above the head of any joy, so the largest private joy a person has is required to carry a mark of the city's ruin. Read against verse 2 the oath also completes an argument. The hands that hung up the lyres in Babylon are the hands put at risk in verse 5, so the silence that began as a refusal becomes a permanent obligation. The final verses have never been comfortable, and the tradition has handled them mostly by leaving them in place while reading them as a report of what people in that condition say. What the psalm records is not policy but the shape grief takes when the people who caused it have gone unpunished.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The wish in verse 9 is not invented by the psalm. Isaiah 13:16 and Nahum 3:10 both describe infants dashed to pieces as the standard end of a sacked city, and the second of those is about a city that had done the sacking. The curse therefore asks for the ordinary fate of empires and asks for nothing worse, which is the only defence the plain sense offers. The mystical reading takes the whole psalm as a statement about where song comes from. Verses 1 to 4 establish that the temple repertoire cannot be produced on demand or on foreign ground, verses 5 and 6 make the speaker's own voice conditional on the city, and verses 7 to 9 are what is left when the singing stops. On that reading כִּנֹּרוֹתֵינוּ (ki-no-ro-tei-nu, v.2), our lyres, are hung up not out of defeat but because the instrument and the place are one thing, and the psalm ends in the register that remains available.
