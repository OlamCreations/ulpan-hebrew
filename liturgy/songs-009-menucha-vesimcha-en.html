Menucha VeSimcha: Rest and joy

Friday night, at the table · מְנוּחָה וְשִׂמְחָה
HomeMenucha VeSimcha
One of the standard Friday night zemirot, and the one that argues rather than praises. Its claim is that the Sabbath is evidence — that the people who keep it and remember it are witnesses to how the world was made, and that the day itself is the testimony. Five stanzas, each rhyming on one sound throughout, and the acrostic of the first letters spells a name that is no longer certain.

שׁוֹמְרָיו (shom-rav) and וְזוֹכְרָיו (ve-zoch-rav) are the two verbs of the two versions of the fourth commandment — keep it, in Deuteronomy, and remember it, in Exodus. The poem does not choose between them either; it makes the people who do both into witnesses, which is a legal word, and מְעִידִים (me-i-dim) is the legal term for testimony.

בְּמִשְׁנֶה (be-mish-ne) is the two loaves on the table, named as the poem is being sung over them. It closes on הָעוֹלָם (ha-o-lam) and הַבָּא (ha-ba) — the world to come — so the last stanza runs from the bread in front of you to the end of history in four lines.

♫ Singing notes

  • Brisk. Around 108 BPM, faster than Tzur Mishelo, and it works well early in the meal while people still have energy.
  • Every line of a stanza rhymes with every other. If a line does not land on that sound, you have skipped one.
  • There is no refrain, which is what makes this one harder for guests than Tzur Mishelo — a table that does not know it will not join in halfway.
  • The last line of each stanza is where the melody resolves. Let it sit before starting the next.

♦ About this song

The argument

The song's claim is unusual for a table song: that Sabbath observance is evidence for creation, and that the observers are witnesses in the legal sense. It states the claim in the first stanza, produces the inventory of what was made in the second, and only then gets to the practicalities.

Keep and remember

Exodus says remember the Sabbath day; Deuteronomy says keep it. The difference has been discussed for two thousand years, and this poem simply uses both in one line and moves on.

Authorship

The acrostic spells a name, and which Moshe it refers to is not settled. Unlike Dror Yikra, whose author signed it and is known, this one carries a signature that no longer identifies anybody with certainty.
