Hadlakat Nerot: Shabbat Candle Lighting

הַדְלָקַת נֵרוֹת · Friday · 18 min before sunset · the start of Shabbat
HomeHadlakat Nerot
Shabbat candle lighting, said by the woman of the house (or, where there is no woman, by a man) 18 minutes before sunset on Friday. The traditional time-cushion (~18 min) lets you light before Shabbat itself begins, since once Shabbat starts you cannot kindle fire. Light at least two candles (recalling the two commands: zachor "remember" Shabbat and shamor "keep" Shabbat). Some families add a candle for each child. Cover the eyes with the hands, say the blessing, uncover the eyes to see the light already burning. After the blessing, Shabbat has begun for the lighter, even if the rest of the household hasn't yet welcomed it.

♦ Lesson

  • When, 18 minutes before sunset Friday evening (or 40 min in Jerusalem). Local Shabbat times are published online; learn to look them up.
  • Who lights, traditionally the woman of the house. If there is no woman present, a man can and should light. Some communities (e.g. Chabad) encourage every girl from age 3 to light her own candle.
  • How many candles, at least 2. Many families add 1 candle per child. The candles should be substantial enough to burn ~3 hours (through the Friday night meal).
  • Sequence, the unusual reversal: usually you say a blessing first, then do the action. With Shabbat candles, you do the action first (light), then cover the eyes, say the blessing, uncover. Reason: saying the blessing already brings Shabbat in for the lighter, and once Shabbat is in you can't kindle. So the action precedes the blessing, a unique inversion in halacha.
  • Same template as Netilat Yadayim, the blessing structure is identical: Baruch Atah... asher kid'shanu... v'tzivanu... + the specific commandment. Once you know one, you know the skeleton of dozens.
  • Vocabulary: l'hadlik = hiphil infinitive of ד.ל.ק (to burn). Qal = "burn", hiphil = "cause to burn / kindle". Same root as delek (fuel) and delika (a fire). Ner = lamp/candle. Shel Shabbat = "of Shabbat", the particle shel marks possession or association, like English "of".

♦ About this prayer

The act that begins Shabbat in every Jewish home, two candles, two open hands, two minutes of quiet before the noise of the meal. The first thing a Jewish child sees of Shabbat.

Origin: Talmudic obligation

The obligation to light Shabbat candles is rabbinic (not Torah law) but ancient, discussed across tractate Shabbat of the Talmud. The blessing wording is from the Geonic period (~7th-10th c.). The rabbis tied the obligation primarily to women because of a midrash on Eve: the first woman "extinguished the candle of the world" (by causing the expulsion from Eden), so women are given the privilege of re-kindling the light of the world every week.

Why two candles

The Ten Commandments appear twice in the Torah, once in Exodus and once in Deuteronomy. The two versions differ slightly on the Shabbat commandment: Exodus says zachor ("remember"); Deuteronomy says shamor ("keep"). The rabbis say both words were spoken simultaneously by God (a divine paradox), and the two candles represent these two commands woven together.

Many families add candles for each child, each guest, or each family member. The minimum is two; there is no maximum.

The moment of silence after lighting

After saying the blessing and uncovering the eyes, many women take a quiet moment to add a personal prayer, for the family, for the children, for the week ahead, for whatever is heavy on the heart. This is one of the most cherished silent moments in the Jewish week. There is no fixed text, it is private, spontaneous, intimate. Some women cry into the candlelight. Some smile. Either is right.

For Sephardim and Mizrahi communities, the candles are sometimes lit at the table where the meal will be eaten; for Ashkenazim, often on a sideboard or a dedicated candle-area. Local custom varies. The blessing text is identical.
