Ma Nishtana: Why is this night different

Pesach seder, asked by the youngest at the table · מַה נִּשְׁתַּנָּה
HomeMa Nishtana
The four questions, asked at the seder by the youngest child who can read them, and the first long piece of Hebrew most people ever learn by heart. It is built to be learnable: one opening line, then four contrasts with identical grammar, so once you can hear the first you can predict all four. It is also not really four questions. There is one question — why is this night different — and four observations that answer it, each of the form on all other nights we do X, on this night Y.

On all other nights we eat either sitting up or reclining; this night, we all recline.
Four contrasts, one grammar. Each begins שֶׁבְּכָל (sheb-chal) — literally "that on all" — and each pivots on הַלַּיְלָה (ha-lai-la), "this night", with no verb at all in the second half. Hebrew does not need one: "this night — all of it matzah" is a complete sentence. Notice too that כֻּלּוֹ (ku-lo), כֻּלּוֹ (ku-lo) and כֻּלָּנוּ (ku-la-nu) are the same word wearing three endings: all of it, all of it, all of us. The suffix is doing the work a separate pronoun does in English.

♫ Singing notes

  • Traditionally the youngest person at the table who can manage it. If several children are close in age they usually do it together, and nobody minds.
  • It is chanted on a recitation tune rather than sung as a song, so the pace follows the reader, not the guitar. If you are playing, follow.
  • Each of the four has the same melodic shape, rising through the first half and falling on the second. Learn one, you have four.
  • The last one is the finish and is usually taken a little slower, so the table knows to answer.

♦ About this song

Where the four come from

The Mishnah in tractate Pesachim gives the child's questions, and its list is not identical to the one recited today — the question about roasted meat belonged to a time when the Passover offering was still brought, and it dropped out. The reclining question took its place.

Reclining

מְסֻבִּין (me-su-bin) and מְסֻבִּין (me-su-bin) describe eating propped on the left side, the posture of a free person at a Roman banquet. The fourth contrast is therefore not about food at all; it is about who you are while eating it, which is the point the whole evening is making.

Dipping twice

The two dippings are the karpas into salt water early in the evening and the bitter herb into charoset later. The question notices a detail of the choreography, which is what a child at the table would in fact notice.

The rubric that is not here

The Haggadah text on Sefaria carries a stage direction before this passage — remove the plate, pour the second cup, the child asks. It is instruction to the host, not words to be recited, and the scaffold dropped it rather than printing it as a line of the song.
