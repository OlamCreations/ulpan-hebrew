Tehilim 58: Is there a God who judges the earth

תְּהִלִּים נ״ח · לַמְנַצֵּחַ אַל
HomePs 58
Twelve verses aimed at judges who have stopped judging. It opens as a question put to the bench, runs through a chain of images for what corrupt power actually is, turns into a curse, and ends with a verdict spoken by ordinary bystanders. The psalm has no fixed place in the service, so most readers meet it going through the book in order. Two things to watch. The words al tashcheth in the heading, do not destroy, also head psalms 57, 59 and 75, and are usually taken as the name of a tune rather than a plea. And three of the four verbs addressed to the judges in verses 2 and 3 end in an extra nun, an old poetic ending that lengthens the line without changing the sense.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four movements. Verses 2 and 3 question a court and then answer their own question. Verses 4 to 6 describe the judges as a species rather than as people, estranged from the womb and deaf by choice. Verses 7 to 10 ask God to disarm them, and every request is a subtraction: teeth out, water drained, a snail gone, a birth that never saw daylight. Verses 11 and 12 step outside the courtroom and report what onlookers conclude. The frame is worth reading twice. The psalm opens with בְּנֵי (bnei, v.2) and אָדָם (a-dam, v.2), the sons of man, as the people being judged, and ends with אָדָם (a-dam, v.12), a man, as the one pronouncing the verdict on the judges. The bench and the public have changed places.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two cruxes and one grammatical tic. The first crux is אֵלֶם (e-lem, v.2). The Masoretic vocalization gives the noun for silence, which is how the older translations read the line, while many modern ones revocalize the same consonants as a plural for gods or mighty ones and take the line as a direct address to the judges themselves. The consonants allow both and the reading is disputed. The second is verse 10, where סִּירֹתֵכֶם (si-ro-te-chem, v.10), your pots, אָטָד (a-tad, v.10), thornbush, and חָרוֹן (cha-ron, v.10), which is the ordinary word for burning anger but here may only mean heat, combine into a sentence nobody has settled. The tic is the extra nun on תְּדַבֵּרוּן (te-da-be-run, v.2), תִּפְעָלוּן (tif-a-lun, v.3) and תְּפַלֵּסוּן (tfa-le-sun, v.3), a poetic ending that adds weight rather than meaning, and all three of those verbs are aimed at the judges.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The deaf cobra of verses 5 and 6 became a standard image for someone who has heard the argument and sealed the ear on purpose, which is a heavier charge than ignorance. The charmer of חוֹבֵר (cho-ver, v.6) and חֲבָרִים (cha-va-rim, v.6) is a working professional, not a magician in a story, and that is the force of the image: the technique exists, and this creature is immune to it. The last verse turns on a grammatical oddity. שֹׁפְטִים (shof-tim, v.12) is a plural participle, judging, attached to a subject usually treated as singular. The same word for God is used elsewhere in the Torah for human judges, in the courtroom passages of Exodus 22, so the closing line can be heard as saying that God judges the earth, or that there are judges in it after all. Which of the two the verse intends is not settled.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Read as a map of one faculty, the psalm is about the ear and the mouth. The judges are asked about their speech in verse 2, they weigh violence with their hands in verse 3, and by verse 5 they have become a creature that has closed the single opening through which correction could arrive, אׇזְנוֹ (oz-no, v.5), its ear. The requests of verses 7 to 10 all attack that same head: the teeth inside the mouth, then the fangs, then the whole body run off as water. What is asked for is not pain but the removal of an instrument. The close reverses the direction. Speech comes back, but from outside the court, and the psalm ends on a mouth that says something true after eleven verses of mouths that could not.
