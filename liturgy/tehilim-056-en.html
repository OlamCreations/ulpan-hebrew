Tehilim 56: In God I trust, I will not be afraid

תְּהִלִּים נ״ו · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 56
Fourteen verses of pressure, and the heading pins them to the worst moment of David's flight: seized by the Philistines in Gath, the city of the champion he had killed, where the book of Samuel has him feign madness to get out alive. The psalm answers that pressure with a refrain rather than an argument, saying the same sentence at verse 5 and again at verse 12 with one word changed. For a learner it is a drill in suffixes: nearly every verb of attack in the first half ends in -ni, me, and nearly every noun of comfort in the second half ends in -kha, Yours.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The shape is complaint, refrain, complaint, refrain, vow. Verses 2 and 3 describe the attack, 4 and 5 answer it, 6 to 8 describe it again from a different angle, and 9 to 12 answer it again and harder. The refrain is the load-bearing structure: verse 5 and verse 12 are the same sentence with one substitution, בָשָׂר (va-sar, v.5) becoming אָדָם (a-dam, v.12), flesh becoming a human being. Both words mean the same thing here, that the enemy is mortal, and saying it twice is the argument. The vow at verse 13 belongs to the same logic: a person who has decided the rescue is real starts owing thanks for it before it arrives.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 9 turns on sound. נֹדִי (no-di, v.9), my wandering, and בְנֹאדֶךָ (vno-de-cha, v.9), Your flask, are near-identical noises from unrelated roots, and the line puts them one after the other so that the wandering and the container for what the wandering produced rhyme. Two other places are genuinely unsettled. מָרוֹם (ma-rom, v.3) can be a vocative, O Most High, or an adverb describing how the enemies fight, arrogantly, and translations split on it. פַּלֶּט (pa-let, v.8) is harder still: read as a noun the line asks whether such people get to escape, read as an imperative it asks God to cast them out, and the difference is one of vocalization rather than consonants.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The tune name of the superscription, the silent dove of distant places, has long been read in the tradition as a description rather than a melody: Israel far from home and unable to speak for itself, which turns a psalm about one man surrounded in Gath into a psalm the community can say. The setting supports the reading, since the David of 1 Samuel 21 survives Gath precisely by making himself unintelligible, scratching at the gates and letting spit run down his beard. The counted wandering of verse 9 is then read as exile counted rather than one flight, and the tears kept in a flask as the claim that nothing of it is lost.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Verse 11 praises the same word twice, once under the name Elohim and once under the four-letter Name, and the standard reading of that pair takes the first as judgement and the second as mercy. The verse then says that the promise is worth praising whichever way it arrives, which is a harder claim than gratitude for rescue and is made before any rescue has happened. The flask and the record of verse 9 are two images of the same thing, a memory that keeps what the world discards, and in the mystical reading tears are not evidence of weakness but a measure being filled, which is why the psalm can ask for them to be stored rather than stopped.
