Tehilim 53: God looked down from heaven

תְּהִלִּים נ״ג · לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל
HomePs 53
This is Psalm 14 again, copied into a different collection and edited on the way. Read the two pages side by side and the edits are easy to list: the heading here is longer and counts as its own verse, so every line lands one number later; the divine name is God throughout where Psalm 14 alternates and uses the four letter name; verse 4 says the whole of it has turned back where Psalm 14 says the whole has turned aside; verse 5 drops the word all before the workers of iniquity; and the two short verses 5 and 6 of Psalm 14 are replaced by one long verse 6 about scattered bones, which shares only its opening three words with what it replaced. For a learner this is the cleanest evidence in the book that these texts were sung, copied and reworked rather than fixed once.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Take the psalm on its own and it moves in three steps. Verses 2 to 4 state the charge and repeat its refrain, verse 5 gives the charge victims, and verse 6 shows the accused collapsing in fear. Verse 7 then steps outside the argument to ask for a rescue that has not come. The engine of the first half is the negative existential particle, three times in verses 2 and 4: there is no God, there is none doing good, there is none, not even one. Note that the fool of נָבָל (na-val, v.2) never argues his case, he says it inwardly, and the psalm answers him with a God who looks in verse 3 rather than with a proof.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three words here are genuinely hard. The heading word מָחֲלַת (ma-cha-lat, v.1) occurs only in the headings of this psalm and one other, and there is no agreement on what it means: a tune, an instrument, or a form of the root for sickness. The verb סָג (sag, v.4) is not the verb Psalm 14 uses at the same point, and the change is one letter, which is exactly the kind of variation copying produces. Hardest is חֹנָךְ (cho-nach, v.6), which the Masoretes vocalize as one who encamps with a second person suffix, that is, the one besieging you, addressed to a listener who has not been introduced. Some readers repoint it, and the reading is disputed. The doubled construction in פָּחֲדוּ (pa-cha-du, v.6) and פַחַד (fa-chad, v.6), dreaded a dread, is a cognate accusative, Hebrew's normal way of intensifying a verb.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The pair of psalms is the case study readers return to. Psalms 42 to 83 are the stretch of the book where the divine name is largely replaced by the word God, and this psalm sits inside that stretch while its twin sits outside it, so the substitution visible between them is not a scribe's slip but a property of the collection each one landed in. On the content side, the denial of verse 2 has long been read not as atheism but as a calculation, the fool granting that there is a God and betting that God does not look, which is why verse 3 answers with looking and not with argument. Verse 6, with its unburied bones, reads as a specific military deliverance, and readers have attached it to more than one siege without the text naming any.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The mystical reading takes the fear of verse 6 seriously as its own event. Nothing is reported to have struck the wicked. They are afraid where there was nothing to be afraid of, and the psalm gives that state a cause outside them, which is what the second half of the verse supplies. Read this way the psalm describes a closed system meeting something it had ruled out, and the terror is the contact itself rather than any punishment. The ending then follows the usual shape: the deliverance comes out of Zion, from a fixed point that was there the whole time, and the two names in יַעֲקֹב (ya-a-kov, v.7) and יִשְׂרָאֵל (yis-ra-el, v.7) mark one people before and after the turning.
