Tehilim 86: Incline Your ear, LORD, and answer me

תְּהִלִּים פ״ו · תְּפִלָּה לְדָוִד
HomePs 86
Seventeen verses, the only psalm attributed to David in the third book of the Psalter, and a clean place to see how biblical Hebrew builds a plea: almost every line is an imperative followed by a reason clause opening with ki, for. Verse 8 is the sentence with which the Torah is taken from the ark on Shabbat, and verse 15 repeats the formula of the Thirteen Attributes from Exodus 34:6. Watch the divine names as you read: the title Adonai falls seven times, the four letter name four times, and the psalm changes register around them.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm alternates between asking and describing, and the joints are easy to see. Verses 1 to 7 ask, each imperative followed by a clause opening with ki, for. Verses 8 to 10 stop asking and describe God. Verse 11 asks for something new, a whole heart, and verse 12 vows the thanks. Verse 14 returns to the enemies, verse 15 answers them with the attribute formula, and verses 16 and 17 ask again. Read the reason clauses in order and you get the argument by itself: I am poor, I am devoted, I trust, I call all day, You are good, You will answer, Your kindness is great.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Verse 15 is a quotation. Its words are those of Exodus 34:6, and the psalm keeps the sequence compassionate, gracious, slow to anger, abundant in kindness and truth, then stops where Exodus continues. The rare word is יַחֵד (ya-ched, v.11), the verb translated here as make one. Its sense is contested: most read a plea for an undivided heart, while others attach it to a root of gladness, which would make the line ask for a glad heart rather than a single one. The page does not settle it. Note also אֲמָתֶךָ (a-ma-te-cha, v.16), son of Your maidservant, which is not a description of birth but a claim of belonging to a household.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The reception of this psalm turned one private verse into a public one. אֵין (ein, v.8) begins the sentence said when the ark is opened and the Torah taken out on Shabbat and festivals, so a line written by a man who called himself poor and needy is now the congregation's opening move. Read that way, the poor and needy of verse 1 is not only David: the community takes his words as its own without changing them, and the request of verse 11 for one heart is heard as a request for a congregation that wants one thing.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Count the names. The title Adonai appears seven times in this psalm and the four letter name four times, and the two are not scattered at random: the four letter name carries the requests to be heard, in verses 1, 6, 11 and 17, while Adonai carries the descriptions of what God is. In the kabbalistic reading, the plea of יַחֵד (ya-ched, v.11) for one heart is the human side of a unity being asked for above, so that the psalm's real request is not rescue from enemies but the removal of division, in the speaker first and then in the nations of verse 9 who come and bow as one.
