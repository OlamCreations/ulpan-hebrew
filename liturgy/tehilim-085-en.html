Tehilim 85: Kindness and truth have met

תְּהִלִּים פ״ה · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִבְנֵי
HomePs 85
Fourteen verses in three movements: what God has already done (2 to 4), what the community is asking for now (5 to 8), and an answer that is heard rather than requested (9 to 14). The root sh-v-b, turn or return, is the spine and appears in all three: You turned back the fortunes, You turned back from Your anger, turn us, will You not turn again, let them not turn back. Verse 11 is the line everyone knows, and it repays slow reading because both verbs are physical: two pairs of abstractions that meet in the street and kiss. In the cycle that reads the whole book across thirty days it falls on the seventeenth.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Three movements and a change of posture. Verses 2 to 4 report five completed acts of favour: You favoured, You turned back, You lifted, You covered, You gathered in. Verses 5 to 8 then ask for what the first movement has just said already happened, which is the whole problem of the psalm in miniature. Verses 9 to 14 are an answer, introduced by a speaker who stops petitioning and listens. Whether the past tense of the opening is memory of a real return or the confident perfect of prophecy is the oldest question about this text, and the psalm does not resolve it. The verb at נָשָׂאתָ (na-sa-ta, v.3) is the ordinary word for picking up a load, so forgiveness here is weight carried off rather than weight cancelled.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The root sh-v-b, turn or return, is the spine, and each occurrence has a different subject: שַׁבְתָּ (shav-ta, v.2) in the report, שׁוּבֵנוּ (shu-ve-nu, v.5) in the petition, תָּשׁוּב (ta-shuv, v.7) in the question, יָשׁוּבוּ (ya-shu-vu, v.9) in the warning. The sequence runs God, us, God, us. Verse 2 also carries a crux that translations show plainly. The two words at שַׁבְתָּ (shav-ta, v.2) and שְׁבִית (shvit, v.2) are read either as returned the captivity, from the root for taking captive, or as restored the fortunes, from the root for turning, and the choice decides whether the psalm is remembering an exile or a change in the weather. It is disputed. The last three words of verse 9 are abrupt in Hebrew, and translators differ over whether לְכִסְלָה (le-chis-la, v.9) closes the divine speech or is the psalmist's own aside about his people.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The four nouns of verse 11 are the cast of a well known midrash. Bereshit Rabbah 8:5, commenting on the plural in let us make man at Genesis 1:26, has kindness, truth, righteousness and peace argue over whether the human being should be created at all; kindness and righteousness argue for it, truth and peace against, and truth is thrown to the ground. The very next verse here, תִּצְמָח (titz-mach, v.12), truth will sprout from the earth, is read as what happens after the throwing. Taken that way the psalm is not describing four qualities that agree, it is describing four qualities reconciled after a quarrel, which is a harder and more interesting claim.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

In the kabbalistic vocabulary the four terms of verse 11 are positions rather than qualities. Kindness and truth belong to the right side and the centre, righteousness names the receiving end of the structure and peace the channel that reaches it, so נִפְגָּשׁוּ (nif-ga-shu, v.11) and נָשָׁקוּ (na-sha-ku, v.11), met and kissed, describe a joining rather than an agreement reached in an argument. The two directions of verse 12 hold the same picture from another angle: something rising from below and something inclining from above. In this reading the human contribution is the sprouting one, and nothing arrives from above that was not first grown from underneath.
