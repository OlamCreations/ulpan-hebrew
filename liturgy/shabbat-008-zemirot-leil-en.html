Zemirot Leil Shabbat: Friday Night Table Songs

HomeZemirot Leil
The Friday-night table songs, zemirot sung between courses or after the meal, before Birkat HaMazon. A genre that developed in medieval Spain, France, and the German lands (~11th-14th c.) and continued to grow through the kabbalistic period in Safed. Each community has its own core set, with significant overlap. The universal favorite is Tzur Mishelo, sung in nearly every Jewish home before Birkat HaMazon on Friday night. Other widely-sung Friday-night zemirot: Kol M'kadesh Shvi'i, Menucha v'Simcha, Yom Shabbaton, Yah Ribbon (Aramaic, by Rabbi Israel Najara, c.1600), Tzama'ah Nafshi. This page features Tzur Mishelo as the representative song. Each zemer has 3-5 stanzas; this page teaches the refrain plus the first verse.

♦ Lesson

  • When, Friday night, throughout the meal. Some families sing between courses; many save them for after the main course, before Birkat HaMazon. Total singing time: 10-30 minutes depending on how many zemirot are sung and at what pace.
  • Who sings, everyone. Zemirot are participatory; the head of the table doesn't lead alone. Children join. Guests join. Many families have a printed booklet (Zemirot Shabbat) at every place setting; some have decorative bencher booklets that double as both the zemirot and Birkat HaMazon.
  • The Tzur Mishelo refrain, the most-recognized Friday-night refrain. Echoes the story of Elisha feeding 100 people from 20 loaves of barley (2 Kings 4:42-44): "They ate and there was some left over, as the LORD had said." The same miracle of bread-multiplied-by-grace is invoked at every Friday-night table.
  • The other major Friday-night zemirot, quick guide:
    • Kol M'kadesh Shvi'i, "All who sanctify the seventh". A four-stanza acrostic by an anonymous medieval author. The first stanza is the most sung.
    • Menucha v'Simcha, "Rest and Joy". A three-stanza acrostic by Rabbi Moshe (medieval France?).
    • Yom Shabbaton, "Day of Rest". By Yehuda HaLevi (Spain, ~1120s). Refrain: "Yonah matzah vo manoach" ("The dove found rest there", recalling Noah's dove finding rest).
    • Yah Ribbon Alam V'almaya, Aramaic. By Rabbi Israel Najara (Damascus/Safed, ~1600). One of the few Aramaic table songs; preserves the mystical tradition.
    • Tzama'ah Nafshi, "My soul thirsts". By Avraham ibn Ezra (Spain, ~1130s). Philosophical and theological.
  • Vocabulary:
    • tzur (צוּר), "rock". A biblical name for God; the Tanakh uses it ~30 times to describe God's solidity and reliability (e.g. Deut 32:4: "the Rock, His work is perfect").
    • emunai (אֱמוּנַי), "my faithful ones". Plural of emun (faithful one), with 1cs possessive suffix. Same root as emunah (faith, faithfulness) and amen.
    • sava'nu (שָׂבַעְנוּ), "we were satisfied". Qal perfect of ש.ב.ע (to be sated). Direct echo of Deut 8:10: "you shall eat and be satisfied (v'savata) and bless", the verse that mandates Birkat HaMazon.
    • hotarnu (הוֹתַרְנוּ), "we have left over". Hiphil perfect of י.ת.ר (to remain). Same root as moter (excess) and l'hotir (to leave a remainder).
    • ki-dvar Adonai (כִּדְבַר יְהוָה), "according to the word of the LORD". Echoes the Elisha miracle ("and they ate and there was some left over, according to the word of the LORD").
  • Common melody, the standard tune for Tzur Mishelo is a slow, lyrical melody in a minor key, easy to sing in unison. Carlebach also has a popular setting. Sephardi communities have their own Andalusian-influenced melodies.

♦ About this prayer

The medieval and early-modern genre of Hebrew table songs sung at Shabbat meals. Bridges the formal liturgy of the synagogue with the informal warmth of the family table. Tzur Mishelo is the universal opener.

Origin of the zemirot genre

The genre developed in the medieval period (~10th-14th centuries). Some zemirot are by named authors with surviving acrostics: Avraham ibn Ezra, Yehuda HaLevi, Yisrael Najara, Shlomo Alkabetz, and others. Many are anonymous medieval folk compositions that entered family use through unknown channels. The Spanish-Sephardi tradition has the richest written corpus; the German-Ashkenazi tradition often preserved zemirot orally before they were eventually printed in the 16th-17th centuries.

The melodies are even more varied than the texts. The same lyric is sung to different tunes in Yemen, Morocco, Poland, Iraq, Italy, and Spanish-Portuguese communities. Modern Israeli versions often combine Carlebach-style chant with Mizrahi elements.

What zemirot do at the table

Practically, zemirot do several things:

1. Mark transitions in the meal. After each course, a zemer signals "we're still at Shabbat dinner, not at an ordinary meal". The singing extends the meal and stops it from becoming purely transactional.

2. Teach children. Children pick up zemirot by ear over years of Shabbat meals. By the time a Jewish child is 10, they've heard Tzur Mishelo 500 times. The melody and words are knit into early memory.

3. Anchor mystical themes. Friday night is, in the kabbalistic tradition, the marriage of the Shechinah (feminine divine) and the Holy One. The zemirot frequently use language of bride/groom, attraction/embrace, mystical union, kept gentle enough that children sing them without noticing the depth.

4. Lengthen Shabbat experientially. The meal extends to 2-3 hours. Conversation slows. Songs are interspersed with stories, divrei Torah, jokes. By the end, Friday night has felt distinct from any weeknight dinner.

Where to learn the rest

The standard reference: any bencher (small printed booklet) sold in any Judaica shop, typically contains Birkat HaMazon plus the 12-15 most common zemirot. Many families have a stack of benchers customized for a specific wedding or bar/bat mitzvah; these become collector items. Online: Sefaria has the full corpus of Shabbat zemirot with translations. YouTube has dozens of recordings of each major zemer in various rite-melodies.

The fastest way to learn: spend a Friday night with a family that sings them. After 3 weeks of regular attendance, you'll have the refrains of Tzur Mishelo, Yom Shabbaton, and Yah Ribbon by heart, and the rest follow.
