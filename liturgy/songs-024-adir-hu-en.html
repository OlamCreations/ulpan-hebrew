Adir Hu: Mighty is He

Pesach seder, near the end of the night · אַדִּיר הוּא
HomeAdir Hu
An alphabet in eight lines. Each line names God with three adjectives in alphabetical order, then the same refrain returns: may He build His house soon. Twenty-two adjectives, one after another, and no argument connecting them — the poem is a list, and the pleasure of it is the list. Sung near the end of the seder, when the reading is done and the table wants something it can shout.

The alphabet closes on תַּקִּיף (ta-kif), and the last refrain is usually the loudest and the fastest of the eight. The song has no ending of its own — it simply runs out of letters.

♫ Singing notes

  • It gets faster. Each refrain is a little quicker than the last, and by the eighth the table is racing. Do not fight it.
  • The refrain is what everyone knows; the three adjectives are what one or two people at the table know. Let them lead the line and bring the room in on יִבְנֶה (yiv-ne).
  • Three chords, C F G, and they do not change for eight verses. This is a good first song to accompany for someone who has been playing a week.
  • In many communities each line is sung twice before the refrain. Ask, or listen for one bar before committing.

♦ About this song

The form

An alphabetical acrostic on the divine attributes, sung in the Nirtzah section at the very end of the seder, alongside Echad Mi Yodea and Chad Gadya. All three are late medieval additions and all three are counting songs of one kind or another.

Why a list and not an argument

The poem makes no claim that requires assent; it names, twenty-two times, and asks for one thing between each naming. That structure is deliberately easy to join, which is what it is for at the point of the evening where it sits.

The house

בֵיתוֹ (vei-to) is the Temple, and the refrain is a petition for its rebuilding. Coming at the end of a night spent recounting a redemption that already happened, it turns the whole seder toward one that has not.
