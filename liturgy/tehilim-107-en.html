Tehilim 107: Let the redeemed of the LORD say so

תְּהִלִּים ק״ז · הֹדוּ לַיהֹוָה
HomePs 107
Forty three verses that open Book Five of the psalms, built out of four rescue stories told in the same shape: people in trouble, a shout, a rescue, a call to give thanks. Wanderers in the desert, prisoners in irons, the sick, and sailors in a storm. Two refrains hold it together, the cry at verses 6, 13, 19 and 28 and the thanksgiving at verses 8, 15, 21 and 31, so a learner who reads those eight verses first will recognise most of the vocabulary of the rest. The last eleven verses drop the refrain and turn into a hymn about reversal, water into desert and desert into water. Watch the word for covenant kindness: it is the last word of verse 1 and the second to last of verse 43, and everything in between is what it looks like in practice.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

Four groups, one shape. Desert wanderers in verses 4 to 9, prisoners in 10 to 16, the sick in 17 to 22, sailors in 23 to 32. Each block states the trouble, then the cry, וַיִּצְעֲקוּ (va-yitz-a-ku, v.6), then the rescue, then the call to give thanks, חַסְדּוֹ (chas-do, v.8). The cry stands at verses 6, 13, 19 and 28; the thanksgiving at 8, 15, 21 and 31. Only the prisoners and the sick are told why they are in trouble, which means the psalm is not claiming that suffering is always earned. From verse 33 the refrains stop and a hymn takes over: rivers to desert, desert to pools, the great brought low and the poor set high. Verse 43 closes by asking the reader to notice the pattern rather than to sing about it.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Three words repay a slow look. The pair rendered as deepest gloom at וְצַלְמָוֶת (ve-tzal-ma-vet, v.10) is the word from the valley of Psalm 23, read by some as a compound of shadow and death and by others as a single noun for thick darkness; both readings are current. The word for pits at מִשְּׁחִיתוֹתָם (mish-chi-to-tam, v.20) is ambiguous in the same way: it can be read as graves, or from a root meaning ruin, so translations split between rescue from the grave and rescue from their own destructive ways. And the calm of יָקֵם (ya-kem, v.29) uses a causative of the verb to stand, which is why some render it as stilling the storm and others as raising up a hush in its place. The Masoretic tradition also marks part of the sailors section with the rare inverted nun sign found at Numbers 10:35 and 36; its exact placement here and its purpose are both disputed.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

The Talmud in Berakhot 54b reads the four groups as a legal list: four people must offer the blessing hagomel, one who crossed the sea, one who crossed the desert, one who was ill and recovered, and one who was released from prison. The psalm becomes the source text for a still living liturgical practice, said in public after the Torah reading, which is exactly the public setting verse 32 asks for. A second reading, common but not tied to one named source, takes the four as four exiles and the return of verse 3 as the ingathering. Note what the plain text does not say: in none of the four cases is the cry described as prayer, and in none of them are the people said to deserve their rescue.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The psalm is bracketed by one word. It ends verse 1 at חַסְדּוֹ (chas-do, v.1) in the singular, His kindness, and returns in verse 43 at חַסְדֵי (chas-dei, v.43) in the plural, the kindnesses, so the forty one verses between them are that single quality broken into instances. In the mystical reading the four descents are not accidents to be avoided but the shape of the movement itself: the wanderer, the prisoner, the sick and the sailor each go down before anything rises, and the storm of verse 25 is spoken by the same voice that hushes it in verse 29. The cry in each block is wordless, a shout rather than a formula, which is read as the point at which a person has nothing left to offer and is answered anyway.
