Tehilim 16: I have set the LORD before me always

תְּהִלִּים ט״ז · מִכְתָּם לְדָוִד
HomePs 16
Eleven verses headed michtam, a word nobody has convincingly explained. There is almost no petition here: one imperative in verse 1, then ten verses of a man taking inventory of what he already holds. The vocabulary is borrowed from land law, portion, share, lot, measuring lines, inheritance, and then applied to God instead of to a field. Psalm 16 opens the ten psalms of the Tikkun HaKlali, and verse 8 is the verse written on the shiviti plaque set in front of a prayer leader.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

An inventory, not a plea. One imperative opens the psalm and nothing else is asked for. Verses 2 to 4 sort out where loyalty goes: first upward, then to the holy ones in the land, then away from another cult and its offerings. Verses 5 and 6 borrow the vocabulary of the tribal land allotment, חֶלְקִי (chel-ki, v.5) and חֲבָלִים (cha-va-lim, v.6) are the share and the measuring ropes of an inheritance, and hand it to God instead of to a field. Verse 8 states the reason the whole structure holds, and verses 9 to 11 are what follows from it in a body that expects to keep living.

רמז · Remez: allusion

אָמַרְתְּ (a-mar-te, v.2) is written with the ending of a second person verb and read as a first person one, which is why translations split between "you said" and "I said" without either side treating the matter as settled. עַצְּבוֹתָם (atz-vo-tam, v.4) is open in a different way: the noun can mean sorrows or idols, and the whole line reads differently depending on the choice. כִלְיוֹתָי (chil-o-tai, v.7) is the kidneys, which biblical Hebrew uses as the organ of counsel and conscience, so the night half of that verse describes being corrected from inside rather than taught from outside.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 8 has had the widest afterlife of anything here. The Rema opens his gloss on the Shulchan Aruch by quoting it and calling it a great principle, and the same words are what gets written on the shiviti plaque hung before a prayer leader, so a sentence about inner posture became a piece of synagogue furniture. The kidneys of כִלְיוֹתָי (chil-o-tai, v.7) are read literally as organs that advise: the Talmud in Berakhot lists the kidneys among the organs that give counsel, which makes the night instruction of that verse self-instruction. Psalm 16 also stands first among the ten psalms of the Tikkun HaKlali, the sequence Rebbe Nachman of Breslov named as a general remedy.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The shiviti tradition takes verse 8 as a practice and not a report. The verb means to place, and also to make level or equal, and the reading it invites is that keeping the Name in view flattens the difference between what frightens a person and what does not. In the kabbalistic vocabulary the right hand of בִּימִינְךָ (bi-min-cha, v.11) is the side of expansion and giving, so ending there rather than on the left is itself part of the claim. Notice also that the psalm asks for nothing after its first line: a soul that has already received its portion has no petition left to make, only a path it needs shown.
