Tehilim 71: Do not cast me off in old age

תְּהִלִּים ע״א · בְּךָ יְהֹוָה
HomePs 71
Twenty-four verses in the voice of someone who has already lived a long time and is asking not to be dropped at the end of it. The Hebrew gives this psalm no heading at all, which is unusual in this part of the book, and its first verse is identical to the opening line of Psalm 31, with the two verses after it reworking the same material more loosely. Three roots carry the argument: shame in verses 1, 13 and 24, abandon in 9, 11 and 18, and the word for always in 3, 6 and 14. In the thirty-day reading cycle it falls on the thirteenth day.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

An old man's petition, in four movements. Verses 1 to 3 are the opening appeal, and they are not new: verse 1 is word for word the opening line of Psalm 31, and the next two reuse its vocabulary of rescue, ear, rock and fortress in a different order, so the appeal reads like a formula being invoked rather than a sentence being invented. Verses 4 to 13 are the crisis, with the specific fear stated in תַּשְׁלִיכֵנִי (tash-li-che-ni, v.9) and repeated at תַּעַזְבֵנִי (ta-az-ve-ni, v.18): not death, but being put down like something no longer worth carrying. Verse 14 is the hinge, and everything after it is praise, promise and instruments. The word to track is the one for always, which lands three times, at תָּמִיד (ta-mid, v.3), תָמִיד (ta-mid, v.6) and תָּמִיד (ta-mid, v.14), each time attached to something the speaker does rather than something he receives.

רמז · Remez: allusion

The psalm is built on one root, the root of shame, and the plot is which way it points. In אֵבוֹשָׁה (e-vo-sha, v.1) he asks not to be shamed; in יֵבֹשׁוּ (ye-vo-shu, v.13) he asks that his accusers be; in בֹשׁוּ (vo-shu, v.24) they are. Nothing else in the psalm is resolved that cleanly. A second root does the same work in the opposite direction: the enemies say in עֲזָבוֹ (a-za-vo, v.11) that God has abandoned him, and he uses their verb in his own request at תַּעַזְבֵנִי (ta-az-ve-ni, v.18), so their taunt and his prayer are the same word with the subject changed. The one genuinely obscure word is גוֹזִי (go-zi, v.6). Translations render it as the one who took me from the womb, but the verb is rare and its root is disputed, and the meaning here is reconstructed from the birth imagery around it rather than from parallel usage.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Read verse 18 as the psalm's real request and the whole thing changes shape. He does not ask for more years so that the years will be pleasant; he asks for enough time to declare God's arm to a generation that has not yet arrived, which makes length of days an obligation rather than a reward. On that reading the fear in verse 9 is not fear of dying but fear of being made irrelevant before the handover is complete. There is also the long standing habit of reading the psalms of the single sufferer as the voice of the whole people, and this one takes it easily: a nation held since the womb, told by onlookers in verse 11 that it has been abandoned, asking in verse 20 to be brought up again from the depths of the earth.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

Two places in this psalm sit below the surface of the ground, and they answer each other. In מִמְּעֵי (mim-ei, v.6) the speaker comes out of his mother's body; in וּמִתְּהֹמוֹת (u-mit-ho-mot, v.20) he is brought back up from the depths of the earth. Between them, verse 20 uses the same verb twice, at תָּשׁוּב (ta-shuv, v.20) and תָּשׁוּב (ta-shuv, v.20), a verb of returning that turns both the reviving and the raising into a coming back rather than a first arrival. Later Jewish mysticism states the pattern as a rule, that the descent is for the sake of the ascent, and the verse supplies the grammar for it: the trouble is credited to God in the same breath as the rescue, one clause apart, with no attempt to separate the two hands.
