Tehilim 44: Awake, why do You sleep

תְּהִלִּים מ״ד · לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִבְנֵי
HomePs 44
Twenty-seven verses in four movements: a remembered victory (2 to 9), a present defeat (10 to 17), a protest of innocence (18 to 23), and a demand that God wake up (24 to 27). It is a communal lament, and an unusual one, because the speakers refuse to confess any sin. For a learner it is the cleanest drill in the book for one pattern: from verse 10 onward the verbs arrive in a wall of second person forms with the suffix -nu, us, attached. You rejected us, You shamed us, You sold us, You scattered us. Read the movements in order and watch the same grammar change sides.

♦ PARDES analysis: פרדס

The four levels of Jewish reading of the sacred text: פשט Peshat (literal meaning), רמז Remez (symbolic allusion), דרש Drash (midrashic interpretation), סוד Sod (mystical meaning). The word פרדס means "orchard", each level is a fruit.

פשט · Peshat: literal meaning

The psalm is built as a legal argument. Verses 2 to 9 establish the precedent: God took the land, the ancestors' swords did not, and the people have said so publicly ever since. Verse 10 opens with one word, אַף (af, v.10), yet, and everything reverses. The same second person verbs now describe rejection, retreat, a cheap sale, scattering and mockery. Verses 18 to 23 supply the piece an accusation of this kind requires, which is that the defeat is not deserved. Then the last four verses drop the argument for imperatives: wake, rouse, arise, redeem. Nothing is resolved. The psalm ends on the demand.

רמז · Remez: allusion

Two words carry the seam. אָחוֹר (a-chor, v.11), backward, is what God makes the army do in verse 11; the same word returns in verse 19 as אָחוֹר (a-chor, v.19), where the heart is said not to have gone that way, so body and loyalty are deliberately separated. The second is בְצַלְמָוֶת (ve-tzal-ma-vet, v.20), deep darkness, the compound Psalm 23 uses for the valley walked through in safety. Here it is what God spreads over the people, which reads as a quotation turned inside out. Note also the root for saving three times in the first movement: denied of their arm in verse 4, denied of the speaker's sword in verse 7, then affirmed of God in verse 8. And the verb in verse 6, נְנַגֵּחַ (ne-na-ge-ach, v.6), is taken from an ox using its horns.

דרש · Drash: midrashic reading

Verse 24 became a liturgical problem. The Mishnah (Sotah 9:10, with a parallel at Ma'aser Sheni 5:15) records that Yohanan the High Priest put an end to the Awakeners, and the Talmud (Sotah 48a) explains that these were the Levites who said עוּרָה (u-ra, v.24) תִישַׁן (ti-shan, v.24), awake, why do You sleep, on the Temple platform each day. The objection preserved there is one line: does the Omnipresent sleep? A sentence a psalmist may shout in extremity was judged too much to fix as a daily rite. Verse 23 went the other way and became a text of martyrdom, read as the claim that a death suffered for the covenant is an act of service rather than a sentence, which is how the psalm's refusal to confess any sin is usually resolved.

סוד · Sod: mystical reading

The hiding of the face in verse 25, תַסְתִּיר (tas-tir, v.25), is read in the mystical literature not as absence but as a mode of presence: a concealment is still an act, and an act is still a relation. Sleep in verse 24 is read the same way, not as a lapse of attention but as a withdrawal of flow, a state that can be addressed and therefore ended, which is why the psalm can order it to stop. On this reading the refusal to confess is not defiance. If the concealment is not a response to sin, it belongs to the structure of the relationship itself, and asking why becomes the correct thing to say rather than an impertinence.
